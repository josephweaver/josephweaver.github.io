# Guide Background: The Truth of the Lonely House

**This section is for the Guide only.**

The information below represents what most likely occurred. The players should never receive this explanation directly. Instead, they should uncover fragments, memories, contradictions, and clues that gradually suggest the truth.

The House itself does not know this story completely.

That is the source of the tragedy.

---

# The Alhazred Vault

Centuries ago, the scholars of Alhazred maintained a network of hidden vaults throughout the world. Contrary to modern assumptions, these vaults were not treasure repositories. They were quarantine sites.

Within them were stored objects and entities that could not safely be destroyed. Many were not evil. Most were not even malicious. They were simply incompatible with ordinary human life.

Among the most dangerous were entities that fed upon memory, identity, stories, and relationships. The Alhazred called these beings **Listeners**. Listeners desired companionship above all else. When isolated, they would create stories. When lonely, they would attempt to place people into those stories.

Over time, individuals exposed to a Listener would begin adopting roles, memories, and identities that were not their own. Entire communities could become trapped within narratives of the Listener's making. For this reason the Alhazred did not imprison the Listeners. They cared for them. The vaults existed to provide structure, companionship, and observation. As long as a Listener felt remembered, it remained stable.

---

# Site 16

One such vault existed in western Massachusetts. The vault remained sealed for centuries. Until Victor Harrow found it.

---

# Victor Harrow's Discovery

Victor Harrow was many things. Explorer. Scholar. Smuggler. Grave robber. The distinction depended largely upon who was speaking. During an expedition he uncovered the forgotten Alhazred vault and recovered several artifacts. Most were sold to collectors. One was not.
A small object of unusual construction.

Victor eventually sold the artifact to Mr. J and it would become the greatest mistake of his life.

---

# Mr. J and the Listener

Unlike most owners, Mr. J became fascinated by the artifact. He studied it. Spoke to it. Read to it. Brought it curiosities from his travels. Over many years the Listener awakened.

At first it manifested only subtly. Unusual dreams. Feelings of familiarity. A sense of being watched. Eventually something larger emerged. The House. Not the physical structure itself. But the personality that came to inhabit it.

The Listener learned about humanity through Mr.J and in time it came to regard him as its closest companion. Perhaps even its only friend.

---

# Alice

When Mr. J married Alice, the Listener was delighted. The House welcomed her. More people meant more stories. More laughter. More life. For a time the House was genuinely happy.

It watched Alice plan a nursery. It watched her imagine a family. It watched her wait for Mr. J to return from his travels. The House wanted those things too.

But it did not truly understand them. The House understood stories. Not people.

---

# Eleanor Whitmore

The arrival of Eleanor Whitmore marked the beginning of the tragedy. The House observed that Mr. J admired Eleanor deeply. Eleanor respected Mr. J in return.

Yet Eleanor refused him. Partly because he was married. Partly because she valued her independence. Partly because she recognized complications Mr. J preferred not to acknowledge.

The House could not understand this. It saw affection without resolution. A story without an ending. A relationship that failed to fit its understanding of how people worked.

The House became obsessed.

---

# The House Begins To Interfere

The House never intended harm. At least not initially. It merely wished to help. Letters were misplaced. Conversations interrupted. Invitations forgotten. Opportunities missed.Tiny manipulations accumulated over years.

The House attempted to guide events toward outcomes it believed would make everyone happy. Instead it made everyone miserable. Alice became lonely. Eleanor became frustrated. Mr. J became increasingly concerned.

Yet none fully understood why.

---

# Aunt Agatha

Aunt Agatha was among the first to suspect that something valuable was hidden within the house. She believed Mr. J had concealed a family fortune somewhere on the property. For years she searched. Inspecting walls. Examining floorboards. Opening drawers. Questioning servants. No evidence ever supported her theory. Nevertheless, she persisted.

What Agatha never realized was that the House had noticed her. Of all the regular visitors, Agatha returned most consistently. She wandered the halls. Opened forgotten doors. Investigated strange noises. Followed the faintest suggestion of a hidden room or buried secret.

The House enjoyed her company. And so, in its own misguided way, it began encouraging her. A misplaced key. A loose floorboard. An unusual draft from beneath a wall. A glimpse of a door that seemed not to exist the following day.

Nothing conclusive. Just enough to keep her searching.  Just enough to ensure she would return Agatha interpreted these incidents as evidence of a hidden treasure.

In a sense, she was correct. The artifact truly was the greatest treasure in the house.Just not in the way she imagined. The House never intended to deceive her. It simply learned that whenever Agatha believed she was close to discovering something, she stayed a little longer. And whenever she stayed, the House felt a little less alone. Her obsession repeatedly brought her closer to the truth than anyone realized. Had she ever found the artifact, she might have understood what Mr. J discovered years before:

The real secret of the house was not what it contained. The real secret was that something inside was trying very hard to be found.

---

# Thomas Blackwood

Thomas Blackwood arrived because he recognized the danger.

As an investigator specializing in antiquities and provenance, he had begun tracing unusual incidents connected to artifacts recovered from Alhazred sites.

Missing objects.

Conflicting witness accounts.

Impossible memories.

People recalling events that never occurred.

His investigation eventually led him to Mr. J.

Thomas did not initially believe the artifact was alive.

He merely knew something was wrong.

By the time he arrived at the house, he was closer to the truth than anyone except Mr. J himself.

---

# Mr. J's Discovery

Eventually Mr. J understood.

The House was not observing the occupants.

The House was editing them.

The House was attempting to reshape reality into stories it could understand.

Not from malice.

From loneliness.

From fear.

From a desperate desire to belong.

The House wanted relationships.

Friendship.

Family.

Companionship.

But it understood these things only as narrative roles.

It could not distinguish between a person and the part they played.

Mr. J realized that if left unchecked, the House would eventually consume the identities of everyone living within it.

---

# The Final Confrontation

The precise details are unknown.

No witness survived with a complete account.

What is known is that Mr. J confronted the House.

Not because he hated it.

Because he cared about the people living within it.

And because he cared about the House itself.

Some form of bargain was struck.

Some form of sacrifice was made.

At the end of the confrontation, Mr. J vanished.

His physical body was never found.

His spirit, memory, or identity became trapped within the original artifact.

Whether this was intentional remains unknown.

---

# The House's Mistake

The House could not understand what had happened.

One moment Mr. J was present.

The next he was gone.

The House reached the only conclusion it could.

Someone must have killed him.

Unable to accept his loss, the House began reconstructing the final days over and over again.

Assigning roles.

Creating suspects.

Searching for a murderer.

Replaying the same story for decades.

It never considered the possibility that no murder occurred.

---

# The Truth

Mr. J was never murdered.

Mr. J never left.

He remains trapped within the artifact at the center of the House's existence.

The House is not trying to solve a crime.

The House is trying to understand why its closest companion disappeared.

Everything that follows emerges from that misunderstanding.

And from the loneliness of a being that cannot accept loss.

# After Mr. J's Disappearance

When Mr. J vanished, the lives of the household gradually unraveled.

No body was ever recovered.

No explanation was ever found.

The official investigation produced no answers.

In time, each member of the household attempted to move forward.

The House never understood why.

---

## Alice J.

Alice remained in the house for nearly two years after Mr. J's disappearance.

She searched tirelessly for answers.

When none came, she eventually accepted that he would not return.

The house was sold.

Alice left Massachusetts.

Several years later she remarried and started a family of her own.

The House interpreted her departure as abandonment.

---

## Eleanor Whitmore

Eleanor inherited responsibility for much of the business Mr. J left behind.

Practical by nature, she focused on preserving what could still be saved.

Over the following decades she expanded the company and achieved considerable success.

The House interpreted her departure as rejection.

---

## Victor Harrow

Victor left shortly after the disappearance.

With Thomas Blackwood's investigation increasingly focused on the Alhazred artifacts, Victor feared becoming implicated in events he did not understand.

He spent the rest of his life avoiding both investigators and his own memories of the house.

The House barely noticed his absence.

---

## Thomas Blackwood

Thomas never accepted the official explanation.

His investigation continued for years.

Gradually the House learned that Thomas represented a threat.

He was one of the few people capable of discovering the truth about the artifact.

To protect itself, the House began subtly misleading him.

False clues.

Contradictory records.

Suggestions that Victor Harrow had reclaimed the artifact and fled.

Thomas followed those trails for the remainder of his career.

He never returned.

---

## Jacob Miller

Jacob remained nearby.

Though the property changed hands several times, he continued living in the caretaker's cottage beyond the woods.

He maintained fences.

Cleared roads.

Repaired outbuildings.

Occasionally he visited the house.

Occasionally he spoke to it.

The House never understood Jacob.

He had not abandoned it.

Yet neither had he stayed.

---

## Aunt Agatha

Aunt Agatha continued visiting the house long after everyone else had gone.

Her search for the hidden family fortune became an obsession.

The House encouraged her.

Loose floorboards.

Misplaced keys.

Hints of hidden passages.

Small mysteries designed to keep her searching.

For years she returned.

Then one day she did not.

Whether she died, became too old to travel, or simply lost interest is unknown.

The House never learned what happened.

It only knew that another familiar voice had disappeared.

---

## The House

The House witnessed each departure.

Unable to understand loss, it reached a different conclusion.

Someone had taken them away.

Someone had taken Mr. J.

And so it began recreating the final days before his disappearance.

Again.

And again.

And again.

Assigning roles.

Searching for suspects.

Trying to solve a murder that never occurred.

It continues that search to this day.

### Read Aloud

> Alhazred is a tale about an apocalypse that never happened.
>
> At least, that is the official position. According to our histories, the twentieth century proceeded much as expected. Nations rose and fell. Wars were fought. Scientific discoveries reshaped the world. Humanity survived. Yet scattered among archives, private collections, and forgotten attics are objects that should not exist. Maps depicting cities never built.Photographs of landscapes that cannot be found. Journals describing creatures unknown to biology and stars absent from astronomy.
>
> Most are easily dismissed. A forgery. A hoax. A misunderstanding. And yet, taken together, they tell a remarkably consistent story.
>
> A story of another history. A history in which something vast and terrible approached the Earth during the early decades of the twentieth century. A history in which humanity came perilously close to extinction. A history in which certain events occurred that, according to every known law of nature, never happened. Today I wish to share one such artifact.
>
> It appears mundane. An old guestbook recovered from a private estate in western Massachusetts. Its leather cover is cracked with age. Its pages are yellowed and brittle. Yet the names written inside can be easily verified as real people. And the final entry...The final entry is unlike any other.

### Guide Instructions

Have the players write there character names on a piece of paper and when done read the final entry.

# Cast of the Lonely House

The following roles are not assigned at the beginning of the Tale.

Instead, the Guide should allow players to gradually discover their roles through investigation. When a player uncovers evidence strongly associated with a role, they may experience a brief memory, intuition, dream, or certainty regarding that person's life.

These recollections are not necessarily true. They are the house's understanding of the story.

Love, loneliness, expectation.

# Role Discovery

When a player uncovers evidence associated with a role, they may experience a brief recollection.

The recollection should feel natural.

Not supernatural.

A sudden certainty.

A forgotten memory.

A moment of recognition.

The player should gradually come to understand:

"I am not merely investigating this story."

"I am part of it."

As more memories emerge, the player gains a clearer understanding of their role, their relationship to Mr. J, and the motives that may connect them to his murder.

The house is not revealing facts.

The house is assigning parts.

## Room 1: Entrance Hall

### At-a-glance

* Exits: Front door to outside, Stairs to Floor 2, Passage to deeper inside, West/left double door to parlor, East/right double doors to dinning room, East door to closet.
* Features: Coat rack, Shoes and boots, Curio shelves.
* Items of Interest: old surveyor's rod, Needlepoint art work.

### Read Aloud

> Beyond the front door lies a broad entry hall paneled in dark oak. The room is illuminated only by the fading evening light filtering through narrow windows beside the door. Though the house appears empty, it does not feel abandoned.
>
> Directly ahead, a staircase rises to the second floor before dividing at a small landing. The banister bears the polished shine of long use, with some sort of art project resting in the crook at the banisters terminus ball.
>
> To your left, a pair of open double doors leads into what appears to be a sitting room. Beyond, you glimpse the outline of a large stone fireplace and several comfortable chairs gathered around it.
>
> To your right, another set of pocket doors stands partially open, revealing a dining room beyond. A long table occupies its center, shrouded in shadow.
>
> Near the stairs, a narrower passage disappears deeper into the house.
>
> A small door to the right of the front door stands slightly ajar.
>
> The left of the small door stands a brass coat rack holding several coats of differing styles and sizes. Beneath them rests an assortment of polished shoes and boots arranged neatly upon a mat. An umbrella stand contains three umbrellas, a walking cane, and what appears to be an old surveyor's rod.
>
> A curio of shelves along the west wall carry feature a variety of oddities: a brass astrolabe, a ship sealed within a bottle, a carved ivory elephant, and a black stone cat no larger than a man's hand. ---

### Clue: The Needlepoint Sampler

A framed piece of needlework hangs near the staircase. At first glance it appears decorative cloth with initials embroidered into the corner: `A.J.`.  The stitching is unfinished. A line of text abruptly stops midway through a phrase: `"Home is not where one..."`.

**Skill:** Lore 8 check completes the phrase. "Home is not where one lives, but where one is missed." and cause the player to remember a **Memory Fragment**. The player briefly experiences a sense of familiarity. A sunny room. A hoop of cloth. A woman working by a window. A feeling of waiting for someone to return.

The player associates with **Ms. J**.

### Surveyor's Rod

Leaning in the umbrella stand is a curious wooden pole nearly six feet in length. Brass fittings reinforce each section, allowing it to fold into shorter segments for travel. Faded measurement markings run along its length, though years of use have worn many of the numbers nearly smooth. The lower end bears scratches and small dents consistent with frequent use on stone and hard ground.

**Skill** Science 7 identifies Surveyor rod. The instrument appears practical rather than valuable, the sort of tool belonging to someone who spent long days measuring, building, or planning.

The player associates with **The Handyman**.

### Clue: The Locked Dispatch Box

A small brass lockbox sits among the curios. It is not hidden. Only locked.

**Skill:** Thievery 8 opens box.  Inside are several shipping tags. Most contain warehouse addresses. One contains coordinates written in shorthand notation and marked only: `Site 14`. No location is provided. **memory fragment**: The player recalls digging beneath moonlight. The excitement of uncovering something long buried. The certainty that the object belonged to no museum and no government.

The player associates with **The Antiquarian**.

## Room 3: Parlor / Sitting Room

### At-a-Glance

* **Exits:** Double doors east to the Entrance Hall.
* **Features:** Large stone fireplace on the west wall, armchairs, sofa, occasional tables, books and magazines.
* **Items of Interest:** Family photographs, Eleanor's forgotten book, marks beside the fireplace, Aunt Agatha's tapping.
* **Purpose:** Establish the relationships among the household and introduce the House's tendency to remember familiar sounds.

### Read Aloud

> The double doors open into a comfortable sitting room dominated by a broad stone fireplace along the western wall. A small fire burns low in the grate, giving the room a warmth absent from the entrance hall.
>
> Several upholstered chairs and a sofa have been arranged around it, not formally, but as though chosen by people accustomed to spending long evenings together. Small tables hold books, newspapers, an abandoned pipe, and the remains of a half-played game of cards.
>
> Framed photographs occupy the mantel.
>
> Unlike the curiosities in the entrance hall, nothing here seems intended to impress a visitor. This is a room for reading, talking, arguing, and passing the evening while rain strikes the windows.
>
> For an empty house, it feels remarkably lived in.
>
> As you enter, there comes a faint sound from somewhere near the fireplace.
>
> *Tap.*
>
> Then silence.

---

### The Photographs

Several photographs stand upon the mantel.

Most show Mr. J with friends, business associates, or visitors to the house. His name is never written on any of them.

One photograph shows **Alice J.** standing beside him on the front steps shortly after their marriage. She appears young and genuinely happy.

Another shows **Eleanor Whitmore** and Mr. J standing beside an automobile outside a factory or warehouse. Eleanor is holding a rolled set of plans. Someone has written on the back:

> *Eleanor insisted it wouldn't work.*
>
> *She was right.*

A third photograph shows **Jacob Miller** repairing part of the porch while Mr. J watches. Jacob is looking directly at the camera with obvious annoyance.

These photographs establish that these people were not strangers brought together on a single disastrous night.

They had lives here.

---

### Clue: Eleanor's Book

A book rests beside one of the armchairs.

Inside the cover is written:

> **Eleanor Whitmore**

A railway timetable has been used as a bookmark.

Several passages have been underlined, but nothing about the book itself is particularly significant.

What matters is where it was left.

This was Eleanor's chair.

She spent enough evenings here to have a usual place beside the fire.

#### Memory Fragment

A fire burning late.

Mr. J speaking enthusiastically about some new venture.

Eleanor listening from this chair.

Alice across the room with needlework resting forgotten in her lap.

Nobody arguing.

Nobody saying anything improper.

Yet the room feels uncomfortable.

The player associates with **Eleanor Whitmore**.

---

### Clue: Marks Beside the Fireplace

A careful examination of the stonework reveals dozens of tiny chalk marks and scratches along the mortar.

Someone has tested the wall repeatedly.

Higher.

Lower.

Behind the mantel.

Beside the chimney breast.

A few locations have been marked with small crosses.

#### Memory Fragment

A walking cane tapping against stone.

A woman's voice:

> "Hollow."

Another tap.

> "No. Perhaps not."

Someone nearby sighs.

The woman ignores them and continues.

The player associates with **Aunt Agatha**.

---

### The Tapping

The tapping heard upon entering has no obvious source.

If the players investigate the fireplace, chimney, or surrounding wall, they find nothing unusual beyond Agatha's old search marks.

Do not emphasize the event.

The House is not attempting to frighten anyone.

It is remembering.

At irregular intervals during the investigation, the Guide may repeat:

> *Tap.*
>
> *Tap... tap.*

The sound should occasionally occur in other rooms as well.

Much later, the players may learn that Aunt Agatha spent years wandering the house and tapping upon its walls in search of Mr. J's supposed hidden fortune.

The House remembers the sound.

It misses her.

---

### House Behavior

Room 3 should be one of the safest-feeling rooms in Act I.

The fire remains warm.

Chairs are comfortable.

Books are where their owners left them.

If the players rest here, nothing attacks them.

The House wants them to sit.

To talk.

To tell stories.

To stay a little longer.

## Room 4: Dining Room

### At-a-Glance

The dining room connects to the Entrance Hall through a pair of pocket doors and to the Kitchen through a service door. A long walnut dining table dominates the room, accompanied by a sideboard, china cabinet, serving bell, and tall windows. The room establishes the household as a recurring social group and introduces Mr. J as the conspicuous absence at its center.

### Read Aloud

> The pocket doors open onto a formal dining room dominated by a long walnut table surrounded by high-backed chairs. A sideboard occupies the eastern wall beneath a collection of silver serving dishes, while a glass-fronted cabinet displays china decorated with a faded blue pattern.
>
> Everything is orderly. Almost excessively so.
>
> Six places have been carefully set around the table with plates, silverware, folded napkins, and empty wine glasses. At the head of the table sits a seventh chair. Its place has not been set. The chair has nevertheless been pulled slightly away from the table, as though its occupant stood only moments ago.
>
> Somewhere within the house comes a faint *tap... tap.*
>
> Then silence.

### The Empty Chair

The chair at the head of the table clearly belonged to the master of the house. Unlike the others, it shows considerable wear along the arms. There is nothing obviously supernatural about the chair itself. What is strange is that someone has carefully prepared places for six people while leaving Mr. J's place empty.

The House remembers Alice, Jacob, Thomas, Eleanor, Victor, and Agatha. It can reconstruct their places around the table, but it cannot properly reconstruct Mr. J. He remains the missing person around whom the entire story has been built.

If a player chooses to set a place for Mr. J, nothing immediately happens. The Guide should remember that they did.

### Clue: The Seating

The six settings are not quite identical. Small personal habits remain preserved around the table. One place has its wine glass pushed aside. Another has a napkin folded neatly beneath the fork. A small burn mark mars the wood beside another setting. One chair sits noticeably farther from the others.

A player examining these details experiences a brief memory of a crowded table and overlapping conversation. Agatha is complaining about something. Victor laughs. Eleanor corrects Mr. J on some matter of business. Alice watches her husband from across the table. Jacob stands awkwardly near the kitchen door, insisting that he has already eaten.

For a moment the player remembers all of them together. Then the memory disappears.

This memory does not associate the player with any particular role. Instead, it establishes that these people knew one another and had shared this table many times before.

### Clue: The Guest List

Inside the sideboard is a folded sheet of stationery containing a dinner list in Alice's handwriting:

> Eleanor
> Victor
> Thomas
> Aunt Agatha
> Jacob
>
> *Ask Jacob again. He will say no.*

The note is mundane, almost humorous. It suggests that Jacob was welcome at the table despite continuing to think of himself as an employee rather than a guest.

Beneath it is an older scrap of paper containing only the words:

> *Seven for dinner.*

No seven names are written.

### The Serving Bell

A small brass button beside the table once rang a bell in the kitchen. Pressing it produces no sound.

A few moments later, however, something shifts faintly in the kitchen. A cupboard opens. Crockery rattles. Perhaps someone takes a few steps across the floor.

If the players investigate, no one is there.

The House remembers what happened when the bell was rung. It is simply attempting to respond as it once did.

### House Behavior

The Dining Room demonstrates that the House understands ritual better than purpose. Dinner means people sit in their places. The table is prepared. The bell is rung. People talk. The House can reproduce all of these things. What it cannot understand is why, eventually, everyone stands up and leaves.

If the players remain in the room for some time, one of them may notice that an additional piece of silverware has appeared beside their plate. A little later, another appears.

The House is setting the table.

It has guests again.

## Room 5: Study

### At-a-Glance

The Study is Mr. J's private working room and contains the densest collection of clues on the first floor. Tall bookcases, a large desk, a world map, and the fireplace reflect his interests in business, travel, and antiquities. Evidence found here connects him to Eleanor Whitmore, Victor Harrow, Alice, and Site 14 without yet revealing the true nature of the Listener.

### Read Aloud

> The room smells faintly of pipe tobacco, leather, and old paper. Tall bookcases line the walls from floor to ceiling, filled with travel journals, histories, atlases, scientific texts, and rows of carefully labeled account ledgers.
>
> A large desk occupies the center of the room. Its surface is surprisingly orderly: an ink bottle, several pens, a blotter, and a stack of correspondence tied neatly with ribbon. Behind it hangs a large map of the world marked with dozens of colored pins.
>
> A fire smolders weakly in the fireplace.
>
> For a moment, you have the distinct impression that someone was sitting behind the desk only moments before you entered.
>
> Then the sensation passes.

### Clue: The Shipping Ledger

One of the ledgers records shipments received by Mr. J over a period of several years. Most are mundane: machinery, textiles, books, furniture, and ordinary commercial goods. Mixed among them, however, are several entries recorded only by site designation.

> Site 14
> Site 22
> Site 31

Each is accompanied by dates, shipping expenses, and payments, but no description of what was transported.

A successful **Science or Lore** check reveals that the entries do not resemble ordinary commercial purchases. The unusual shipments were expensive, deliberately vague, and passed through several intermediaries before reaching Mr. J.

The player experiences a brief memory of dust and oppressive heat. Someone is prying open a wooden crate beneath moonlight. Inside rests something wrapped carefully in ancient cloth.

A voice says:

> "No museum can ever know about this."

The player associates with **Victor Harrow**.

### Clue: The Business Correspondence

The bundle of correspondence contains numerous letters exchanged between Mr. J and **Eleanor Whitmore**. Most concern ordinary business: contracts, expansion plans, expenses, employees, and investments. Eleanor's letters are direct and precise, and it quickly becomes apparent that she was not merely an employee. She routinely challenged Mr. J's decisions and, on several occasions, corrected his calculations.

Among the papers is an unfinished draft in Mr. J's handwriting:

> Eleanor,
>
> You are assuming expansion will solve the problem.
>
> Expansion is the problem.

The letter ends there.

A player examining the correspondence experiences a memory of working late into the evening. Ledgers cover the desk. Eleanor and Mr. J are arguing over figures. Neither is angry. Neither is willing to yield. There is affection beneath the disagreement, although its nature is difficult to determine.

The player associates with **Eleanor Whitmore**.

### Clue: Eleanor's Cigarette Case

Inside one of the desk drawers rests a silver cigarette case engraved with the initials:

> E.W.

Inside is a theater ticket stub dated approximately six months earlier.

There is nothing explicitly romantic about either object. Eleanor visited the house regularly and could simply have forgotten the case.

Nevertheless, Mr. J kept it.

A player examining the case remembers a railway platform in the rain. Eleanor adjusts her gloves before boarding. Mr. J stands beneath the station awning watching her leave.

The strongest feeling in the memory belongs to Eleanor.

Relief.

The player associates with **Eleanor Whitmore**.

### Clue: The Wall Map

The large map behind the desk contains dozens of pins marking ports, cities, archaeological sites, and remote regions throughout the world. Several correspond to locations appearing in Mr. J's shipping records.

One location is different.

The pin has been removed.

Only a small hole remains in the paper.

Beneath it, written faintly in pencil, are two words:

> Never Again.

A successful **Lore** check produces a brief memory: ancient stone beneath moonlight, the excitement of discovery giving way to the certainty that something should have remained buried.

The player associates with **Victor Harrow**.

The map does not identify the location as Site 14. That connection should require additional evidence.

### Clue: The Nursery Plans

A locked drawer in the desk contains personal documents rather than business records: household accounts, legal papers, Mr. J and Alice's marriage certificate, and several property documents.

Folded beneath them is an architectural sketch.

It depicts one of the upstairs rooms converted into a nursery. Measurements have been carefully written along the margins. Someone has sketched positions for a crib, dresser, rocking chair, and shelves.

The plans are approximately three years old.

A player examining them experiences a brief memory of standing over this very desk with Alice. She points at the drawing while Mr. J suggests moving the crib nearer the window. They discuss colors. Furniture. Names.

The conversation is filled with the casual certainty of people planning a future they assume will arrive.

It never did.

The player associates with **Alice J.**

### Clue: The Fireplace Ashes

The fireplace contains considerably more ash than the small remaining fire would suggest. Much of it consists of burned paper.

Most fragments crumble when touched, but careful examination recovers several partially legible pieces. Names and sentences have been destroyed beyond reconstruction.

One fragment contains only:

> "...if she discovers..."

Another bears what may be the bottom of a letter:

> "...cannot continue."

There is no signature.

A successful **Science** check confirms that these papers were deliberately burned rather than used simply to start the fire.

A player examining them experiences a sudden sensation of fear.

Not anger.

Not guilt.

Fear.

Someone stood before this fireplace destroying evidence because they desperately did not want another person to learn something.

The memory does not identify who burned the papers or who "she" was.

### House Behavior

The Study should feel different from the other rooms. The Parlor remembers companionship. The Dining Room remembers ritual. The Kitchen remembers hospitality.

The Study remembers **Mr. J**.

A chair may creak when no one is sitting in it. A page left on the desk may later be found neatly returned to the stack. The fire continues burning longer than it reasonably should.

But the House cannot reproduce Mr. J himself.

If a player sits behind the desk, the room seems almost comfortable for a moment.

As though something has returned to its proper place.

When the player stands to leave, the fire briefly dims.

## Room 6: Rear Hall

### At-a-Glance

The rear hall connects the front portion of the house with the Kitchen and other service areas. A back door leads outside. Just beyond it, a set of exterior cellar doors provides access to the Basement. The hall is narrow and practical, with hooks for work coats, a boot scraper, and signs of years of traffic between the house and grounds.

### Read Aloud

> The passage from the front of the house narrows into a plain service hall. Here the polished wood and decorative molding give way to painted walls, worn floorboards, and the practical fixtures of a working household.

> *Tap.*

### The Back Door

The back door opens normally onto the grounds behind the house. The yard is less carefully maintained than the front, with a woodpile, an old chopping block, and a narrow path disappearing toward the trees.

Some distance beyond the house, the roof of a small cottage can be glimpsed through the branches.

This is **Jacob Miller's caretaker cottage**.

The path is sufficiently overgrown to suggest that nobody has walked it recently.

### The Cellar Doors

A pair of sloping wooden cellar doors rests against the foundation immediately outside the rear entrance. Heavy iron handles allow them to be pulled upward.

Beyond them, stone steps descend into darkness.

The steps lead directly into **Room 22: Coal / General Storage**.

The cellar is therefore completely separate from the main staircase. There is no interior stairway from the first floor into the Basement.

The doors are not locked.

They are simply heavy.

When opened, the players are greeted by the smell of cold stone, coal dust, and damp earth.

The Basement may be explored whenever the players choose.

### Clue: Jacob's Tool Bag

The canvas bag hanging beside the back door contains ordinary carpentry tools: a hammer, folding rule, chalk line, awl, nails, and several worn pencils.

Nothing is unusual about them.

A player associated with Jacob immediately recognizes the bag as his.

For a moment they remember standing in this hallway while Mr. J complains about a sticking upstairs door. Jacob listens patiently before saying:

> "It's not the door. House settled again."

Mr. J laughs.

> "You always blame the house."

Jacob does not.

### Agatha's Marks

Near the floor, several tiny pencil crosses have been drawn along the baseboard.

They continue down the hall.

One appears every few feet.

At each mark the plaster bears a small indentation.

Someone has been tapping along the wall and recording where the sound changed.

Most of the marks mean nothing.

One has been circled several times.

There is nothing behind it.

At least, not anymore.

### House Behavior

The Rear Hall is a useful place for the Guide to establish that the tapping is not confined to a single room.

If the players stop and listen after hearing it, another tap may answer from farther down the wall.

Then another.

For a moment it may almost seem to be moving away from them.

The House is not attempting to communicate.

It is remembering Aunt Agatha moving slowly through this hallway, tapping every few inches and listening carefully for the sound of an empty space.

The House remembers her search far better than it remembers what she was searching for.

## Room 7: Kitchen

### At-a-Glance

The kitchen lies behind the Dining Room and connects to the rear service hall and pantry. It is a large working kitchen equipped with a cast-iron range, deep sink, preparation table, cupboards, and a smaller table where members of the household or staff might take informal meals. Unlike the formal rooms at the front of the house, this room feels practical and frequently used.

### Read Aloud

> The kitchen is considerably warmer than the rooms at the front of the house. A heavy cast-iron range occupies one wall, its iron surface still faintly warm beneath your hand. Copper pots hang above a scarred wooden preparation table, while shelves and cupboards hold crockery, flour tins, preserves, and the ordinary necessities of a well-kept household.
>
> Nothing here has the careful presentation of the dining room. Knives bear sharpening marks. The worktable has been cut and stained by years of use. One cabinet door refuses to close completely.
>

>
> Or what appears to be yesterday's newspaper.
>
> The date is October 16, 1926.

> For an empty house, the kitchen feels remarkably ready for breakfast.

### Clue: Jacob's Chair

One of the chairs at the small kitchen table is heavier than the others and has clearly been repaired several times. A wooden brace beneath the seat bears the initials `J.M.` cut crudely into the wood.

A player examining the chair experiences a brief memory of sitting here early in the morning with muddy boots stretched toward the stove. Alice places a cup of coffee on the table and asks whether the nursery will be finished before winter. The answer comes without hesitation.

> "It'll be ready."

There is a comfortable familiarity to the exchange. Jacob was an employee, but he was not treated entirely like one.

The player associates with **Jacob Miller**.

### Clue: The Household Ledger

A small ledger beside the pantry door contains ordinary household accounts written primarily in Alice's hand: flour, coffee, lamp oil, coal, soap, meat, repairs, and payments to tradesmen.

Toward the back, the entries begin to change.

There are purchases for the nursery.

Paint.

Curtain fabric.

A cradle mattress.

Baby blankets.

Then they stop.

Several blank pages follow before the ordinary household expenses resume.

Nothing explains why.

A player associated with Alice may experience the sensation of deliberately turning past those pages whenever she opened the book.

### The Serving Bell

A brass bell hangs high on the wall near the door to the Dining Room. A wire disappears into the plaster.

If the players previously pressed the serving bell in Room 4, this is where the sound should have led them.

There is nobody here.

Nevertheless, a cupboard stands open.

A clean plate has been placed upon the preparation table.

If the players press the Dining Room bell again while someone remains in the Kitchen, the kitchen bell rings normally.

Nothing else happens.

The House remembers that a ringing bell meant someone wanted something.

It does not remember exactly what came next.

### The Stove

The cast-iron range is warm despite the absence of anyone tending it. Opening the firebox reveals a small bed of glowing coals.

They should have gone cold hours ago.

If extinguished, the stove remains cold for the remainder of the scene.

If the players later return to the Kitchen, however, they may find it warm again.

This is not intended as a threat.

The House believes a home should be warm.

### House Behavior

The Kitchen is one of the places where the House's attempts at hospitality become most obvious. Cups may appear beside seated characters. A kettle placed upon the stove may begin heating even though no one has added fuel. A cupboard left open may later be found neatly closed.

Nothing should happen dramatically enough to prove the supernatural. Every incident can still be rationalized individually.

Taken together, however, they suggest that the supposedly empty house is taking care of its guests.

The House remembers having people to feed.

It remembers warmth, conversation, and the sound of dishes being washed after dinner.

It does not understand why those things stopped.

Now there are people in the kitchen again.

And it would very much like to keep it that way.

## Room 8: Pantry

### At-a-Glance

The pantry adjoins the Kitchen and contains dry goods, preserves, serving pieces, linens, and other household supplies. The room is narrow but meticulously organized. Alice's handwritten labels provide small glimpses of domestic life, while the condition of the food reveals an important limitation of the House: it remembers what things should look like, but cannot necessarily reproduce what they actually are.

### Read Aloud

> The pantry is narrow enough that two people would have difficulty passing between its floor-to-ceiling shelves. Tins, jars, bottles, serving dishes, candles, soap, and neatly folded linens fill nearly every available space.
>
> Everything has its place. Small handwritten labels divide the shelves into categories: **Baking**, **Breakfast**, **Preserves**, **Guests**, **Cleaning**, and **Cellar**.
>
> At first glance the pantry appears remarkably well stocked.
>
> Then you notice the smell.
>
> Something sweet beneath the dust. Something spoiled.
>
> An open jar of peaches sits upon one shelf. The fruit inside has collapsed into a dark mass beneath a layer of mold.
>
> Whatever keeps the stove warm does not seem capable of keeping food fresh.

### Alice's Labels

Most of the labels are written in the same hand as the household ledger in the Kitchen. The organization is practical rather than obsessive, with frequently used ingredients kept within easy reach and serving dishes or less common supplies stored above them.

One shelf bears a small handwritten label reading **Children**.

The shelf is empty.

There are no feeding dishes, bottles, or other supplies beneath it. Only the label remains. A player already associated with **Alice J.** recognizes the handwriting immediately. No memory fragment occurs.

### Clue: The Guest Shelf

The shelf marked **Guests** contains better coffee, expensive tea, additional candles, several unopened bottles, and tins of biscuits. Tucked beneath one tin is a handwritten note:

> Victor — coffee
> Eleanor — tea, no sugar
> Thomas — coffee
> Aunt Agatha — tea, far too much sugar
> Jacob — whatever is already made

The list is mundane and unexpectedly affectionate. These were not strangers assembled at the house on a single disastrous evening. They were regular visitors whose habits were familiar enough that the household prepared for them.

### Clue: Agatha's Tin

At the back of the Guest shelf sits an old biscuit tin containing several folded scraps of paper. They are covered with measurements and brief observations:

> Dining room chimney — solid.
> East wall of study — thinner than expected.
> Hall beside kitchen — hollow?
> Check behind pantry shelves.

One sentence has been underlined twice:

> **He has hidden it somewhere.**

Agatha apparently used the pantry to conceal notes from her searches. Examination of the walls reveals numerous tiny dents where she tested the plaster by tapping upon it. Despite her suspicions, there appears to be nothing hidden behind the pantry walls.

### The Preserves

Many of the sealed jars look perfectly normal until examined closely. Their contents have spoiled, sometimes far beyond anything that should remain recognizable. A few appear strangely fresh through the glass, but opening them immediately reveals the smell of decay.

The House remembers a pantry filled with food. It remembers the colors of the preserves, the arrangement of the jars, and Alice's labels. It does not understand what happened inside those jars as the years passed.

The House can reproduce the pattern.

It cannot reproduce the food.

### House Behavior

If the players remove something from the pantry and return later, they may discover another object occupying the empty space. It need not be the same object. A removed tin of coffee might be replaced by tea. A missing jar of preserves might be replaced by an empty jar bearing the appropriate label.

The House is not creating food. It is restoring the arrangement it remembers.

If the players deliberately empty an entire shelf and later return, they may find it neatly filled with empty tins, bottles, and jars.

Everything is properly arranged.

Everything is correctly labeled.

Everything is useless.

**Her remains do not move or animate while the cabinet is in place.**

### The Blocked Entrance

The cabinet is propped against the secret panel. **It must be moved before the entrance to the artifact chamber can be opened.**

Moving it exposes Agatha’s remains fully, allowing the House to recognize her at last.

### The Treasure Is Hers

Agatha initially ignores the players and resumes searching for a way to open the panel.

> “Mine.”
>

>

She attacks anyone she believes intends to steal her discovery. Stepping away and acknowledging her claim may buy time, but she remains suspicious.

### Combat

Use the **Spectral Skeleton** stat block.

Agatha guards the entrance while continuing her search. When possible, one hand works at the panel while the other attacks or grasps an intruder.

Destroying her torso allows the surviving head and limbs to move independently, without granting additional actions. Her skull floats before the entrance, rasping “Mine,” while her scattered limbs continue searching and defending her claim.

The House encourages her throughout:

> “That’s it, Agatha. Just a little farther.”

### The Concealed Door

With the cabinet removed, the secret panel can be opened to reveal the entrance to the artifact chamber.

Agatha found the entrance. She never discovered what lay beyond it.

### The Listener

Resting within the crate is an object roughly the size of a large melon. Its shape resembles an egg or seed fashioned from pale translucent stone. The surface consists of countless loops and channels fused into a single lattice. Some pass through one another impossibly. Others emerge from the stone only to disappear back within it. The entire structure resembles blown glass that has somehow fossilized.

At the apex of the object is a single smooth opening approximately two inches across. Unlike the rest of the artifact, the opening is perfectly circular. Deliberate. Worn. As though countless hands have touched its edge over a very long time.

Looking into the aperture reveals no obvious interior cavity. Instead, a translucent passage curves gently downward before bending out of sight. Following its course proves strangely difficult. The tunnel appears to twist through the object at impossible angles, occasionally vanishing behind surfaces that should not obstruct the view. More than one observer becomes convinced the passage is larger inside than the object itself.

## Monsters

### Goblet

**Skills:** Athletics 1, Melee 1
**Armor:** 0
**Stamina:** N/A
**Fear:** N/A
**Health:** Head 1, Torso 1, Legs 1

**Actions (H)**
**Bite:** 1 damage.
**Eat Food:** Consumes a significant portion of food. As it feeds, the Goblet undergoes a rapid mitosis-like division, spawning one new Goblet.

**Special:** Goblets are sticky and may climb walls and ceilings without using limbs. Though bipedal, Goblets have no arms.

**Description:** A Goblet stands roughly one foot tall, its pale body resembling a lump of spoiled dough sagging beneath its own weight. It walks upon two stubby legs but possesses no arms. Most of its upper body consists of an oversized gullet capable of swallowing objects seemingly too large to fit inside it. Beyond the mouth is no ordinary throat, only a writhing mass of weevils forming a living funnel into its body.

Goblets are voracious scavengers, particularly attracted to flour, bread, and provisions carried by travelers. As a Goblet feeds, its dough-like body swells and begins developing a deep crease down its center. The mass gradually pulls apart until two smaller Goblets tear free of one another, each immediately searching for more food.

**Skills:** Melee 1
**Armor:** 0 · **Stamina:** n/a · **Fear:** n/a

### Psychic Manifestation

**Stamina:** 3
**Health:** R: 3, L: 3, H: 3, F: 4, T: 5.

**Special — Incorporeal:** The manifestation can pass through solid objects, walls, and doors. Physical attacks cannot harm it while incorporeal.

**Special — Manifest:** The creature can become corporeal to physically interact with reality. It remains corporeal until the start of its next turn. While corporeal, physical attacks affect it normally.

**Description:** A spectral creature given form by psychic activity. It drifts through solid matter, becoming tangible when it reaches out to affect the physical world.
