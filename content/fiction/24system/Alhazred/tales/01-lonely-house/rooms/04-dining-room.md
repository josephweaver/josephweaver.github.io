## Room 4: Dining Room

### At-a-Glance

The dining room connects to the Entrance Hall through a pair of pocket doors and to the Kitchen through a service door. A long walnut dining table dominates the room, accompanied by a sideboard, china cabinet, serving bell, and tall windows. The room establishes the household as a recurring social group and introduces Mr. J as the conspicuous absence at its center.

### Read Aloud

> The pocket doors open onto a formal dining room dominated by a long walnut table surrounded by high-backed chairs. A sideboard occupies the eastern wall beneath a collection of silver serving dishes, while a glass-fronted cabinet displays china decorated with a faded blue pattern.
>
> Everything is orderly. Almost excessively so.
>
> Six places have been carefully set around the table with plates, silverware, folded napkins, and empty wine glasses. At the head of the table sits a seventh chair. Its place has not been set. The chair has nevertheless been pulled slightly away from the table, as though its occupant stood only moments ago.
>
> Somewhere within the house comes a faint *tap... tap.*
>
> Then silence.

### The Empty Chair

The chair at the head of the table clearly belonged to the master of the house. Unlike the others, it shows considerable wear along the arms. There is nothing obviously supernatural about the chair itself. What is strange is that someone has carefully prepared places for six people while leaving Mr. J's place empty.

The House remembers Alice, Jacob, Thomas, Eleanor, Victor, and Agatha. It can reconstruct their places around the table, but it cannot properly reconstruct Mr. J. He remains the missing person around whom the entire story has been built.

If a player chooses to set a place for Mr. J, nothing immediately happens. The Guide should remember that they did.

### Clue: The Seating

The six settings are not quite identical. Small personal habits remain preserved around the table. One place has its wine glass pushed aside. Another has a napkin folded neatly beneath the fork. A small burn mark mars the wood beside another setting. One chair sits noticeably farther from the others.

A player examining these details experiences a brief memory of a crowded table and overlapping conversation. Agatha is complaining about something. Victor laughs. Eleanor corrects Mr. J on some matter of business. Alice watches her husband from across the table. Jacob stands awkwardly near the kitchen door, insisting that he has already eaten.

For a moment the player remembers all of them together. Then the memory disappears.

This memory does not associate the player with any particular role. Instead, it establishes that these people knew one another and had shared this table many times before.

### Clue: The Guest List

Inside the sideboard is a folded sheet of stationery containing a dinner list in Alice's handwriting:

> Eleanor
> Victor
> Thomas
> Aunt Agatha
> Jacob
>
> *Ask Jacob again. He will say no.*

The note is mundane, almost humorous. It suggests that Jacob was welcome at the table despite continuing to think of himself as an employee rather than a guest.

Beneath it is an older scrap of paper containing only the words:

> *Seven for dinner.*

No seven names are written.

### The Serving Bell

A small brass button beside the table once rang a bell in the kitchen. Pressing it produces no sound.

A few moments later, however, something shifts faintly in the kitchen. A cupboard opens. Crockery rattles. Perhaps someone takes a few steps across the floor.

If the players investigate, no one is there.

The House remembers what happened when the bell was rung. It is simply attempting to respond as it once did.

### House Behavior

The Dining Room demonstrates that the House understands ritual better than purpose. Dinner means people sit in their places. The table is prepared. The bell is rung. People talk. The House can reproduce all of these things. What it cannot understand is why, eventually, everyone stands up and leaves.

If the players remain in the room for some time, one of them may notice that an additional piece of silverware has appeared beside their plate. A little later, another appears.

The House is setting the table.

It has guests again.
