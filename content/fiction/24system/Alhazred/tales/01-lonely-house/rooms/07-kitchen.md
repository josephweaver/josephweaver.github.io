## Room 7: Kitchen

### At-a-Glance

The kitchen lies behind the Dining Room and connects to the rear service hall and pantry. It is a large working kitchen equipped with a cast-iron range, deep sink, preparation table, cupboards, and a smaller table where members of the household or staff might take informal meals. Unlike the formal rooms at the front of the house, this room feels practical and frequently used.

### Read Aloud

> The kitchen is considerably warmer than the rooms at the front of the house. A heavy cast-iron range occupies one wall, its iron surface still faintly warm beneath your hand. Copper pots hang above a scarred wooden preparation table, while shelves and cupboards hold crockery, flour tins, preserves, and the ordinary necessities of a well-kept household.
>
> Nothing here has the careful presentation of the dining room. Knives bear sharpening marks. The worktable has been cut and stained by years of use. One cabinet door refuses to close completely.
>
> Near the windows stands a small wooden table surrounded by mismatched chairs. A chipped coffee cup rests upon it beside yesterday's newspaper.
>
> Or what appears to be yesterday's newspaper.
>
> The date is October 16, 1926.
>
> For an empty house, the kitchen feels remarkably ready for breakfast.

### Clue: Jacob's Chair

One of the chairs at the small kitchen table is heavier than the others and has clearly been repaired several times. A wooden brace beneath the seat bears the initials `J.M.` cut crudely into the wood.

A player examining the chair experiences a brief memory of sitting here early in the morning with muddy boots stretched toward the stove. Alice places a cup of coffee on the table and asks whether the nursery will be finished before winter. The answer comes without hesitation.

> "It'll be ready."

There is a comfortable familiarity to the exchange. Jacob was an employee, but he was not treated entirely like one.

The player associates with **Jacob Miller**.

### Clue: The Household Ledger

A small ledger beside the pantry door contains ordinary household accounts written primarily in Alice's hand: flour, coffee, lamp oil, coal, soap, meat, repairs, and payments to tradesmen.

Toward the back, the entries begin to change.

There are purchases for the nursery.

Paint.

Curtain fabric.

A cradle mattress.

Baby blankets.

Then they stop.

Several blank pages follow before the ordinary household expenses resume.

Nothing explains why.

A player associated with Alice may experience the sensation of deliberately turning past those pages whenever she opened the book.

### The Serving Bell

A brass bell hangs high on the wall near the door to the Dining Room. A wire disappears into the plaster.

If the players previously pressed the serving bell in Room 4, this is where the sound should have led them.

There is nobody here.

Nevertheless, a cupboard stands open.

A clean plate has been placed upon the preparation table.

If the players press the Dining Room bell again while someone remains in the Kitchen, the kitchen bell rings normally.

Nothing else happens.

The House remembers that a ringing bell meant someone wanted something.

It does not remember exactly what came next.

### The Stove

The cast-iron range is warm despite the absence of anyone tending it. Opening the firebox reveals a small bed of glowing coals.

They should have gone cold hours ago.

If extinguished, the stove remains cold for the remainder of the scene.

If the players later return to the Kitchen, however, they may find it warm again.

This is not intended as a threat.

The House believes a home should be warm.

### House Behavior

The Kitchen is one of the places where the House's attempts at hospitality become most obvious. Cups may appear beside seated characters. A kettle placed upon the stove may begin heating even though no one has added fuel. A cupboard left open may later be found neatly closed.

Nothing should happen dramatically enough to prove the supernatural. Every incident can still be rationalized individually.

Taken together, however, they suggest that the supposedly empty house is taking care of its guests.

The House remembers having people to feed.

It remembers warmth, conversation, and the sound of dishes being washed after dinner.

It does not understand why those things stopped.

Now there are people in the kitchen again.

And it would very much like to keep it that way.
