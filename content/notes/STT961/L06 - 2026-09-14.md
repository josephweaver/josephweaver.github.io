# 1. Empirical Measure and Empirical Process - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.1, p. 80

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.1. Empirical Processes

**Def.** The empirical measure $P_n$ of a sample of random elements $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ in a measurable space $(\mathcal X,\mathcal A)$ is a discrete random measure:

$$
P_n(C)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n I(X_i\in C)
=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{X_i}(C),\qquad C\in\mathcal A.
$$

$\delta_{X_i}$: Dirac measure.

*Textbook clarification: The notes call this “Dirac's $\delta$-function.” Here $\delta_x$ is the measure assigning $C$ the value $I(x\in C)$, as in §2.1, p. 80, rather than an ordinary density function.*

Given a collection $\mathcal F$ of measurable functions $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$, the empirical measure induces a map $\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$ by

$$
f\longmapsto P_nf=\int f\,dP_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i).
$$

**Remark.** $Pf=\int f\,dP$.

**Def.** Let $P$ be the common distribution of the $X_i$. Define

$$
f\longmapsto G_nf
=\sqrt n(P_n-P)f
=\int f\,d\{\sqrt n(P_n-P)\}
=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(f(X_i)-\int f\,dP\right).
$$

$G_n:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$ is called the empirical process indexed by $\mathcal F$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The observations need not be real numbers or vectors. They could be functions, graphs, images, or other random elements in a measurable space. The empirical-measure construction is familiar, but its setting is more general.

The notation $Pf$ emphasizes that integration turns a function into a real number. A measure can therefore be viewed as a map acting on a class of functions. In response to a question, the professor said that the course will later consider continuity and derivatives of suitable maps involving measures, but no such derivative was defined here.

For a fixed function, $G_nf$ is simply a centered sample average with the usual square-root sample-size scaling. The novelty is to let the indexing function vary over a whole class.

# 2. Fixed-Function Limits and Glivenko-Cantelli Classes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.1, p. 81

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

For a fixed $f\in\mathcal F$:

**1. LLN.**

$$
P_nf\xrightarrow{\mathrm{as}}Pf,
\qquad P|f|<\infty.
$$

**2. CLT.**

$$
G_nf\rightsquigarrow N\left(0,P(f-Pf)^2\right),
\qquad Pf^2<\infty.
$$

*Textbook clarification: My notes say “provided $Pf$ exists” and “provided $Pf^2$ exists.” For these finite-valued limits the conditions are finite first absolute moment and finite second moment, respectively. The usual LLN and CLT here are for independent, identically distributed observations with law $P$, not merely observations sharing a common marginal law. This is the empirical-process setup in §2.1, pp. 80-83.*

**Def.**

$$
\|Q\|_{\mathcal F}
=\sup\{|Qf|:f\in\mathcal F\}
=\sup\left\{\left|\int f\,dQ\right|:f\in\mathcal F\right\}.
$$

**Def.** A class $\mathcal F$ for which

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0
$$

in outer probability or outer almost surely is called a **Glivenko-Cantelli class**:

$$
P^\star\text{-GC}\quad\text{or}\quad\mathrm{as}^\star\text{-GC class}.
$$

The dependence on the law $P$ can be indicated by “$P$-Glivenko-Cantelli.”

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The question is whether the fixed-function law of large numbers can hold uniformly over a class. The professor motivated this with a class of models, such as neural networks with a specified architecture: one may want a guarantee that applies throughout the class rather than only to a single model chosen in advance. A uniform guarantee can then support consistency of a method selected from that class.

The supremum notation is a convenient way to write this uniform comparison of empirical and population averages. Its role is to control all the functions under consideration at once.

# 3. Donsker Classes and the Brownian Bridge - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.1, equations (2.1.1)-(2.1.2), pp. 81-82

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Suppose

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|f(x)-Pf|<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathcal X.
$$

Then $G_n$ can be viewed as a map into $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

*Correction: My notes write $G_n:\mathcal X\to\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$. The empirical process depends on the entire sample: on a canonical finite-sample space it is $G_n:\mathcal X^n\to\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$, or it is a map from the underlying probability space on which the sample is defined. The function-indexed map for a realized sample is $f\mapsto G_nf$. See §2.1, pp. 80-81 and the canonical-space convention on p. 83.*

**Def.** A class $\mathcal F$ for which

$$
G_n\rightsquigarrow G\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
$$

where $G$ is a tight Borel measurable element in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$, is called a **Donsker class**, or **$P$-Donsker class**.

**Remark 1.** If $G_n\rightsquigarrow G$, then for every $f_1,\ldots,f_k\in\mathcal F$, the multidimensional CLT gives

$$
(G_nf_1,\ldots,G_nf_k)\rightsquigarrow N_k(0,\Sigma),
$$

where

$$
\Sigma_{ij}=P\bigl[(f_i-Pf_i)(f_j-Pf_j)\bigr].
$$

Weak convergence in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$ implies convergence of marginals. Thus $\{Gf:f\in\mathcal F\}$ must be a Gaussian process with mean zero and covariance function

$$
E(Gf_1\,Gf_2)
=P\bigl[(f_1-Pf_1)(f_2-Pf_2)\bigr]
=P(f_1f_2)-(Pf_1)(Pf_2).
$$

This is the **$P$-Brownian bridge**.

**Example.**

$$
\mathcal F=\{I_{[0,t]}:0\leq t\leq1\}.
$$

[The notes draw a bridge path pinned at zero at both endpoints of $[0,1]$.]

*Textbook clarification: The familiar standard Brownian bridge arises for these indicators when $P$ is the uniform distribution on $[0,1]$. The general covariance above is the $P$-Brownian-bridge covariance (§2.1, p. 82); the uniform-law qualification is not written beside the sketch.*

**Remark 2.** If $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker, then it is $P$-Glivenko-Cantelli, in outer probability and outer almost surely.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The Donsker property is the process version of a central limit theorem. The earlier work in $\ell^\infty(T)$ now applies with the function class $\mathcal F$ taking the place of the general index set $T$.

Finite-dimensional limits identify the mean and covariance of the candidate Gaussian limit. The professor asked the class to recognize the bridge covariance from the classical Donsker theorem. Although the index set now consists of functions, the covariance retains the same centered-product structure.

The professor contrasted the uniform LLN with the uniform CLT: the latter gives the former, but the two are not the same requirement. No proof of the outer-almost-sure implication was supplied in this lecture.

# 4. Empirical Distribution Functions - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.1, Example 2.1.3, p. 82

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. in $\mathbb R^d$. Let

$$
\mathcal F=\{I_{(-\infty,t]}:t\in\mathbb R^d\}.
$$

Then

$$
t\longmapsto P_nI_{(-\infty,t]}
=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n I(X_i\leq t),
$$

viewed in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$, is the empirical distribution function. The vector inequality is coordinatewise.

For every probability law $P$, $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker by the classical Donsker theorem.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

In one dimension the indicators correspond to left half-lines; in several dimensions they correspond to lower-left orthants. Identifying an indicator with its endpoint $t$ connects the function-indexed notation with the familiar empirical CDF.

The professor said that these classical cases will also follow from the more general tools developed later. The class names honor the earlier results; they do not mean that the original classical theorems were already stated for arbitrary function spaces.

# 5. Pre-Gaussian Classes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.1.2, p. 89; §1.5, Example 1.5.10

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Remark 3.** A Gaussian process $\{Gf:f\in\mathcal F\}$ with the specified mean and covariance exists on one probability space by Kolmogorov's theorem.

**Def.** A class $\mathcal F$ is called **pre-Gaussian** if there exists a tight Borel measurable version of this Gaussian process $G$ from some probability space $(\Omega',\mathcal A',P')$ into $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

*Correction: My notes append “in $G_n\rightsquigarrow G$” to this existence statement. Pre-Gaussianity requires existence of the tight Gaussian candidate with the specified covariance, not convergence of the empirical process on another probability space. Removed that extra convergence requirement. Section 2.1.2, p. 89, explicitly distinguishes pre-Gaussianity from the stronger Donsker property. The recording also becomes misleading at this point, so it is not used to add a different-space convergence assertion.*

**Def.** Introduce the seminorm

$$
\rho_P(f)=\bigl(P(f-Pf)^2\bigr)^{1/2}
=\left\{\int\left(f-\int f\,dP\right)^2\,dP\right\}^{1/2}.
$$

**Lemma.** $\mathcal F$ is pre-Gaussian if and only if $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded for the semimetric $(f,g)\mapsto\rho_P(f-g)$ and there exists a version of $G$ with uniformly $\rho_P$-continuous sample paths

$$
f\longmapsto Gf.
$$

(By Chapter 1.5.)

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor emphasized that specifying normal distributions for every finite collection is not the same step as constructing the whole process on one probability space. Kolmogorov's theorem addresses the consistency and existence issue; the additional tightness and path properties are what matter for the function-space limit.

The seminorm is related to variance. It is only a seminorm because a zero value need not mean the original function is identically zero. Applied to $f-g$, it becomes the semimetric used to compare indices in the class.

# 6. Asymptotic Equicontinuity - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.1.2, equation (2.1.8), pp. 89-90

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma.** For $\mathcal F\subset L^2(P)$, the class is $P$-Donsker if and only if it is totally bounded for $\rho_P$ and satisfies the asymptotic equicontinuity condition: for every $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star\left(
\sup_{\substack{f,g\in\mathcal F\\\rho_P(f-g)<\delta}}
|G_n(f-g)|>\varepsilon
\right)=0.
$$

*Correction: My notes label the total-boundedness requirement simply “in $L^2(P)$-norm,” while the displayed increment condition uses the centered seminorm $\rho_P$. The direct characterization uses $\rho_P$, as follows from §1.5, Theorem 1.5.7 and Example 1.5.10, and §2.1.2, p. 89. To substitute the uncentered $L^2(P)$ metric without loss requires the qualification $\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|Pf|<\infty$ given in the footnote on p. 89 and Problems 2.1.1-2.1.2, p. 93. Without it, unbounded constant functions show why the distinction matters.*

Equivalently, for every $\delta_n\downarrow0$,

$$
\|G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0,
$$

where

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \rho_P(f-g)<\delta\}.
$$

“$\delta$-enlargement of class of differences.”

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor emphasized that $\mathcal F_\delta$ is a class of differences, not simply a ball around one fixed function or a $\delta$-enlargement of the original class. Both $f$ and $g$ can vary, subject to being close in the indicated semimetric.

The goal of the coming lectures is to develop usable sufficient conditions for these uniform convergence statements. Rather than proving each result directly from its definition, one wants inequalities and general theorems that reduce the task to checking properties of a function class. Maximal inequalities will provide control over the suprema appearing in asymptotic equicontinuity.
