# 1. Maxima Under a Bernstein-Type Tail Bound - §2.2.2, Lemma 2.2.10, pp. 102-103

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma.** Let $X_1,\ldots,X_m$ be arbitrary random variables with

$$
P(|X_i|>x)\le2\exp\left\{-\frac12\frac{x^2}{b+ax}\right\},
\qquad x>0,\ i=1,\ldots,m,
$$

for some $a,b>0$. Then

$$
\left\|\max_{1\le i\le m}X_i\right\|_{\psi_1}
\le K\left[a\log(1+m)+\sqrt b\sqrt{\log(1+m)}\right],
$$

where $K$ is a universal constant.

*Correction: My notes put $|X_i|\le x$ inside the probability and say $x\in\mathbb R$. This is a tail bound, so the event is $|X_i|>x$ and the threshold is $x>0$ (Lemma 2.2.10, pp. 102-103). The chapter label at the top of the page is premature: this lemma is still in §2.2.2.*

**Proof.** If $0<x\le b/a$, then $ax\le b$, and

$$
P(|X_i|>x)\le2\exp\left\{-\frac12\frac{x^2}{2b}\right\}
=2e^{-x^2/(4b)}.
$$

This is a Gaussian tail. If $x>b/a$, then $ax>b$, and

$$
P(|X_i|>x)\le2\exp\left\{-\frac12\frac{x^2}{2ax}\right\}
=2e^{-x/(4a)}.
$$

This is an exponential tail. Use $\psi_2$ for the Gaussian part and $\psi_1$ for the exponential part. Write

$$
U_i=X_i1\{|X_i|\le b/a\},\qquad
V_i=X_i1\{|X_i|>b/a\},\qquad X_i=U_i+V_i.
$$

By the triangle inequality, comparison of Orlicz norms, and the maximal inequality,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\max_iX_i\right\|_{\psi_1}
&\le\left\|\max_i|U_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}
+\left\|\max_i|V_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}\\
&\le C\left\|\max_i|U_i|\right\|_{\psi_2}
+\left\|\max_i|V_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}\\
&\le K\psi_2^{-1}(m)\max_i\|U_i\|_{\psi_2}
+K\psi_1^{-1}(m)\max_i\|V_i\|_{\psi_1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Textbook clarification: Absolute values in the two intermediate maxima make the triangle bound valid for arbitrary signed variables, and the comparison of $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ norms is up to a constant. The two truncated variables satisfy the corresponding tail bounds for all positive thresholds (Lemma 2.2.10, p. 103).*

Recall

$$
\psi_1^{-1}(m)=\log(1+m),\qquad
\psi_2^{-1}(m)=\sqrt{\log(1+m)},
$$

and the earlier lemma

$$
P(|X|>x)\le K_0e^{-cx^p}
\implies
\|X\|_{\psi_p}\le\left(\frac{1+K_0}{c}\right)^{1/p}.
$$

Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\max_iX_i\right\|_{\psi_1}
&\le K\sqrt{\log(1+m)}\sqrt{\frac{1+2}{1/(4b)}}
+K\log(1+m)\frac{1+2}{1/(4a)}\\
&\le\widetilde K\left[a\log(1+m)+\sqrt b\sqrt{\log(1+m)}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The Bernstein form captures two regimes in one inequality: Gaussian behavior near the origin and exponential behavior farther out. Matching each piece with the appropriate Orlicz function is the main idea. Constants from the previous maximal inequalities depend only on the fixed functions $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$, so they can all be absorbed into a universal constant.

This concluded the lecture's treatment of §2.2. The professor connected the results to the techniques developed along the way: chaining, Chernoff optimization, and manipulations of integrals. The next technique is symmetrization.

# 2. Symmetrization and the Product Probability Space - §2.3.1, pp. 107-108

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_n$ be i.i.d. Rademacher random variables, independent of $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$:

$$
P(\epsilon_i=1)=P(\epsilon_i=-1)=\frac12.
$$

Compare

$$
f\longmapsto(P_n-P)f=\int f\,dP_n-\int f\,dP,
\qquad P_nf=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf(X_i),
$$

with

$$
f\longmapsto P_n^\circ f:=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i).
$$

**Idea:** limit theorems for $P_n-P$ correspond to limit theorems for $P_n^\circ$.

Conditionally on $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, the process $f\mapsto P_n^\circ f$ is a Rademacher process, hence sub-Gaussian.

The original space is $(\mathcal X^n,\mathcal A^n,P^n)$. We now work on

$$
(\mathcal X^n,\mathcal A^n,P^n)\times(\mathcal Z,\mathcal G,Q).
$$

*Textbook clarification: The displayed correspondence is the motivation, not an unconditional assertion that every limit theorem transfers without hypotheses. In the outer-expectation formulation, the data are coordinate projections on the product space, and auxiliary signs depend only on the additional coordinate (§2.3.1, pp. 107-108).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Both processes are centered. The first becomes the usual empirical process after multiplying by $\sqrt n$. The advantage of the second is conditional: once the observations are fixed, the available sub-Gaussian maximal inequalities apply. One must then track the data-dependent quantities that replace the deterministic weights in a Rademacher process. This is one reason universal constants matter.

The professor explained that the probability space is enlarged when independent auxiliary variables are needed. Rather than proving that all desired copies can be constructed on the original space, the proof explicitly adds product coordinates. Expectations with subscripts indicate which coordinates are integrated while the others remain fixed.

# 3. The First Symmetrization Inequality - §2.3.1, Lemma 2.3.1, pp. 108-109

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma 1.** For every nondecreasing convex $\Phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and class $\mathcal F$ of measurable functions,

$$
E^*\Phi(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F})
\le E^*\Phi(2\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}).
$$

**Proof.** Let $(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)$ be an independent copy of $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$. Enlarge the space to

$$
(\mathcal X^n,\mathcal A^n,P^n)
\times(\mathcal Z,\mathcal G,Q)
\times(\mathcal X^n,\mathcal A^n,P^n).
$$

Since $Ef(X_i)=Ef(Y_i)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
&=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X_i)-Ef(Y_i))\right|\\
&=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left|E_Y\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X_i)-f(Y_i))\right|\\
&\le\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}E_Y\left|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X_i)-f(Y_i))\right|\\
&\le E_Y^*\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X_i)-f(Y_i))\right|.
\end{aligned}
$$

Use monotonicity of $\Phi$, then Jensen's inequality, then take the outer expectation over $X$:

$$
E_X^*\Phi(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F})
\le E_X^*E_Y^*\Phi\left(\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X_i)-f(Y_i))\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\le E_{X,Y}^*\Phi\left(\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(f(X_i)-f(Y_i))\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right).
$$

*Correction: My notes run through iterated outer expectations as if they could always be exchanged with a joint outer expectation. The needed passage is an inequality, $E_X^*E_Y^*\le E_{X,Y}^*$, by Lemma 1.2.6, p. 11, exactly as used in the proof of Lemma 2.3.1, p. 109. No unrestricted outer Fubini equality is assumed.*

The differences are symmetric, so insert the independent signs:

$$
E^*\Phi(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F})
\le E_\epsilon E_{X,Y}^*\Phi\left(\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_i(f(X_i)-f(Y_i))\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right).
$$

Put

$$
A=\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F},
\qquad B=\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

By the triangle inequality, monotonicity, and convexity,

$$
\Phi(A+B)=\Phi\left(\frac12(2A)+\frac12(2B)\right)
\le\frac12\Phi(2A)+\frac12\Phi(2B).
$$

The $X$ and $Y$ terms have the same distribution. Thus the bound is

$$
E^*\Phi\left(2\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)\right|\right)
=E^*\Phi(2\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor explained each use of the assumptions on $\Phi$: nondecreasingness preserves inequalities after the supremum and triangle bounds, while convexity permits Jensen's inequality and the final averaging of the two doubled terms. The basic supremum step is that every individual function is bounded by the supremum over the class, so taking expectation and then supremum gives the required inequality.

The independent-copy difference is symmetric because exchanging $X_i$ and $Y_i$ changes its sign without changing its law. That is what permits Rademacher weights to be inserted. The same construction is used in machine learning. For empirical-process limit theorems, it shifts much of the difficult equicontinuity work to conditional sub-Gaussian inequalities, leaving the task of controlling the resulting quantities that depend on the data.

# 4. Two-Sided Symmetrization for Centered Processes - §2.3.2, Lemma 2.3.6, pp. 111-112

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma 2.** Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ be independent stochastic processes with mean zero. Then

$$
E^*\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\le E^*\Phi\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\le E^*\Phi\left(2\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
$$

for every nondecreasing convex $\Phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ and arbitrary functions $\mu_i:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$.

*Correction: My notes omit “convex” from the qualification after this statement. It is required for both inequalities (Lemma 2.3.6, pp. 111-112). The Rademacher variables are independent of the processes and the outer expectations use the product-space convention.*

The second inequality follows by redoing the proof of Lemma 1.

For the first, let $(Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)$ be an independent copy of $(Z_1,\ldots,Z_n)$. Fix any $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\in\{-1,1\}^n$. Since $EY_i(f)=0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_Z^*\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum e_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
&=E_Z^*\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum e_i(Z_i-E_YY_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)\\
&\le E_{Z,Y}^*\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum e_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)\\
&=E_{Z,Y}^*\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)\\
&\le E_{Z,Y}^*\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
+\frac12\left\|\sum Y_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)\\
&\le\frac12E_{Z,Y}^*\Phi\left(\left\|\sum Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
+\frac12E_{Z,Y}^*\Phi\left(\left\|\sum Y_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)\\
&=E_Z^*\Phi\left(\left\|\sum Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Average over the fixed sign vectors to obtain the left inequality.

*Correction: The handwritten proof moves directly from random signs to an arbitrary fixed sign vector. The random-sign expectation is the average of the fixed-sign expressions, not their individual value. Signs may be removed from the differences $Z_i-Y_i$ because those differences are symmetric; mean zero alone does not imply symmetry of $Z_i$ (Lemma 2.3.6, p. 112).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

This generalizes the first lemma in two ways: it applies to general independent centered processes rather than just the empirical measure, and it gives a comparison in both directions. The same independent-copy, Jensen, triangle, and convexity steps are reused. Arbitrary deterministic functions $\mu_i$ are allowed in the upper bound because they cancel when the independent-copy difference is formed.

The professor announced a separate probability symmetrization lemma for the next lecture. The choice $\Phi(x)=x$ works here, but a threshold indicator is not convex, so probability inequalities cannot simply be obtained by inserting that indicator into the present result.
