# 1. Outer Expectation and Outer Probability (van der Vaart & Wellner, §1.2, p. 6)

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 1.2. Preliminaries

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal A,P)$ be an arbitrary Prob. space.

$$
T:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}=[-\infty,\infty]
$$

is a map.

**Def.** The outer integral of $T$ with respect to $P$ is

$$
E^*T
:=
\inf\left\{
EU:
U\ge T,\;
U:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}
\text{ is measurable and }EU\text{ exists}
\right\}.
$$

*Textbook clarification (van der Vaart & Wellner, §1.2, p. 6): $EU$ exists when at least one of $EU^+$ and $EU^-$ is finite; both cannot be infinite.*

The outer prob. of an arbitrary set is

$$
P^*(B)
:=
\inf\left\{
P(A):
A\supset B,\;
A\in\mathcal A
\right\}.
$$

**Remark.** Inner integral

$$
E_*T=-E^*(-T).
$$

$$
P_*(B)=1-P^*(\Omega-B).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

$\overline{\mathbb R}$ is the **extended real line**, so $T$ is allowed to take the values $-\infty$ and $+\infty$. Unlike an ordinary random variable in classical probability, the map $T$ is not necessarily measurable. This is one of the issues the course needs to handle.

In modern probability and statistics, objects can arise for which measurability is difficult to establish or the measurable structure becomes too complicated to work with directly. To define $E^*T$, consider **measurable upper bounds** $U\ge T$ whose expectations exist, and take the infimum of their expectations.

Outer probability is the analogous construction for sets: surround an arbitrary set $B$ by measurable sets $A\supset B$ and take the smallest possible probability. In classical probability, a set outside the $\sigma$-algebra normally is not assigned a probability. Outer probability provides a way to work with such sets without rebuilding the probability space.

Inner expectation can be obtained from outer expectation by the negative transformation

$$
E_*T=-E^*(-T),
$$

which converts the infimum construction into the corresponding lower/supremum construction.

The professor intends to use **outer expectation as the default notion of expectation** later in the course, eventually suppressing the $*$ when the context is clear. This lets the course avoid repeatedly dealing with measurability issues.

# 2. Measurable Cover Function (Envelope) (van der Vaart & Wellner, Lemma 1.2.1, pp. 6–7)

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma** (about measurable cover function (envelope))

For any map $T:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}$ $\exists$ a measurable map $T^*:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}$ such that

$$
T^*\ge T
\quad\text{and}\quad
T^*\le U\text{ a.s.}
$$

For every measurable $U:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}$ with $U\ge T$ a.s.

Then for any such $T^*$ we have

$$
E^*T=ET^*
$$

provided $ET^*$ exists.

$$
E^*T<\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad ET^*\text{ exists}.
$$

*Correction: My notes had an incomplete finiteness statement. Lemma 1.2.1 (p. 7) confirms that $E^\star T<\infty$ guarantees $ET^\star$ exists. Also made explicit that the competing majorant $U$ must be measurable.*

**Def.** $T^*$ is a minimal measurable majorant.

(there is a whole equivalence class of a.s. equal functions)

$$
T_*=-(-T)^*
$$

is a maximal measurable minorant.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor described this as an important lemma about a *measurable cover function*, sometimes called an *envelope*. However, the term envelope is used for several different objects in weak convergence theory, so its meaning depends on the context.

The lemma establishes that for any arbitrary map $T$, even when $T$ is not measurable, there exists a measurable function $T^\star$ that bounds it from above and is almost surely no larger than any other measurable upper bound. The outer expectation of $T$ can therefore be calculated using the ordinary expectation of $T^\star$, provided that expectation exists.

The professor emphasized that this measurable cover is not unique as a function. There is an entire equivalence class of measurable covers that are equal almost surely. This resembles ordinary integration, where modifying a function on a set of measure zero does not change its integral.

The main significance is that **we can return to classical probability theory**. Instead of working directly with a potentially nonmeasurable map, we can work with its measurable cover and apply familiar integration results.

The professor noted that the proof is constructive and resembles a real analysis proof but skipped it, recommending the textbook for anyone interested.

A corresponding construction produces the maximal measurable minorant $T_\star$. This provides the lower counterpart of the minimal measurable majorant and will also be useful when discussing Fubini's theorem.

# 3. Fubini's Theorems — Classical Fubini (van der Vaart & Wellner, §1.2, pp. 10–11 (background to Lemma 1.2.6))

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Fubini's Theorems

Let

$$
T:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}
$$

be a map on

$$
(\Omega_1\times\Omega_2,\mathcal A_1\times\mathcal A_2,P_1\times P_2).
$$

**Remark:** classical Fubini's Th.

If $f$ is nonnegative and measurable on $A\times B$ (or integrable with respect to the product measure), then

$$
\int_{A\times B}f(x,y)\,dP(x,y)
=
\int_A\left(\int_B f(x,y)\,dP_2(y)\right)dP_1(x).
$$

*Correction: My notes reversed the differential order in the iterated integral. Corrected the inner measure to $P_2$ and outer measure to $P_1$. Also added the nonnegativity or integrability condition required for the stated equality to be well-defined.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced Fubini's theorem in the context of a product probability space. We begin with two probability spaces and construct their product, giving us a larger probability space on which functions of two coordinates can be defined. The map $T$ need not be measurable, although the classical theorem initially considers measurable functions.

Classical Fubini's theorem establishes that integration over a product space can be performed through repeated integration over the individual spaces. The integral over the product measure agrees with the corresponding iterated integral, under the appropriate measurability and integrability conditions.

The professor emphasized that this apparently simple equality conceals substantial mathematical work. In particular, **after integrating over one coordinate, the resulting function of the other coordinate must be measurable**. Establishing this is an important part of classical integration theory. The proof involves approximations, limits, and inequalities in both directions.

The purpose of reviewing classical Fubini's theorem is to establish a starting point for extending it to potentially nonmeasurable functions using outer and inner expectations. The professor indicated that the extended result can be proved by returning to classical Fubini's theorem through measurable majorants.

# 4. Fubini in the Non-Measurable World — Theorem 1 (van der Vaart & Wellner, Lemma 1.2.6, p. 11)

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Fubini in Non-Measurable World

**Th. 1.**

$$
E_*T
\leq E_{1*}E_{2*}T
\leq E_1^*E_2^*T
\leq E^*T.
$$

Where $\forall \omega_1\in\Omega_1$,

$$
(E_2^*T)(\omega_1)
=
\inf\left\{
\int U(\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2):
\begin{array}{l}
U:\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}\text{ meas.}\\
U(\omega_2)\geq T(\omega_1,\omega_2)
\quad\forall\omega_2\\
\text{and }\int U\,dP_2\text{ exists}
\end{array}
\right\}.
$$

$$
E_1^*\left(\underbrace{E_2^*T}_{\text{map in }\Omega_1\to\overline{\mathbb R}}\right)
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced a generalized version of Fubini's theorem that applies even when $T$ is not measurable. Unlike classical Fubini, which establishes equality between the product integral and iterated integrals, the generalized theorem establishes a **chain of inequalities involving inner and outer expectations**.

For each fixed $\omega_1$, we consider the function $\omega_2\mapsto T(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ and calculate its outer expectation with respect to $P_2$. This produces a new map $E_2^*T$ defined on $\Omega_1$. Importantly, **this resulting map need not be measurable**. Nevertheless, we can still take its outer expectation with respect to $P_1$.

In classical Fubini's theorem, measurability of the function resulting from the first integration is an important requirement that must be established. By using outer expectations, we can construct the iterated integral without first proving that the intermediate function is measurable.

The professor emphasized that the inner and outer iterated expectations need not coincide. Unlike classical Fubini, equality is not guaranteed for arbitrary nonmeasurable maps. The theorem gives bounds within which these expectations lie.

The professor noted that the proof is relatively straightforward compared with classical Fubini because it uses the measurable majorant from the preceding lemma. This allows the argument to return to classical integration theory rather than developing a completely new integration theorem.

# 5. Fubini in the Non-Measurable World — Proof of Theorem 1 (van der Vaart & Wellner, Lemma 1.2.6, p. 11)

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Proof** only proof

$$
E_1^*E_2^*T\leq E^*T.
$$

Assume $E^*T<\infty$ by lemma find $T^*$;

$$
E^*T=ET^*
$$

with $(T^*)^+$ integrable.

$\omega_2\mapsto T^*(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ is a meas. majorant for $P_1$-a.a. $\omega_1$, of $\omega_2\mapsto T(\omega_1,\omega_2)$.

$$
\int T^*(\omega_1,\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2)
\geq E_2^*T(\omega_1)
$$

for a.a. $\omega_1$.

$$
\int T^*
=
\int(T^*)^+-\int(T^*)^-.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor chose to prove only the inequality $E_1^\star E_2^\star T\leq E^\star T$. The middle inequality follows from the relationship between inner and outer expectations, while the remaining inequality can be obtained by applying the negative transformation used to define inner expectation. This latter argument was left as homework.

The proof begins by assuming $E^\star T<\infty$, since the case $E^\star T=+\infty$ makes the desired upper bound immediate. By the measurable cover lemma, we can choose a measurable majorant $T^*$ whose ordinary expectation equals the outer expectation of $T$.

For almost every $\omega_1$, the section $\omega_2\mapsto T^\star (\omega_1,\omega_2)$ is a measurable majorant of the corresponding section of $T$. Its integral with respect to $P_2$ must therefore be at least the infimum defining $E_2^\star T(\omega_1)$.

The professor then returned to **classical Fubini's theorem**. Because the measurable majorant can be decomposed into its positive and negative parts, the integration can be handled through these components under the stated integrability condition.

The resulting function of $\omega_1$, obtained by integrating $T^*$ over $\omega_2$, provides a measurable majorant for $E_2^*T$. Taking its expectation with respect to $P_1$ and applying classical Fubini gives the desired inequality:

$$
E_1^*E_2^*T
\leq
\int_{\Omega_1}\int_{\Omega_2}
T^*(\omega_1,\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2)\,dP_1(\omega_1)
=
ET^*
=
E^*T.
$$

The central idea is that **the generalized Fubini inequality can be established using classical Fubini applied to a measurable majorant**. The professor emphasized that this is a recurring mathematical technique: extend an existing theorem by reducing the new problem to a setting where the classical result already applies.

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor assigned the remaining inequality from Theorem 1 as homework, suggesting the use of the identity $E_*T=-E^*(-T)$ to convert an outer expectation inequality into an inner expectation inequality. This allows the preceding proof to be reused with the appropriate change of signs.

Theorem 2 addresses circumstances under which the generalized Fubini inequality becomes an equality. The first probability space is assumed to be a **separable metric space equipped with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra**. The function $T$ must also satisfy a condition controlling how much its value changes when its first argument changes.

Specifically, changes in $T$ between two points $\omega_1$ and $\omega_1'$ are bounded by their metric distance multiplied by a function $H$ of the second coordinate. The outer expectation of $H$ must be finite. This condition is required for sufficiently small distances between the points.

Under these conditions, the outer expectation over the product space equals the repeated expectation. Importantly, **$T$ itself is not required to be measurable**, but additional structure is needed to recover equality.

The professor emphasized that this result is not automatic for arbitrary nonmeasurable functions. The assumptions concerning separability, the Borel $\sigma$-algebra, and the metric provide the structure needed for the theorem. These concepts would be reviewed in the next lecture.

The proof was omitted because it relies on standard integration theory rather than introducing techniques central to the course. The professor recommended consulting the textbook for the proof.
