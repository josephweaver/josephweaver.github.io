# 1. Shattering and the VC Index - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.1, pp. 134-135

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.6. Vapnik-Cervonenkis Classes of Sets and Functions (VC Classes)

**Definition.** Let $\mathcal C$ be a collection of subsets of a set $\mathcal X$.

An arbitrary set of $n$ points $\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ has $2^n$ subsets. We say $\mathcal C$ picks out a subset of $\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ if this set can be formed as

$$
C\cap\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}
\qquad\text{for some }C\in\mathcal C.
$$

$\mathcal C$ shatters $\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ if each of its $2^n$ subsets can be picked out by $\mathcal C$.

The VC index of a class $\mathcal C$ is the smallest $n$ for which no set of size $n$ is shattered by $\mathcal C$.

$$
\Delta_n(\mathcal C,x_1,\ldots,x_n)
=\operatorname{card}\{C\cap\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}:C\in\mathcal C\},
$$

$$
V(\mathcal C)=\inf\left\{n:\max_{x_1,\ldots,x_n}
\Delta_n(\mathcal C,x_1,\ldots,x_n)<2^n\right\}.
$$

A collection $\mathcal C$ is called a VC class of sets if $V(\mathcal C)<\infty$.

*Textbook clarification: The notes use “VC index (dimension).” The convention here is the textbook's VC index, the first size that cannot be shattered. The convention that calls the largest shattered size the VC dimension differs by one. The infimum of the empty set is $\infty$; the textbook's VC classes consist of measurable sets (§2.6.1, p. 135).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced VC classes through their connection with statistical learning. The opening historical discussion emphasized that mathematical ideas can take a long time to be recognized and used. The class terminology is named after Vapnik and Chervonenkis.

“Picking out” concerns the intersection with the chosen finite collection of points, not the whole set $C$ outside that collection. Many different members of $\mathcal C$ can produce the same intersection, but that intersection counts only once in $\Delta_n$. Shattering requires all possible subsets, including the empty set and the entire collection.

The professor warned that the definitions can feel abstract on a first encounter and turned to examples to make the combinatorial condition concrete. The maximum in the definition asks whether any arrangement of $n$ points can be shattered; failure must hold for every such arrangement at the first nonshattered size.

# 2. Half-Lines and Intervals - van der Vaart & Wellner, Example 2.6.1, p. 135

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 1.**

$$
\mathcal C=\{(-\infty,c]:c\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

For $n=1$, both $\varnothing$ and $\{x_1\}$ can be picked out.

For $n=2$, take $x_1<x_2$. The subsets $\varnothing$, $\{x_1\}$, and $\{x_1,x_2\}$ can be picked out. It is impossible to pick out $\{x_2\}$ alone.

$$
V(\mathcal C)=2.
$$

**Example 2.**

$$
\mathcal C=\{[a,b]:a<b,\ a,b\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

For $n=1$ and $n=2$, the points can be shattered. For $n=3$, with $x_1<x_2<x_3$, an interval cannot pick out $x_1$ and $x_3$ alone.

$$
V(\mathcal C)=3.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The half-lines $(-\infty,c]$ are the familiar sets whose probabilities define a cumulative distribution function. The obstruction with two points is geometric: once a left half-line reaches the rightmost point, it necessarily includes the smaller point as well. Moving the cutoff cannot overcome that obstruction.

Allowing both endpoints of an interval to vary makes the class richer and permits every subset of two points. With three ordered points, however, selecting the smallest and largest forces inclusion of the middle point. The professor connected intervals with the usual construction of integration: intervals lead to step functions, which are then used to build more general measurable and integrable functions. These elementary classes provide familiar examples of controlled combinatorial complexity.

# 3. Half-Spaces Through the Origin - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.5, dimension argument related to Lemma 2.6.15, p. 146

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 3.** The space is $S=\mathbb R^d$, and

$$
\mathcal H=\{\text{all half-spaces with boundary passing through }0\}.
$$

**Claim.** $V(\mathcal H)=d+1$.

We need to show that there exist $d$ vectors in $\mathbb R^d$ that are shattered by $\mathcal H$, and that every set of $d+1$ vectors in $\mathbb R^d$ cannot be shattered by $\mathcal H$.

Let

$$
v_i=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0),\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,
$$

with $1$ in the $i$th coordinate. For every $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}$, take $\lambda_i>0$ for $i\in J$ and $\lambda_i<0$ for $i\notin J$. Then $\lambda\in\mathbb R^d$ and

$$
\langle\lambda,v_i\rangle=\lambda_i,
$$

$$
\lambda_i>0\iff i\in J\iff\langle\lambda,v_i\rangle>0
\iff v_i\in H,
$$

where

$$
H=\{h:\langle\lambda,h\rangle\geq0\}\in\mathcal H.
$$

Let $\{x_1,\ldots,x_{d+1}\}$ be arbitrary vectors in $\mathbb R^d$. After relabeling, there exist $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\in\mathbb R$ such that

$$
x_{d+1}=\sum_{i=1}^d\alpha_i x_i.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The notes suppress the relabeling. Linear dependence of $d+1$ vectors guarantees a nonzero coefficient in a dependence relation, and that vector can be solved for and labeled $x_{d+1}$. It does not guarantee that an arbitrarily preselected last vector is in the span of the first $d$. This is the finite-dimensional dependence argument used in Lemma 2.6.15, p. 146.*

Suppose we can shatter this set by $\mathcal H$. Consider

$$
J=\{i:1\leq i\leq d,\ \alpha_i\geq0\}\subset\{1,\ldots,d+1\}.
$$

There is a half-space $H\in\mathcal H$ with $x_i\in H$ if and only if $i\in J$. Thus $x_{d+1}\notin H$. Write

$$
H=\{h:\langle h,\ell\rangle\geq0\}.
$$

But

$$
\langle x_{d+1},\ell\rangle
=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^d\alpha_i x_i,\ell\right\rangle
=\sum_{i=1}^d\alpha_i\langle x_i,\ell\rangle\geq0.
$$

For $i\in J$, both factors are nonnegative; for $i\notin J$, both are negative. Consequently $x_{d+1}\in H$, a contradiction.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The half-space example moves from the real line to a vector space. The professor described VC arguments as often combinatorial and elementary because the question concerns finite sets, even when the ambient space is continuous.

The two directions of the index calculation have different quantifiers. To show that the index exceeds $d$, one convenient collection of $d$ vectors is enough, so the canonical basis is chosen. To rule out index greater than $d+1$, the argument must handle every collection of $d+1$ vectors. Linear dependence supplies the obstruction. The professor explicitly allowed relabeling when the initially chosen last vector cannot be written in terms of the others.

In the contradiction step, the selected index set deliberately excludes the dependent vector. The signs of the coefficients determine which of the other vectors must be included. Those signs force the dependent vector into the same half-space, contradicting the requested selection.

# 4. General Half-Spaces and Convex Sets - van der Vaart & Wellner, Problems 2.6.14-15, p. 152

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 4.** $S=\mathbb R^d$,

$$
\mathcal H=\{\text{all half-spaces}\}
=\bigl\{\{h:\langle\ell,h\rangle\geq c\}:\ell\in\mathbb R^d,\ c\in\mathbb R\bigr\}.
$$

$$
V(\mathcal H)=d+2.
$$

**Example 5. Not VC.** For $d\geq2$, let $S=\mathbb R^d$ and

$$
\mathcal C=\{\text{all convex sets}\}.
$$

$$
V(\mathcal C)=\infty.
$$

The diagram places arbitrarily many points on a circle and joins selected points to form a convex polygon. The class is “too rich.”

*Correction: My notes stated this for $\mathbb R^d$ without a dimension restriction. The non-VC assertion requires $d\geq2$; in one dimension convex sets are intervals. Problem 2.6.15, p. 152, states the restriction and uses points on the rim of the unit ball. The diagram means that one chooses points on a circle, not that an arbitrary configuration is transformed into a circle.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Removing the requirement that the boundary pass through the origin introduces an additional free constant. The professor explained the increase from $d+1$ to $d+2$ by absorbing that constant into an extra coordinate and viewing the affine half-space as a homogeneous half-space in one higher dimension.

The convex-set example shows that a geometrically natural class can still be too rich to be VC. The circle construction permits every selection from arbitrarily large finite collections placed on the circle. The professor contrasted this with restricting polygons to a fixed maximum number of sides. Such a restriction limits the number of half-spaces being intersected and gives a VC class, with complexity depending on the dimension and the fixed bound on the number of sides.

# 5. Sign Sets of a Finite-Dimensional Function Space - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.5, Lemma 2.6.17(v), p. 147

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 6.** Let $\mathcal F$ be a $d$-dimensional linear space of functions on $S$. Define

$$
\mathcal C=\bigl\{\{x\in S:f(x)\geq0\}:f\in\mathcal F\bigr\}.
$$

**Claim.** $V(\mathcal C)=d+1$.

**Proof.** There is a linearly independent basis $g_1,\ldots,g_d$ in $\mathcal F$. For every $f\in\mathcal F$,

$$
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^d\alpha_i g_i(x)
\qquad\text{for some }\alpha\in\mathbb R^d,\quad x\in S.
$$

Define

$$
G:S\to\mathbb R^d,\qquad G(x)=(g_1(x),\ldots,g_d(x)).
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\{x:f(x)\geq0\}
&=\left\{x:\sum_{i=1}^d\alpha_i g_i(x)\geq0\right\}\\
&=\{x:\langle\alpha,G(x)\rangle\geq0\}
=G^{-1}(H_\alpha),
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
H_\alpha=\{h:\langle h,\alpha\rangle\geq0\}.
$$

Both inclusions hold, so

$$
\mathcal C=G^{-1}(\mathcal H),\qquad V(\mathcal C)=V(\mathcal H)=d+1.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The notes infer equality of VC indices directly from the inverse-image representation. Lemma 2.6.17(v), p. 147, guarantees only $V(G^{-1}\mathcal H)\leq V(\mathcal H)$ for a general map. Here equality also holds because linear independence of the $g_i$ implies that the vectors $G(x)$ span $\mathbb R^d$. Choosing $d$ independent such vectors permits all sign patterns, giving the required lower bound. This supplies the missing justification, without changing the claim. This is a class of zero-level sets; it is not the VC-subgraph statement of Lemma 2.6.15.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

This final example was intended to connect the complexity of a class of sets with the ordinary linear dimension of a space of functions. The professor suggested polynomial spaces of fixed finite dimension as an example. Writing each function in a basis turns its nonnegative set into a condition on the inner product of its coefficient vector with the evaluation vector $G(x)$.

The professor emphasized the role of linear independence: a redundant collection of basis functions would describe a smaller-dimensional space. The transcript describes the map $G$ as onto or bijective, but that does not follow from linear independence. The relevant fact is that its image spans $\mathbb R^d$, as clarified above. The intended argument is the reduction to half-spaces and the preservation of enough independent evaluation vectors to realize the lower bound.

The professor then connected VC complexity to uniform Glivenko-Cantelli results and, in statistical learning, to consistency of sample-based procedures. Because empirical measures are supported on finitely many observed points, a characterization in terms of finite selections is especially natural. The next lecture would use these combinatorial properties to obtain entropy bounds and apply the empirical-process results already proved, rather than establish every uniform convergence theorem separately.
