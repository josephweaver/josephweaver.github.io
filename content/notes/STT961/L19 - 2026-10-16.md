# 1. Smooth Function Classes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.7.1, p. 154

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.7. Bracketing Numbers

For $\alpha>0$, denote by $\underline\alpha$ the greatest integer strictly smaller than $\alpha$.

$$
0<\alpha\leq1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\underline\alpha=0.
$$

For $k=(k_1,\ldots,k_d)$, with nonnegative integer coordinates, denote

$$
k_\bullet=\sum_{m=1}^d k_m.
$$

Define the operators

$$
D^k=\frac{\partial^{k_\bullet}}
{\partial x_1^{k_1}\partial x_2^{k_2}\cdots\partial x_d^{k_d}}.
$$

For a function $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$, $\mathcal X\subset\mathbb R^d$, define

$$
\|f\|_\alpha
:=\max_{k:k_\bullet\leq\underline\alpha}\sup_{x\in\mathcal X^\circ}|D^kf(x)|
+\max_{k:k_\bullet=\underline\alpha}
\sup_{\substack{x,y\in\mathcal X^\circ\\x\ne y}}
\frac{|D^kf(x)-D^kf(y)|}{\|x-y\|^{\alpha-\underline\alpha}}.
$$

Here $\mathcal X^\circ$ is the interior of $\mathcal X$.

*Correction: My notes put $k_\bullet\leq\underline\alpha$ in the second maximum. The textbook definition applies that Hölder quotient to the highest derivatives, $k_\bullet=\underline\alpha$, and takes both suprema over interior points (§2.7.1, p. 154). This distinction matters on general domains.*

Let $C_M^\alpha(\mathcal X)$ be the set of continuous functions $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$ with

$$
\|f\|_\alpha\leq M.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor emphasized that $\underline\alpha$ is not the ordinary integer part when $\alpha$ is an integer. It is always strictly smaller than $\alpha$, so at an integer it equals that integer minus one. This convention leaves a positive Hölder exponent for the highest derivative, including exponent one in the integer case.

The interior restriction avoids taking derivatives at boundary points where an entire small ball need not lie inside the domain. The professor described the class as having bounded derivatives through the indicated order, with a Lipschitz-type condition on the last derivative. The class becomes smoother as $\alpha$ increases.

These are familiar classes in nonparametric statistics. The professor connected them with density and regression estimation, where one studies optimal convergence rates over classes defined by such smoothness bounds.

# 2. The Uniform Covering Bound - van der Vaart & Wellner, Theorem 2.7.1, p. 155

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal X$ be a bounded, convex subset of $\mathbb R^d$ with nonempty interior. There is a constant $K$, depending on $\alpha$ and $d$ only, such that for every $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
\log N\bigl(\varepsilon,C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X),\|\cdot\|_\infty\bigr)
\leq K\lambda(\mathcal X^1)
\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{d/\alpha},
$$

where $\lambda(\mathcal X^1)$ is the Lebesgue measure of

$$
\mathcal X^1=\{x:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal X)<1\}.
$$

The diagram enlarges the convex set by one unit.

**Proof notation.**

$$
a\lesssim b\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\leq Cb,
$$

where $C$ depends on $\alpha$ and $d$ only. Write $\beta=\underline\alpha$.

If $f\in C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X)$, then $f$ is continuous. Without loss of generality, take $\mathcal X$ open, so Taylor expansions work everywhere in $\mathcal X$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor checked that the theorem is first a covering-number result; the bracketing result comes afterward. The exponent $d/\alpha$ was emphasized because its size relative to the relevant threshold will matter when the entropy integral is evaluated.

The set $\mathcal X^1$ is the original domain enlarged by a layer of thickness one. Its volume enters through a geometric bound on the number of small balls needed to cover the domain. The geometric claim was not proved in class.

Unlike the preceding VC arguments, this proof uses classical real analysis. Both the domain and the derivatives are bounded, suggesting that one can discretize their values and then count the resulting possibilities. The notation $\lesssim$ suppresses constants depending only on the specified structural parameters, not on the particular function or on the discretization scale. Continuity allows the proof to work in the interior and avoid separate Taylor expansions at boundary points.

# 3. Discretized Derivatives and Taylor Approximation - van der Vaart & Wellner, proof of Theorem 2.7.1, pp. 155-156

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Fix $\delta=\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}\leq1$. Form a $\delta$-net $x_1,\ldots,x_m\in\mathcal X$.

**Claim.**

$$
m\lesssim\lambda(\mathcal X^1)\delta^{-d}.
$$

This geometric covering argument is not proved here.

For every $k=(k_1,\ldots,k_d)$ with $k_\bullet\leq\beta$, and every $f\in C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X)$, introduce

$$
A_kf=\left(
\left\lfloor\frac{D^kf(x_1)}{\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}}\right\rfloor,
\ldots,
\left\lfloor\frac{D^kf(x_m)}{\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}}\right\rfloor
\right).
$$

The vector $\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}A_kf$ consists of the values $D^kf(x_i)$ discretized on a grid of mesh size $\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}$.

If $A_kf=A_kg$ for every $k$ with $k_\bullet\leq\beta$, choose $i$ such that $\|x-x_i\|\leq\delta$. Taylor expansion gives

$$
(f-g)(x)=\sum_{k_\bullet\leq\beta}
D^k(f-g)(x_i)\frac{(x-x_i)^k}{k!}+R,
$$

$$
|R|\lesssim\|x-x_i\|^\alpha\leq\delta^\alpha.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|(f-g)(x)|
&\lesssim\sum_{k_\bullet\leq\beta}
\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}\frac{\delta^{k_\bullet}}{k!}
+\delta^\alpha\\
&\leq\delta^\alpha(1+e^d)\lesssim\delta^\alpha=\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Correction: My notes end with a literal inequality $\delta^\alpha(1+e^d)<\delta^\alpha=\varepsilon$. The fixed factor cannot be dropped in a literal inequality. The conclusion is $\|f-g\|_\infty\lesssim\varepsilon$, so the ensuing covering radius is $C\varepsilon$ for a constant $C$ (Theorem 2.7.1 proof, pp. 155-156).*

Thus

$$
N\bigl(C\varepsilon,C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X),\|\cdot\|_\infty\bigr)
$$

is bounded by the number of different matrices $A_f$ whose rows are $A_kf$, $k_\bullet\leq\beta$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The construction discretizes two things: the locations in the domain and the values of the derivatives at those locations. Each derivative is rounded on a grid with a mesh appropriate to its order. The professor described this as dividing a bounded collection of possible values into small pieces and then counting the pieces.

The reason this coding supplies a cover is that two functions with the same discretized derivative data must be uniformly close. Every point of the domain is near a net point, so Taylor expansion transfers the agreement at the net points to the rest of the domain. The professor explained the multi-index notation: $(x-x_i)^k$ is the product of the coordinate powers, and $k!$ is the product of the coordinate factorials.

The exact representation of the Taylor remainder was not essential to the argument presented. Its order, controlled by the smoothness condition, is what allows the remainder and all the rounded derivative terms to be bounded at the same $\delta^\alpha$ scale. The professor repeatedly stressed that these comparisons are only up to constants.

# 4. Counting the Derivative Matrices - van der Vaart & Wellner, proof of Theorem 2.7.1, p. 156

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Since $f\in C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X)$,

$$
|D^kf(x_i)|\leq1\qquad\text{for every }i.
$$

There are at most $(1+\beta)^d$ multi-indices $k$ with $k_\bullet\leq\beta$. The number of possible values for one entry of $A_kf$ is at most

$$
\frac2{\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}}+2\leq2+2\delta^{-\alpha}.
$$

For each $x_i$, there are at most

$$
(2+2\delta^{-\alpha})^{(\beta+1)^d}
$$

possible columns of $A_f$. “We just discretize the derivatives.”

Without loss of generality, choose and order $x_1,\ldots,x_m$ so that, for every $j>1$, there is $i<j$ with

$$
\|x_i-x_j\|<2\delta.
$$

Then

$$
D^kf(x_j)=\sum_{k_\bullet+\ell_\bullet\leq\beta}
D^{k+\ell}f(x_i)\frac{(x_j-x_i)^\ell}{\ell!}+R,
$$

where

$$
|R|\lesssim\|x_j-x_i\|^{\alpha-k_\bullet}.
$$

Writing $A_{k+\ell}f(x_i)$ for the entry corresponding to $x_i$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|D^kf(x_j)-\sum_{k_\bullet+\ell_\bullet\leq\beta}
\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet-\ell_\bullet}
A_{k+\ell}f(x_i)\frac{(x_j-x_i)^\ell}{\ell!}\right|
&\lesssim\sum_{k_\bullet+\ell_\bullet\leq\beta}
\frac{\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet-\ell_\bullet}}{\ell!}(2\delta)^{\ell_\bullet}
+(2\delta)^{\alpha-k_\bullet}\\
&\lesssim\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Given the earlier column, $A_kf(x_j)$ ranges over integers in an interval of length proportional to

$$
\frac{\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}}{\delta^{\alpha-k_\bullet}}=1.
$$

Therefore the number of different $A_f$ is bounded by

$$
(2+2\delta^{-\alpha})^{(\beta+1)^d}C^{m-1}.
$$

Taking logarithms,

$$
\log N\bigl(C\varepsilon,C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X),\|\cdot\|_\infty\bigr)
\leq(m-1)\log C+(\beta+1)^d\log(2+2\delta^{-\alpha}).
$$

Here $m\lesssim\lambda(\mathcal X^1)\varepsilon^{-d/\alpha}$ and $\delta^{-\alpha}=1/\varepsilon$.

*Textbook clarification: The final displayed comparison in my notes is written with an informal “$\sim$.” The matrix count provides an upper bound, not an asymptotic equivalence. Bounding the logarithmic term by a constant times $\varepsilon^{-d/\alpha}$ and rescaling $\varepsilon$ finishes the stated theorem (p. 156).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The count of multi-indices is deliberately crude. Allowing every coordinate of $k$ to range independently from zero to $\beta$ overcounts the indices whose total is at most $\beta$, but the resulting bound is sufficient. Bounded derivatives likewise restrict every discretized entry to finitely many integer values.

Counting every column independently would be wasteful. The professor instead used an ordering of the net in which each new point has a previously chosen neighbor within distance $2\delta$. Taylor expansion around that neighbor restricts the possible derivative values at the new point. After dividing by the same mesh size used in discretization, the interval of possible integer values has bounded length independent of $\delta$.

Thus the first column receives the crude count, while each subsequent column contributes only a fixed factor. The professor summarized the construction as assembling the discretized derivatives into one large matrix, observing that each such matrix determines a small collection of uniformly close functions, and then using Taylor estimates to count how many matrices are possible. The proof's purpose was to show the mechanism rather than optimize its constants.

# 5. From Uniform Covers to Brackets - van der Vaart & Wellner, Corollary 2.7.2, p. 157

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Corollary.** Let $\mathcal X$ be a bounded, convex subset of $\mathbb R^d$ with nonempty interior. There is a constant $K$, depending on $d$, $\alpha$, and $\operatorname{diam}(\mathcal X)$, such that

$$
\log N_{[]}\bigl(\varepsilon,C_1^\alpha(\mathcal X),L_r(Q)\bigr)
\leq K\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{d/\alpha}
$$

for every $r\geq1$, $\varepsilon>0$, and probability measure $Q$ on $\mathbb R^d$.

*Correction: The exponent at the end of my handwritten corollary appears as $d/\varepsilon$. The complete recording says $d/\alpha$, agreeing with Corollary 2.7.2, p. 157, and the preceding covering theorem.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor ended with the bracketing consequence to connect the covering proof with the chapter's title. The bound holds for every underlying probability measure $Q$ and every $r\geq1$, making it a uniform tool rather than a result tied to one particular distribution.

The professor emphasized that when the resulting entropy condition yields the Donsker property, this uniformity allows a universal Donsker conclusion. The needed smoothness-versus-dimension calculation was left for the subsequent examples. The proof method here relies on discretization and Taylor expansion rather than on VC combinatorics, and the professor described these smoothness classes as classical objects already familiar from real analysis.
