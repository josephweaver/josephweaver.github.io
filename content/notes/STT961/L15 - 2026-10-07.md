# 1. Completing Total Boundedness - §2.5.1, Theorem 2.5.2, pp. 127-129

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall the uniform entropy condition

$$
\int_0^\infty\sup_Q\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q))}\,d\epsilon<\infty.
$$

We have proved

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
\qquad\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

It remains to show that $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded in $L_2(P)$. We showed

$$
\|(P_n-P)g^2\|_{\mathcal F_\infty}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0,
$$

where

$$
\mathcal F_\infty=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \|f-g\|_{P,2}<\infty\}.
$$

*Correction: The notes label the difference class itself as the Glivenko-Cantelli class at this step. The result used here is the Glivenko-Cantelli property of the **squared-difference class** $\mathcal F_\infty^2$ (Theorem 2.5.2 proof, pp. 128-129).*

Choose a sufficiently large $n$ and a realization with

$$
\|(P_n-P)g^2\|_{\mathcal F_\infty}<\epsilon^2.
$$

For $f,g\in\mathcal F$,

$$
P(f-g)^2=(P-P_n)(f-g)^2+P_n(f-g)^2.
$$

The first term is at most $\epsilon^2$. Thus an $\epsilon$-net with centers in $\mathcal F$ for $L_2(P_n)$ is a $\sqrt2\epsilon$-net for $L_2(P)$. The integral assumption gives a finite empirical cover, hence a finite population cover. Therefore $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded in $L_2(P)$.

*Textbook clarification: The proof selects a realization of a discrete measure for which the uniform error is small; it does not assert that convergence in probability gives the bound on every sample. If the original covering centers lie outside $\mathcal F$, take a finer cover and choose representatives in its nonempty cells, changing only a fixed radius factor. This suffices for total boundedness (p. 129; covering-center convention on p. 83).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The uniform law of large numbers established during the previous part of the proof transfers an empirical cover into a population cover. The radius becomes slightly larger, but the number of covering balls remains finite. This completes the missing total-boundedness requirement and hence the proof of the nonbracketing Donsker theorem.

# 2. The Classical Donsker Example - §2.5.1, Example 2.5.4, p. 129

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example.**

$$
\mathcal F=\{1_{(-\infty,t]}:t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

For every probability measure $Q$ and $0<\epsilon\le1$,

$$
N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(Q))
\le N_{[]}(\epsilon^2,\mathcal F,L_1(Q))
\le\frac K{\epsilon^2}.
$$

For indicators $f,g$,

$$
\int(f-g)^2\,dQ<\epsilon^2
\iff\int|f-g|\,dQ<\epsilon^2.
$$

The envelope is $F=1$, and

$$
\int_0^1\sqrt{\log(K/\epsilon^2)}\,d\epsilon
=\int_0^1\sqrt{\log K-2\log\epsilon}\,d\epsilon<\infty.
$$

With $u=\log K-2\log\epsilon$,

$$
d\epsilon=-\frac{\sqrt K}{2}e^{-u/2}\,du,
$$

so

$$
\int_0^1\sqrt{\log(K/\epsilon^2)}\,d\epsilon
=\frac{\sqrt K}{2}\int_{\log K}^\infty u^{1/2}e^{-u/2}\,du
\le C\Gamma(3/2)<\infty.
$$

Also $P^\star 1=1<\infty$.

*Correction: My notes give the change of variable only schematically and write an $e^{-u}$ factor with the unrescaled variable. The explicit substitution yields $e^{-u/2}$; a further rescaling gives the gamma integral. This verifies the entropy-integral finiteness used in Example 2.5.4, p. 129.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The covering rate is uniform over $Q$, and the envelope is constant. The indicator structure makes the remaining measurability conditions straightforward. Consequently the class is Donsker for every underlying distribution. In general, the challenging part of this theorem is controlling a supremum over the infinite collection of finitely discrete probability measures, not merely checking one empirical measure.

# 3. Weak L2 Size and the Bracketing Donsker Theorem - §2.5.2, Theorem 2.5.6, pp. 129-130

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Define

$$
\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}
:=\sup_{x>0}\bigl(x^2P(|f|>x)\bigr)^{1/2}.
$$

This is almost $L_2$, but not quite. It is not a norm: the triangle inequality fails. There is an equivalent norm, up to a constant.

**Donsker theorem with bracketing.** Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions such that

$$
\int_0^\infty\sqrt{\log N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_{2,\infty}(P))}\,d\epsilon
+\int_0^\infty\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))}\,d\epsilon
<\infty.
$$

Moreover, assume the envelope $F$ possesses a weak second moment:

$$
P^\star (F>x)=o(x^{-2})\qquad(x\to\infty).
$$

Then $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.

*Textbook clarification: The envelope condition is the little-$o$ tail condition, not merely finiteness of the weak-$L_2$ quantity (Theorem 2.5.6, p. 130, and Problem 2.5.6, p. 133).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The quantity resembles an $L_2$ norm but has only a triangle inequality up to a constant. The professor mentioned homework about its properties and comparison with ordinary norms. Fixed multiplicative constants are not an obstacle for the entropy arguments.

The bracketing theorem uses the population measure $P$, avoiding the supremum over $Q$ and the additional difference-class measurability conditions in the preceding theorem. Its envelope assumption is weaker than a finite ordinary second moment. On the other hand, the underlying measure is usually unknown and bracketing numbers may be harder to control. The two Donsker criteria are not generally ordered in strength; their usefulness depends on the class.

# 4. Bernstein's Inequality and the Chaining Plan - §2.5.2, Equation (2.5.5), p. 130

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Proof idea.** Use Bernstein's inequality. For square-integrable bounded $f$, a valid version is

$$
P(|\mathbb G_nf|>x)
\le2\exp\left\{-\frac{x^2/2}{Pf^2+\frac{1}{3}\|f\|_\infty x/\sqrt n}\right\}.
$$

For a finite class $\mathcal H$ with $|\mathcal H|\ge2$, the Bernstein maximal inequality yields

$$
E\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal H}
\lesssim
\max_{f\in\mathcal H}\frac{\|f\|_\infty}{\sqrt n}\log|\mathcal H|
+\max_{f\in\mathcal H}\|f\|_{P,2}\sqrt{\log|\mathcal H|}.
$$

Balance the two terms at the scale

$$
\|f\|_\infty\asymp
\frac{\sqrt n\,\|f\|_{P,2}}{\sqrt{\log|\mathcal H|}}.
$$

To deal with an infinite class, use a chaining argument.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor gave the plan before the detailed proof so that its purpose would not disappear among the constructions. Bernstein's maximal bound has two contributions. The truncation level should make them comparable. A finite class permits direct use of the bound, so an infinite class will be replaced by progressively finer finite approximations. Both balls and brackets enter that construction. Unlike the preceding proof, the argument will not proceed by first proving a Glivenko-Cantelli result for squared differences.

# 5. Nested Partitions and Finite Approximations - §2.5.2, Proof of Theorem 2.5.6, pp. 130-131

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

For every $q\in\mathbb N$, construct a partition

$$
\mathcal F=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_q}\mathcal F_{qi}
$$

into disjoint sets such that

$$
\sum_{q=1}^\infty 2^{-q}\sqrt{\log N_q}<\infty,
$$

$$
\left\|\left(\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{qi}}|f-g|\right)^\star\right\|_{P,2,\infty}
\le2^{-q},
\qquad
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{qi}}\|f-g\|_{P,2}\le2^{-q}.
$$

Why does this exist? Cover $\mathcal F$ with finitely many $L_2(P)$-balls and with $L_{2,\infty}(P)$-brackets at the corresponding dyadic scales. Intersect and make the resulting pieces disjoint. Do this in a nested way. The weighted entropy sum is controlled by the two finite entropy integrals.

*Textbook clarification: Fixed radius factors can be reduced to obtain the stated diameter bounds. Intersections alone need not be disjoint, so one also disjointifies. Making partitions successive refinements multiplies their cardinalities, while logarithms turn these products into sums; the dyadic weighted sum remains finite (pp. 130-131).*

Choose a fixed $f_{qi}\in\mathcal F_{qi}$, and set

$$
\pi_qf=f_{qi}\quad\text{if }f\in\mathcal F_{qi},
$$

$$
\Delta_qf=\left(\sup_{g,h\in\mathcal F_{qi}}|g-h|\right)^\star
\quad\text{if }f\in\mathcal F_{qi}.
$$

When $f$ runs through $\mathcal F$, both $\pi_qf$ and $\Delta_qf$ run through at most $N_q$ functions. The partition diagram groups each cell with its selected representative.

*Textbook clarification: $\Delta_qf$ is a measurable function of the sample-space point, obtained by taking a measurable majorant of the pointwise cell diameter. It is not a single numerical diameter. It is constant as a choice of function when the index $f$ varies within a partition cell (p. 131).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor described the construction rather than proving every cardinality estimate. Ball covers control the ordinary $L_2$ diameter and bracket covers control the pointwise oscillation in weak $L_2$. Refining at successive dyadic scales connects the partitions to the entropy integrals.

One arbitrary member is selected from each cell. This collapses the infinite index class to finitely many representative functions at each level. The number grows with the level, but its growth is controlled by the entropy sum. The transcript's informal reference to the diameter objects as “numbers” is read in conjunction with the written pointwise definition: their values depend on the underlying point.

# 6. Tightness and the Stopped-Chain Decomposition - §1.5, Theorem 1.5.6, p. 36; §2.5.2, p. 131

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall: weak convergence in $\ell^\infty(T)$ is established through convergence of finite-dimensional marginals and asymptotic tightness.

**Theorem 1.5.6.** A net $X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\ell^\infty(T)$ is asymptotically tight if $X_\alpha(t)$ is asymptotically tight in $\mathbb R$ for every $t$, and for every $\epsilon,\eta>0$ there is a finite partition $T=\bigcup_{i=1}^kT_i$ such that

$$
\limsup_\alpha P^\star\left(
\sup_i\sup_{s,t\in T_i}|X_\alpha(s)-X_\alpha(t)|>\epsilon
\right)<\eta.
$$

For the present process it is enough to show

$$
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star\left(\|\mathbb G_n(f-\pi_{q_0}f)\|_{\mathcal F}>\epsilon\right)=0
\qquad(\epsilon>0).
$$

Define

$$
a_q=\frac{2^{-q}}{\sqrt{\log N_{q+1}}}.
$$

For $q>q_0$, define the pointwise indicator functions

$$
A_{q-1}f=1\{\Delta_{q_0}f\le\sqrt n a_{q_0},\ldots,
\Delta_{q-1}f\le\sqrt n a_{q-1}\},
$$

$$
B_qf=1\{\Delta_{q_0}f\le\sqrt n a_{q_0},\ldots,
\Delta_{q-1}f\le\sqrt n a_{q-1},\Delta_qf>\sqrt n a_q\},
$$

and

$$
B_{q_0}f=1\{\Delta_{q_0}f>\sqrt n a_{q_0}\}.
$$

*Correction: The last threshold in the handwritten definition of $A_{q-1}f$ is indexed $a_q$. It must be $a_{q-1}$ to test the preceding level, consistently with $B_qf$ and the definition on p. 131.*

*Textbook clarification: Since the partitions are nested, $A_qf$ and $B_qf$ are constant in the index $f$ on a cell of level $q$, but still depend on the sample-space point. The sequence can be taken with $N_q\ge2$ so that the displayed thresholds are defined; a finite or singleton class is immediate. The limiting statement takes $n\to\infty$ first, followed by $q_0\to\infty$ (p. 131).*

Decompose pointwise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f-\pi_{q_0}f
&=(f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f\\
&\quad+\sum_{q=q_0+1}^\infty(f-\pi_qf)B_qf\\
&\quad+\sum_{q=q_0+1}^\infty(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f.
\end{aligned}
$$

Either there is a unique $q_1$ for which $B_{q_1}f=1$, or every $B_qf$ is zero and the surviving chain continues through the $A_qf$ terms.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The approximation criterion is an application of the earlier finite-partition characterization of tightness, with the function class now serving as the index set. The indicators connect the chain to the partition: $A$ records that all tests so far have passed, and $B$ records the first failure.

If a test fails, the decomposition consists of the earlier finite chain and the remaining approximation error at that stopping level. If no test fails, the first two terms vanish and the telescoping chain converges to the desired difference. The professor explained that the particular threshold $a_q$ will become motivated by the later estimates; one can think of starting with a generic threshold and choosing it so the bounds balance.

The next lecture would apply the empirical process to each of the three pieces and show that each is negligible. Those estimates were not yet proved in this lecture.
