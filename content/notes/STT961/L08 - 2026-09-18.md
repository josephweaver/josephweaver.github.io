# 1. Completing the Orlicz Maximal Inequality - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, Lemma 2.2.2, pp. 96-97

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### §2.2. Orlicz Norm

$$
\|X\|_\psi=\inf\left\{A>0:E\psi\left(\frac{|X|}{A}\right)\leq1\right\}.
$$

**Lemma.**

$$
\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|\right\|_\psi
\leq K\psi^{-1}(m)\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|X_i\|_\psi.
$$

*Textbook clarification: This is the continuation of the preceding lecture's lemma, with $\psi$ convex, nondecreasing, nonzero, $\psi(0)=0$, and $\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}\psi(x)\psi(y)/\psi(cxy)<\infty$ for some $c>0$. The abbreviated opening statement in my notes does not repeat all its hypotheses. The proof bounds the maximum of absolute values (Lemma 2.2.2, pp. 96-97).*

**Proof.** For $y\geq1$ and $A>0$,

$$
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Ay}\right)
\leq\sum_{i=1}^m\frac{\psi(c|X_i|/A)}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1),
$$

provided

$$
\psi(x)\psi(y)\leq\psi(cxy),\qquad x,y\geq1.
$$

Set

$$
A=c\max_{1\leq k\leq m}\|X_k\|_\psi.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Ay}\right)
&\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^m E\psi\left(|X_i|/\max_k\|X_k\|_\psi\right)}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1)\\
&\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^m E\psi\left(|X_i|/\|X_i\|_\psi\right)}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1)\\
&\leq\frac{m}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1).
\end{aligned}
$$

Also, by monotonicity,

$$
E\psi\left(\frac{\max_i|X_i|}{Ay}\right)
=E\max_i\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Ay}\right)
\leq\frac{m}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1).
$$

*Textbook clarification: This calculation concerns the nontrivial case $0<\max_i\|X_i\|_\psi<\infty$. Zero-norm terms are zero almost surely and can be omitted; an infinite right-hand side makes the desired inequality immediate. The textbook proof on p. 97 uses the same scaling.*

Assume $\psi(1)\leq\tfrac12$ and choose

$$
y=\psi^{-1}(2m).
$$

Then

$$
\frac{m}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1)\leq\frac12+\frac12=1.
$$

Hence

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_\psi
\leq Ay
=c\max_k\|X_k\|_\psi\,\psi^{-1}(2m).
$$

By convexity and $\psi(0)=0$,

$$
\psi^{-1}(2m)\leq2\psi^{-1}(m).
$$

This proves the desired inequality under the additional assumptions.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The proof resumes with the inequality obtained last time. Taking $A$ to be a constant times the largest individual norm makes each expectation in the sum at most one. The choice of $y$ then forces the entire expression to be at most one, which is exactly what is needed to produce an admissible scale in the Orlicz-norm definition.

The professor emphasized that the bound need not be attained at the best possible scale: exhibiting an admissible scale is enough for an upper bound. The remaining factor two is absorbed by the constant through convexity. However, two convenient assumptions were introduced during the argument and still have to be removed.

# 2. Removing the Extra Assumptions - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, proof of Lemma 2.2.2, p. 97

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

The original condition on $\psi$ was

$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(cxy)}<\infty
\quad\text{for some }c>0.
$$

If $\psi(1)>\tfrac12$, consider

$$
\phi(x)=\frac{\psi(x)}{2\psi(1)}.
$$

Then $\phi(1)=\tfrac12$.

More generally, there exist constants $0<\sigma\leq1$ and $\tau>0$ such that

$$
\phi(x)=\sigma\psi(\tau x)
$$

satisfies the strengthened conditions used in the proof, including

$$
\phi(x)\phi(y)\leq\phi(c'xy),\qquad x,y\geq1,
$$

for a suitable constant $c'>0$, and $\phi(1)\leq\tfrac12$.

*Correction: My notes write $\sigma\geq1$. The normalization in the textbook proof uses $0<\sigma\leq1$ (p. 97). This permits the downward rescaling needed to arrange the value at one. All the scaling constants depend on $\psi$ only.*

Apply the inequality to $\phi$ and return to the $\psi$-norm. The rescaling constants are absorbed into $K$.

*Textbook clarification: The norm comparison used in the textbook is $\|X\|_\psi\leq\|X\|_\phi/(\sigma\tau)\leq\|X\|_\psi/\sigma$ for this range of $\sigma$ (p. 97). The lecture does not give the detailed construction of $\sigma,\tau$.*

**Remark.**

$$
\psi^{-1}(m)\longrightarrow\infty\qquad(m\to\infty).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor explained that a rescaled function can satisfy the convenient conditions used in the proof. Proving the inequality for that function and comparing its norm with the original norm changes only the constant. The details of finding the rescaling were left as an exercise rather than worked out on the board.

Even with a slowly growing inverse, this finite-maximum inequality does not directly solve the problem of a supremum over infinitely many indices. The next step is to combine it with information about the size and geometry of the index set.

# 3. Covering, Packing, and Entropy - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, Definition 2.2.3, p. 98

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $(T,d)$ be an arbitrary semimetric space.

The **covering number** $N(\varepsilon,d)$ is the minimum number of balls of radius $\varepsilon$ needed to cover $T$.

A collection of points is called **$\varepsilon$-separated** if the distance between any pair of distinct elements is greater than $\varepsilon$.

The **packing number** $D(\varepsilon,d)$ is the maximum number of $\varepsilon$-separated points in $T$.

$$
\log N(\varepsilon,d),\qquad\log D(\varepsilon,d)
$$

are called entropy numbers of $T$.

[The notes draw a covering by overlapping balls and a separated-point packing of the same set.]

$$
N(\varepsilon,d)\leq D(\varepsilon,d)
\leq N(\varepsilon/2,d).
$$

**Remark.** If $T$ is totally bounded, then for each $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
N(\varepsilon,d),D(\varepsilon,d)<\infty.
$$

As $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, these numbers increase and may tend to infinity.

*Correction: My notes write $N,D\to\infty$ without qualification. They are nondecreasing as the radius decreases, but need not diverge, for example when $T$ has finitely many semimetric-distinguishable points. Definition 2.2.3 and the following discussion on p. 98 require finiteness at every positive radius, not divergence at zero.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Different sources may use covering entropy or packing entropy, so one should check the definition rather than rely on the word “entropy” alone. The inequalities make the two closely related for the purposes of these bounds.

Total boundedness gives finite numbers at a fixed resolution. Asymptotic equicontinuity requires examining smaller and smaller scales, so their growth as the radius decreases becomes important. The next inequality measures that growth by an integral.

# 4. The Maximal Inequality for Increments - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, Theorem 2.2.4 and Corollary 2.2.5, p. 98

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Theorem.** Let $\psi$ be convex, nondecreasing, and nonzero, with $\psi(0)=0$ and

$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(cxy)}<\infty
\quad\text{for some }c>0.
$$

Let $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ be a separable stochastic process with

$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_\psi\leq C d(s,t),\qquad s,t\in T,
$$

for some semimetric $d$ on $T$ and a constant $C$. Then for every $\eta>0$ and $\delta>0$,

$$
\left\|\sup_{d(s,t)\leq\delta}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
\leq K\left[
\int_0^\eta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+\delta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right],
$$

where $K$ depends only on $\psi$ and $C$.

*Correction: The displayed theorem on handwritten p. 4 lacks the square on the packing number in its second term. The square is required by Theorem 2.2.4, p. 98, and is consistent with the lecture's subsequent interval calculation. It counts possible pairs at the initial scale.*

*Textbook clarification: The separability assumption makes the relevant suprema agree almost surely with suprema over a suitable countable subset, as explained in the footnote on p. 98. It should not be dropped when moving from finite maxima to the process supremum.*

**Corollary.** The constant $K$ can be chosen so that

$$
\left\|\sup_{s,t\in T}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
\leq K\int_0^{\operatorname{diam}T}
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

*Textbook clarification: $\operatorname{diam}T=\sup_{s,t\in T}d(s,t)$, the largest pairwise distance, not an inscribed-ball radius. This is the diameter used in Corollary 2.2.5, p. 98, and its proof on p. 100.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The increment assumption links the distributional behavior of the process to a semimetric on its index set. That semimetric need not be one originally supplied with the set. The resulting constant depends only on $\psi$ and the increment constant $C$, while the geometry enters through the packing numbers.

The professor said the proof would be important because it introduces the **chaining method**, a technique useful beyond this one result. Rather than start a long proof with little class time left, the lecture next considered a simple example to see the form of the bound.

# 5. An Interval Example - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, application of Definition 2.2.3 and Theorem 2.2.4, p. 98

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let

$$
T=[0,L]\subset\mathbb R,\qquad d(s,t)=|s-t|.
$$

A ball of radius $\varepsilon$ is an interval of length $2\varepsilon$ before intersecting with $T$. The notes estimate

$$
N(\varepsilon,d)\approx\frac{L}{2\varepsilon},
\qquad D(\varepsilon,d)\approx\frac{L}{\varepsilon}.
$$

*Correction: My notes use equal signs for these counts. Counts are integers and endpoint/strict-separation conventions matter, so the displayed ratios describe the small-radius scale, not exact formulas. This is checked directly from Definition 2.2.3, p. 98. For example, when $0<\varepsilon<L$, the packing number for strict separation is $\lceil L/\varepsilon\rceil$, not generally $L/\varepsilon$.*

Thus, for fixed $L>0$, both entropy numbers have leading growth of order

$$
\log(1/\varepsilon)
\qquad(\varepsilon\downarrow0).
$$

For

$$
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,
\qquad\psi_p^{-1}(m)=\{\log(1+m)\}^{1/p},
$$

the lecture's small-scale form of the two terms is

$$
\int_0^\eta\left\{\log\left(1+\frac L\varepsilon\right)\right\}^{1/p}\,d\varepsilon
+\delta\left\{\log\left(1+\frac{L^2}{\eta^2}\right)\right\}^{1/p}.
$$

*Textbook clarification: This is a schematic evaluation of the theorem using the interval counts' small-scale behavior, up to fixed constants, not an exact equality for its right-hand side. The exact theorem still contains $D(\varepsilon,d)$ and $D(\eta,d)^2$. This qualification follows from the counting correction immediately above and Theorem 2.2.4, p. 98.*

Take $p=1$ and integrate by parts:

$$
\int_0^\eta\log\left(1+\frac L\varepsilon\right)\,d\varepsilon
=\eta\log\left(1+\frac L\eta\right)
+L\log(\eta+L)-L\log L.
$$

The corresponding schematic bound is therefore

$$
\eta\log\left(1+\frac L\eta\right)
+L\log(\eta+L)-L\log L
+\delta\log\left(1+\frac{L^2}{\eta^2}\right).
$$

The theorem holds for every $\eta>0$ and $\delta>0$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The example is intended to show roughly what the abstract integral produces on a familiar index set. The professor explicitly cautioned that constants might be missing in the board calculation. The logarithmic terms give comparatively slow growth, and having both $\eta$ and $\delta$ available is useful when choosing scales that tend to zero in later equicontinuity arguments.
