# 1. Counting Traces and Sauer's Lemma - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.1, Lemma 2.6.2 and Corollary 2.6.3, pp. 135-136

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.6. VC Classes

$$
V(\mathcal C)=\inf\left\{n:\max_{x_1,\ldots,x_n}\Delta_n(\mathcal C,x_1,\ldots,x_n)<2^n\right\}
$$

is the VC index.

**Goal:** Show that covering numbers of VC classes grow polynomially in $1/\varepsilon$ as $\varepsilon\to0$.

*Textbook clarification: The notes say “entropy numbers” in this goal. Theorem 2.6.4 bounds $N$, the covering number, polynomially. Thus its logarithm, the entropy, grows at most logarithmically in $1/\varepsilon$. The distinction matters when the later VC-hull result bounds $\log N$ instead (§2.6.1, p. 135; §2.6.3, p. 142).*

**Lemma.** Let $\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ be arbitrary points. The total number of subsets $\Delta_n(\mathcal C,x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ picked out by $\mathcal C$ is bounded above by the number of subsets of $\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}$ shattered by $\mathcal C$.

**Corollary (Sauer's lemma).** For a VC class of sets $\mathcal C$,

$$
\max_{x_1,\ldots,x_n}\Delta_n(\mathcal C,x_1,\ldots,x_n)
\leq\sum_{j=0}^{V(\mathcal C)-1}\binom nj
=O\bigl(n^{V(\mathcal C)-1}\bigr)
\qquad(n\to\infty).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor began by recalling that the VC index records the first size at which no configuration can have all its subsets picked out. The purpose of the combinatorial definition is to make the entropy conditions in the Donsker theorems easier to verify. Directly finding covering numbers can be difficult, whereas checking a VC property is often more manageable.

The counting lemma and Sauer's lemma were stated without proof. The professor noted that their proofs use specialized combinatorial arguments that are useful in statistical learning but would not recur enough in this course to justify developing them here. The role of these facts in this lecture is to turn finite-set complexity into a polynomial bound.

# 2. Covering Numbers of VC Classes of Sets - van der Vaart & Wellner, Theorem 2.6.4, p. 136

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Theorem.** There is a universal constant $K$ such that, for every VC class of sets $\mathcal C$, every probability measure $Q$, every $r\geq1$, and $0<\varepsilon<1$,

$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal C,L_r(Q))
\leq K V(\mathcal C)(4e)^{V(\mathcal C)}
\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{r(V(\mathcal C)-1)}.
$$

**Example 4.** For $S=\mathbb R^d$ and $\mathcal H$ the class of all half-spaces,

$$
V(\mathcal H)=d+2,
$$

so

$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_r(Q))
\leq K(d+2)(4e)^{d+2}
\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{r(d+1)}.
$$

$$
\mathcal F=\{I_H:H\in\mathcal H\}.
$$

“Too simple.”

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor emphasized that $K$ is genuinely universal: it does not depend on the underlying space, the probability measure, or the particular VC class. The $e$ in $4e$ is the base of the exponential function. The theorem makes the dependence on the VC index and on $r$ explicit.

For half-spaces, the bound describes how many $L_r(Q)$ balls are sufficient to cover the entire class. The professor remarked that obtaining such a bound directly by a geometric construction would be much harder. A more specialized argument might improve constants, but the general theorem already provides the useful rate.

Passing from sets to their indicator functions is a legitimate way to obtain a function class, but the professor called this construction too simple for the more general statistical models of interest. This motivated looking for a richer correspondence between functions and sets.

# 3. VC-Subgraph Classes and Their Covering Numbers - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.2, Theorem 2.6.7, p. 141

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Definition.** The subgraph of a function $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$ is a subset of $\mathcal X\times\mathbb R$ given by

$$
\{(x,t):t<f(x)\}.
$$

**Definition.** A collection $\mathcal F$ of measurable functions is called a VC-subgraph class if the collection of all subgraphs of $f\in\mathcal F$ is a VC class of sets in $\mathcal X\times\mathbb R$. If this subgraph class is $\mathcal C$,

$$
V(\mathcal F):=V(\mathcal C).
$$

**Theorem.** For a VC-subgraph class of functions $\mathcal F$ with measurable envelope function $F$ and $r\geq1$, for any probability measure $Q$ with $\|F\|_{L_r(Q)}>0$,

$$
N\bigl(\varepsilon\|F\|_{L_r(Q)},\mathcal F,L_r(Q)\bigr)
\leq K V(\mathcal F)(16e)^{V(\mathcal F)}
\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{r(V(\mathcal F)-1)},
\qquad0<\varepsilon<1,
$$

where $K$ is a universal constant.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Instead of beginning with sets and taking indicators, the professor began with functions and associated a set with each function. A subgraph lives in $\mathcal X\times\mathbb R$, even when $\mathcal X$ is not the real line or an ordinary Euclidean domain. The familiar picture of the region below a graph is therefore only a visualization of the general definition.

The index of the function class is inherited from this collection of subgraphs. The covering theorem has the same general form as the one for sets, but the covering radius is scaled by the envelope norm, and the numerical factor changes from $4e$ to $16e$. The professor mentioned that sharper constants have been studied, but did not pursue those refinements or prove the theorem in class.

# 4. The Donsker Property and the Entropy Integral - van der Vaart & Wellner, Theorems 2.5.2 and 2.6.8, pp. 127 and 142

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal F$ be a pointwise separable, $P$-pre-Gaussian VC-subgraph class with envelope $F$ satisfying

$$
P^\star (F>x)=o(x^{-2})\qquad(x\to\infty).
$$

Then $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.

**Remark in proof.** The key question is

$$
\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N}\,d\varepsilon<\infty.
$$

From the preceding polynomial bound, with $V=V(\mathcal F)$, the relevant integral is bounded by a constant multiple of

$$
\int_0^1\sqrt{C-r(V-1)\log\varepsilon}\,d\varepsilon.
$$

Put $u=C-r(V-1)\log\varepsilon$. Then

$$
\varepsilon=e^{(C-u)/(r(V-1))},\qquad
 d\varepsilon=-\frac1{r(V-1)}e^{(C-u)/(r(V-1))}\,du.
$$

After this change of variable and a rescaling,

$$
\int_C^\infty\sqrt u\,e^{-(u-C)/(r(V-1))}\,du
\leq\widetilde C\int_0^\infty\sqrt v\,e^{-v}\,dv
=\widetilde C\,\Gamma\left(\frac32\right)<\infty.
$$

*Textbook clarification: This entropy calculation gives the direct application of Theorem 2.5.2 under a square-integrable envelope. Theorem 2.6.8, p. 142, is the stronger result with a weak second-moment tail condition and a separate pre-Gaussian assumption; the textbook does not derive that refinement from the entropy calculation alone. When $V=1$, the entropy bound is constant and the change of variable dividing by $V-1$ is unnecessary.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor singled out finiteness of the entropy integral as the main calculation to inspect. Taking the logarithm of a polynomial covering bound leaves a constant and a multiple of $-\log\varepsilon$. The change of variable converts the singular-looking integral near $\varepsilon=0$ into an exponentially decaying integral over an unbounded interval, which is controlled by the gamma integral.

The conceptual point was that a function class may look complicated when one tries to construct its covers directly, yet still be small enough for the entropy integral. The VC index controls that complexity. The professor used this calculation to explain why the earlier Donsker machinery applies, subject to the accompanying assumptions in the stated theorem.

# 5. Convex Hulls and VC-Hull Classes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.3, Theorem 2.6.9, pp. 142-144

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Definition.** The symmetric convex hull of a class $\mathcal F$, denoted by $\operatorname{sconv}(\mathcal F)$, is

$$
\left\{\sum_{i=1}^m a_i f_i:
\sum_{i=1}^m|a_i|\leq1,\ f_i\in\mathcal F,\ m\geq1\right\}.
$$

**Definition.** A set $\mathcal F$ of measurable functions is called a VC-hull class if there is a VC-subgraph class of functions $\mathcal G$ such that every $f\in\mathcal F$ is the pointwise limit of a sequence

$$
f_m\in\operatorname{sconv}(\mathcal G).
$$

**Theorem.** Let $Q$ be a probability measure on $(\mathcal X,\mathcal A)$. Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions with measurable envelope $F$ such that $QF^2<\infty$ and

$$
N\bigl(\varepsilon\|F\|_{L_2(Q)},\mathcal F,L_2(Q)\bigr)
\leq C\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^V,
\qquad0<\varepsilon<1.
$$

There exists a constant $K$, depending only on $C$ and $V$, such that

$$
\log N\bigl(\varepsilon\|F\|_{L_2(Q)},
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}\mathcal F,L_2(Q)\bigr)
\leq K\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{2V/(V+2)}.
$$

The closure contains pointwise limits.

*Textbook clarification: Theorem 2.6.9 is stated for $\operatorname{conv}\mathcal F$. Its proof and the observation on p. 144 also cover the symmetric convex hull. With $QF^2<\infty$, pointwise limits dominated by $F$ are $L_2(Q)$ limits, so passing to the pointwise sequential closure does not change the asserted entropy order. The bar in the notes is interpreted in this sense.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The symmetric convex hull permits finite weighted sums with either sign, with the sum of the absolute weights bounded by one. There is no fixed upper bound on the number of terms. Passing to a VC-hull class also permits pointwise limits of such sums.

The professor compared this construction with building integration theory from indicators, then step functions, then limits. Here the starting pieces come from a VC-subgraph class rather than only from an elementary collection of indicators. This creates much larger function classes while retaining useful control over complexity.

The convex-hull theorem was stated for a generic class satisfying a polynomial covering bound, not only for a VC class. VC-subgraph classes supply an important instance of its hypothesis. The professor explicitly explained the bar over the hull as allowing limiting functions, and did not present the proof of the entropy theorem.

# 6. Entropy of VC-Hull Classes - van der Vaart & Wellner, Corollary 2.6.12, p. 145

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Corollary.** For any VC-hull class $\mathcal F$ and probability measure $Q$,

$$
\log N\bigl(\varepsilon\|F\|_{L_2(Q)},\mathcal F,L_2(Q)\bigr)
\leq K\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{2-2/V_m(\mathcal F)},
$$

where $K$ depends only on $V_m(\mathcal F)$.

*Textbook clarification: The notes use $V(\mathcal F)$ in this bound. Corollary 2.6.12 uses $V_m(\mathcal F)$, the VC index of the generating VC-subgraph class, not a claim that the hull itself is VC-subgraph. Here $F$ is the inherited envelope; the finite-envelope-norm case is the useful entropy bound.*

Writing $V=V_m(\mathcal F)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N}\,d\varepsilon
&\leq\sqrt K\int_0^1\varepsilon^{-1+1/V}\,d\varepsilon\\
&=\sqrt K\left.\frac{\varepsilon^{1/V}}{1/V}\right|_0^1
=\sqrt K\,V<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor emphasized the change in what is being bounded: after taking a hull, it is the logarithm of the covering number that has a polynomial bound. The exponent remains strictly below two, which is enough for the square-root entropy integral to converge at zero.

For a fixed VC-hull class, the displayed integral is finite. The professor cautioned that this does not by itself give satisfactory control when the generating VC index is allowed to increase. The constant $K$ also depends on that index, and its dependence may be complicated. In applications using increasingly rich classes as the sample size grows, one has to track both this constant and the explicit index dependence. The professor mentioned slowly growing choices, such as logarithmic growth, as a motivation for studying more precise bounds, not as a general theorem established in this lecture.

# 7. VC-Major Classes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.6.4, Lemma 2.6.13 and Theorem 2.6.14, pp. 145-146

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Alternative definition.** A class $\mathcal F$ of measurable functions is called a VC-major class if the sets

$$
\bigl\{\{x:f(x)>t\}:f\in\mathcal F,\ t\in\mathbb R\bigr\}
$$

form a VC class of sets.

**Lemma.** A bounded VC-major class is a scalar multiple of a VC-hull class for sets.

**Idea of proof.** For a function $f:\mathcal X\to[0,1]$, define

$$
f_m(x)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m I\left\{f(x)>\frac im\right\}.
$$

Then

$$
\mathcal F\subseteq
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}
\bigl\{I_{\{f>t\}}:f\in\mathcal F,\ t\in\mathbb R\bigr\},
$$

where the closure is pointwise sequential closure.

*Correction: My notes displayed an equality with the closed convex hull and used $I_{\{f<t\}}$, although the preceding approximation uses $\{f>i/m\}$. The argument proves containment, and the generating level sets have the same “$>$” direction as the approximation. Corrected against Lemma 2.6.13, p. 145.*

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal F$ be a pointwise separable VC-major class with measurable envelope $F$ such that

$$
\int_0^\infty\sqrt{P^\star (F>x)}\,dx<\infty.
$$

Then $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.

VC-subgraph, VC-major, VC-hull $\longrightarrow$ Donsker, under the stated conditions. Bypass a direct weak-convergence argument.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor contrasted the two constructions. For VC-subgraphs, the threshold $t$ is an extra coordinate in the set space. For VC-major classes, $t$ is a parameter indexing level sets in the original space $\mathcal X$. The latter viewpoint resembles a family with a function-valued component and a scalar level parameter. The professor described these as two approaches that developed in parallel and noted that VC-subgraph terminology is encountered more often, although VC-major classes also appear.

The approximation by level-set indicators explains why a bounded VC-major class is contained in a suitable VC hull. The professor stressed the boundedness used in placing the function values in a fixed interval. The displayed averages are step-function approximations; taking their limits recovers the functions under consideration. The claim needed is the containment recorded above, rather than equality of the original class with every function in the hull.

The professor closed by emphasizing how to use these results in a statistical argument. Before proving a functional central limit theorem directly, inspect the structure of the underlying class. It may already be VC-subgraph, VC-major, or a suitable VC hull, or the model assumptions may be chosen so that one of these results applies. Under the required conditions, the Donsker property then supplies uniform central-limit behavior that can be used to analyze functionals of the model class. The next lecture would turn from these covering-number results to bracketing numbers in Chapter 2.7.
