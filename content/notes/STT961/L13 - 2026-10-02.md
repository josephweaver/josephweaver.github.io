# 1. P-Measurable Classes - §2.3.1, Definition 2.3.3, pp. 109-110

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Definition.** A class $\mathcal F$ of measurable functions $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$ on a probability space $(\mathcal X,\mathcal A,P)$ is called a $P$-measurable class if

$$
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\longmapsto
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^ne_if(x_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
$$

is measurable on the completion of $(\mathcal X^n,\mathcal A^n,P^n)$ for every $n\ge1$ and every $(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\in\mathbb R^n$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The norm notation means a supremum over the class. The requirement concerns weighted sums because the proof will introduce Rademacher weights. It is a measurability condition on the whole supremum, not just on each individual function. The professor said that such conditions would usually be assumed when needed rather than repeatedly interrupting the arguments to discuss them.

# 2. Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem Without Bracketing - §2.4, Theorem 2.4.3, p. 123

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $\mathcal F$ be a $P$-measurable class of measurable functions with envelope $F$ such that $P^\star F<\infty$. Let

$$
\mathcal F_M=\{f1\{F\le M\}:f\in\mathcal F\}.
$$

If

$$
\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))=o_{P^\star }(n)
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0,\ M>0,
$$

then

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}^\star\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely and in mean}.
$$

In particular, $\mathcal F$ is a Glivenko-Cantelli class.

*Textbook clarification: The entropy condition means $n^{-1}\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))\to0$ in outer probability. Unlike the bracketing theorem, the norm here uses the empirical measure $P_n$, so the covering number is random (Theorem 2.4.3, p. 123).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Every function is truncated using the same envelope event, rather than a separate threshold on each function. The professor contrasted the empirical $L_1(P_n)$ norm with the $L_1(P)$ norm in the bracketing result. The former uses the sample, but requires a convergence statement about random entropy instead of mere finiteness. The lecture would prove convergence in mean and refer to a separate result for the almost-sure assertion.

# 3. Proof by Truncation and Conditional Symmetrization - §2.4, Theorem 2.4.3 and Lemma 2.4.5, pp. 123-125

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

By the first symmetrization lemma and measurability,

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\le2E_XE_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Multiply each summand by

$$
1\{F(X_i)\le M\}+1\{F(X_i)>M\}=1.
$$

Split and use the triangle inequality:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
&\le2E_X^\star E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_M}\\
&\quad+2E_X^\star E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)1\{F(X_i)>M\}\right\|_{\mathcal F}\\
&\le2E_X^\star E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_M}
+2P^\star [F1\{F>M\}].
\end{aligned}
$$

The last term tends to zero as $M\to\infty$.

Fix $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$. Consider an $\epsilon$-net $\mathcal G$ in $L_1(P_n)$ covering $\mathcal F_M$. Choose

$$
|\mathcal G|=N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n)).
$$

Then

$$
E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_M}
\le E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal G}+\epsilon.
$$

By the maximal inequality for $\psi_2$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_M}
&\le K\sqrt{1+\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))}\\
&\qquad\times\sup_{g\in\mathcal G}
\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\psi_2\mid X}
+\epsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

Recall the Rademacher process $Y_a=\sum_i\epsilon_ia_i$. Hoeffding's inequality gives

$$
\|Y_a-Y_b\|_{\psi_2}\le\sqrt6\,\|a-b\|_2.
$$

Here

$$
Y_g=\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_i\frac{g(X_i)}n,
$$

so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|Y_g\|_{\psi_2\mid X}
&\le\sqrt6\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{g^2(X_i)}{n^2}}\\
&=\frac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt n}\sqrt{P_ng^2}
\le\frac{\sqrt6}{\sqrt n}M.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Correction: My notes write an equality for the general Rademacher increment Orlicz norm; Hoeffding gives an upper bound, not equality (Lemma 2.2.7, pp. 100-101). The notes also identify minimal covering centers with a subset of $\mathcal F_M$. Covering centers need not lie in the class (Definition 2.1.5, p. 83). They may instead be clipped to $[-M,M]$, which does not increase their distance from any $g\in\mathcal F_M$ and supplies the displayed bound by $M$. This retains the same covering cardinality and the proof of Theorem 2.4.3, pp. 123-124.*

Consequently,

$$
H_{n,M}:=E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_ig(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_M}
\le KM\sqrt{\frac6n}\sqrt{1+\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))}+\epsilon.
$$

The first term on the right is $o_{P^\star }(1)$ for fixed $M$ and $\epsilon$. As $\epsilon>0$ is arbitrary,

$$
H_{n,M}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Also $0\le H_{n,M}\le M$, so its outer expectation tends to zero. Hence

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\le2P^\star [F1\{F>M\}].
$$

Letting $M\to\infty$ proves convergence in mean. Lemma 2.4.5 gives almost-sure convergence.

*Correction: The last handwritten lines switch from the conditional Rademacher supremum to $E_\epsilon\|P_n-P\|$ and write convergence to $\epsilon$. The conditional quantity is $H_{n,M}$; the bound gives an upper limit at most $\epsilon$ for every $\epsilon>0$, hence convergence to zero. The final integration uses the deterministic bound $M$. This is the limiting argument in Theorem 2.4.3, p. 124.*

*Textbook clarification: Lemma 2.4.5 identifies appropriate measurable versions as a reverse **submartingale**, converging almost surely to a constant. The lecture refers to the result rather than proving it (pp. 124-125).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The truncation separates the bounded part from an envelope tail whose expectation becomes small. After fixing the sample, the only randomness in the conditional Orlicz norm comes from the Rademacher variables. The coordinates $g(X_i)/n$ are then deterministic coefficients, making the earlier Rademacher inequality directly applicable.

The finite net replaces the original supremum at a cost of $\epsilon$: add and subtract a nearby center, then use $|\epsilon_i|=1$ to bound the difference by its empirical $L_1$ distance. The entropy condition makes the finite-net bound small. Boundedness permits the subsequent integration over the sample.

In response to a question, the professor emphasized the order of limits: **first let $n\to\infty$ with $M$ fixed, then let $M\to\infty$**. The almost-sure argument uses a reverse-martingale-type result that would not otherwise be needed in the course, so its proof was omitted; interested students were directed to Lemma 2.4.5.

# 4. The Empirical Distribution Example - §2.4, Application of Theorem 2.4.3, pp. 123-124

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example.** The classical Glivenko-Cantelli case:

$$
\mathcal F=\{1_{(-\infty,c]}:c\in\mathbb R\},\qquad F(x)=1\quad(x\in\mathbb R).
$$

Then

$$
P^\star F=\int_{\mathbb R}1\,dP=1<\infty,
$$

and

$$
\mathcal F_M=\begin{cases}
\{0\},&0<M<1,\\
\mathcal F,&M\ge1.
\end{cases}
$$

Consider the order statistics

$$
-\infty=X_{(0)}<X_{(1)}\le\cdots\le X_{(n)}<X_{(n+1)}=\infty.
$$

For every $c\in\mathbb R$, choose the last observed value not exceeding $c$, or the zero function if there is none. The corresponding indicator $g_c$ agrees with $1_{(-\infty,c]}$ at all sample points:

$$
\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n
\left|1_{(-\infty,c]}(X_j)-g_c(X_j)\right|=0<\epsilon.
$$

Therefore

$$
N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P_n))\le n+2,
\qquad
\frac{\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P_n))}{n}
\le\frac{\log(n+2)}n\longrightarrow0.
$$

*Correction: My notes use $X_{(i)}<c\le X_{(i+1)}$ and the indicator at $X_{(i+1)}$ to claim zero empirical distance. That upper indicator may include an extra observation. Choosing the last observed value at or below $c$ gives the required agreement, including ties. In fact at most $n+1$ distinct sample patterns occur, so the written bound $n+2$ is valid but not sharp. This verifies the covering-number condition of Theorem 2.4.3 (p. 123).*

*Textbook clarification: The class is $P$-measurable: indicators with rational cutpoints form a countable pointwise approximation class, as in Example 2.3.4, p. 110. No density assumption on $P$ is required.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

It is unnecessary to calculate the smallest possible cover. Any cover with at most $n+2$ members is enough because its logarithm divided by $n$ tends to zero. The constant envelope makes the integrability and truncation conditions immediate. This recovers the same classical result as the bracketing proof, although bracketing looked more natural for this particular example.

# 5. Comparison of the Criteria and the Donsker Program - §§2.4-2.5, pp. 122-127

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Glivenko-Cantelli criteria:

With bracketing,

$$
N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P))<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0.
$$

Without bracketing,

$$
\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))=o_{P^\star }(n)
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon,M>0,
\qquad P^\star F<\infty,
$$

with the stated measurability condition. Some classes are more natural for one approach than the other.

*Textbook clarification: The crossed comparison in my notes is not an equivalence theorem between these hypotheses. They are the distinct sufficient criteria of Theorems 2.4.1 and 2.4.3 (pp. 122-123); the latter also includes its measurability and integrability assumptions.*

Next: Chapter 2.5, Donsker theorems. Recall

$$
\mathcal F\text{ is }P\text{-Donsker}
\iff
\mathbb G_n=\sqrt n(P_n-P)\rightsquigarrow G
$$

for a tight Borel-measurable limit in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

For an appropriate functional $\Phi$,

$$
\Phi(\mathbb G_n)\rightsquigarrow\Phi(G).
$$

*Textbook clarification: The last implication requires the continuity condition of the continuous mapping theorem, rather than holding for an arbitrary functional (§1.3, Theorem 1.3.6; the Donsker setup is recalled at the start of §2.5, p. 127).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The bracketing condition uses the unknown population measure, whereas the nonbracketing condition uses a data-based norm but asks for more intricate asymptotic control. Which route is convenient depends on the class. The transcript describes the earlier CDF bracketing illustration through a density, but the textbook confirms the result for every distribution, so the existence of a density is not a requirement.

Glivenko-Cantelli results are uniform laws of large numbers; Donsker results are the corresponding process central limit theorems. The professor planned to present two Donsker approaches, with and without bracketing, and anticipated longer proofs involving chaining.

In applications, a method or test statistic is often represented as a functional of an empirical process. Establishing a Donsker result then gives a limiting functional of a Gaussian process. This does not necessarily finish the practical problem: the Gaussian covariance and the distribution of the functional may still depend on the unknown $P$. The professor mentioned bootstrap methods as a later way to estimate such limiting distributions, rather than treating the abstract convergence theorem as the final computational answer.
