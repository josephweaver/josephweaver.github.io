# 1. Moment Semimetrics and Continuity in Mean - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.5, Lemma 1.5.9, pp. 39-40

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

$$
\rho_p(s,t)=\left(E|X(s)-X(t)|^p\right)^{1/(p\vee1)}.
$$

**Def.** $X$ is uniformly $\rho$-continuous in the $p$th mean if

$$
E|X(s_n)-X(t_n)|^p\longrightarrow0
\quad\text{whenever}\quad
\rho(s_n,t_n)\longrightarrow0,
\qquad s_n,t_n\in T.
$$

**Lemma.** Let $X$ be a tight Borel measurable map into $\ell^\infty(T)$. Then there exists a semimetric on $T$ for which almost all paths of $X$ are uniformly continuous and $T$ is totally bounded.

Moreover, for any fixed $p>0$, the following statements are equivalent:

(i) For $\rho_p$, the set $T$ is totally bounded and almost all paths of $X$ are uniformly $\rho_p$-continuous.

(ii) For every semimetric $\rho$ for which almost all paths of $X$ are uniformly $\rho$-continuous, the map $t\mapsto X(t)$ is uniformly $\rho$-continuous in $p$th mean.

(iii) There exists a semimetric $\rho$ for which $T$ is totally bounded, $t\mapsto X(t)$ is uniformly $\rho$-continuous in $p$th mean, and almost all paths of $X$ are uniformly $\rho$-continuous.

*Textbook clarification: The final line of (iii) is abbreviated in my notes as “paths [… $\rho$-cont.]”; Lemma 1.5.9, p. 39, confirms that it is uniform $\rho$-continuity of almost all sample paths, separate from continuity in mean. The moment expressions must be finite wherever $\rho_p$ is used as a semimetric.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor returned to the choice of semimetric in the weak-convergence criteria. Finite-dimensional convergence and asymptotic tightness remain the goals, and a candidate limit can suggest a useful semimetric. The moment semimetric is convenient, but the point of the equivalences is that one has some freedom in how to establish the needed continuity properties. It is not a claim that every semimetric works without the stated conditions.

# 2. Gaussian Processes and Weak Convergence - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.5, Example 1.5.10, pp. 40-41

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Example: Gaussian Processes

**Def.** A stochastic process $X$ is called Gaussian if

$$
(X(t_1),\ldots,X(t_k))
$$

has a multivariate normal distribution in $\mathbb R^k$ for every $t_1,\ldots,t_k\in T$ and every $k=1,2,\ldots$.

**Corollary.** Let $X$ be a Gaussian process with the semimetrics $\rho_p$. Let $X_\alpha$ be a net of random elements with values in $\ell^\infty(T)$. Then there exists a version of $X$ which is a tight Borel measurable map into $\ell^\infty(T)$, and

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X,
$$

if and only if, for some $p>0$ (and then for every $p>0$):

1. The marginals of $X_\alpha$ converge weakly to the corresponding marginals of $X$.
2. $X_\alpha$ is asymptotically equicontinuous in probability with respect to $\rho_p$.
3. $T$ is totally bounded for $\rho_p$.

**Remark.** Gaussian random variables are completely characterized by their means and covariances.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Gaussian processes are a large subject that could occupy an entire course; only the facts needed here are being introduced. The professor connected the definition through finite-dimensional normal vectors with the construction of an entire process, mentioning Kolmogorov's theorem and the role of the mean and covariance functions.

The choice of semimetric and the requirement that the index set be totally bounded must work together. # 3. Four Modes of Convergence - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.9, Definition 1.9.1, pp. 52-53

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $X_\alpha,X:\Omega\to\mathbb D$, where $(\mathbb D,d)$ is an arbitrary metric space.

*Textbook clarification: These maps have a common underlying probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal A,P)$ in Definition 1.9.1, p. 52. They need not be measurable.*

**1. Outer probability.** $X_\alpha$ converges in outer probability to $X$ if

$$
d(X_\alpha,X)^*\xrightarrow{P}0.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
P^*\{d(X_\alpha,X)>\varepsilon\}
=P\{d(X_\alpha,X)^*>\varepsilon\}\longrightarrow0
\quad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
$$

Notation: $X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^*}X$.

**2. Almost uniformly.** $X_\alpha$ converges almost uniformly to $X$ if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a measurable set $A$ with

$$
P(A)\geq1-\varepsilon
$$

and $d(X_\alpha,X)\to0$ uniformly on $A$.

Notation: $X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X$.

**3. Outer almost surely.** $X_\alpha$ converges outer almost surely to $X$ if

$$
d(X_\alpha,X)^*\longrightarrow0\quad\text{a.s.}
$$

for some versions of the measurable covers $d(X_\alpha,X)^*$.

Notation: $X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{as}^*}X$.

**4. Almost surely.** $X_\alpha$ converges almost surely to $X$ if

$$
P_*\left\{\lim_\alpha d(X_\alpha,X)=0\right\}=1.
$$

Notation: $X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{as}}X$.

**Remark.** For nonmeasurable nets these definitions are not equivalent, and the usual implications need not all hold.

*Correction: My notes say broadly that these definitions “do not imply each other.” That wording is too strong: some implications do hold, as the next topic records. The warning in §1.9, pp. 52-53, is that the usual classical relations cannot all be carried over. In particular, for nonmeasurable sequences even everywhere convergence need not imply the first three modes. The textbook subsequently uses the outer-almost-sure and almost-sure notions primarily for sequences.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The star on the distance denotes a measurable cover. Since the maps are not assumed measurable, an event defined through their distance may fail to be in the original sigma-field; this is why outer probability is needed. Almost uniform convergence instead gives uniform control on a measurable set of arbitrarily large probability, rather than on the whole sample space.

The professor stressed that working in a supremum norm can bring measurability difficulties even when individual coordinates look harmless. The uniform norm is useful and strong, but its behavior need not be obtained simply by taking an $L^p$ argument and sending $p$ to infinity. Familiar convergence implications therefore need to be checked in this setting rather than assumed. The counterexamples were discussed as warnings, not developed in the lecture.

# 4. Relations Between the Modes - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.9, Lemmas 1.9.2-1.9.3, p. 53

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $X$ be Borel measurable.

**1.**

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{as}^*}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{P^*}X.
$$

**2.**

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{P^*}X
$$

if and only if every subsequence $X_{n'}$ has a further subsequence $X_{n''}$ with

$$
X_{n''}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{as}^*}X.
$$

**3. Egorov's theorem.**

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{as}^*}X
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X.
$$

**4.**

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sup_{\beta\succeq\alpha}d(X_\beta,X)^*\xrightarrow{P}0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sup_{\beta\succeq\alpha}d(X_\beta,X)\xrightarrow{P^*}0.
$$

**5.**

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^*}X.
$$

*Textbook clarification: Items 1-3 are statements for sequences $X_n$, whereas items 4-5 hold for general nets $X_\alpha$. The positions of the stars in item 4 follow Lemma 1.9.3, p. 53: in the middle expression each individual distance is replaced by its measurable cover; in the last expression the supremum is handled by outer probability.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor presented these as the relations that can still be used with the appropriate measurability assumptions on the limit. They allow a convergence result to be strengthened in some theoretical arguments. The distinction between a sequence and a general net matters, even when the notation looks similar.

# 5. Continuous Mapping - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.9, Theorem 1.9.5, pp. 54-55

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $g:\mathbb D\to\mathbb E$ be continuous at every point of a Borel set $\mathbb D_0\subset\mathbb D$. Let $X$ be Borel measurable with $P(X\in\mathbb D_0)=1$. Then

(i)

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^*}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g(X_\alpha)\xrightarrow{P^*}g(X).
$$

(ii)

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g(X_\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}g(X).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

This is another version of the continuous mapping principle, now for the stronger modes of convergence rather than weak convergence. The professor said that convergence in outer probability will be used frequently and may be called simply convergence in probability when the possible measurability issue is understood. Almost uniform convergence can also be useful when uniform control over more complicated weighted processes is needed.

# 6. Connections with Weak Convergence - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.10, Lemma 1.10.2, pp. 57-58

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $Y_\alpha,X_\alpha,X$ be arbitrary maps, with $X$ Borel measurable.

(i) If $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$ and

$$
d(X_\alpha,Y_\alpha)\xrightarrow{P^*}0,
$$

then $Y_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$.

(ii) If $X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^*}X$, then $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$.

(iii) For a constant $c\in\mathbb D$,

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^*}c
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow c.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The first part explains a common proof strategy in statistics. A statistic or process of interest may be difficult to analyze directly, so one finds a simpler surrogate whose weak convergence is easier to prove. If the distance between the two tends to zero in outer probability, they have the same weak limit. The professor emphasized that this is the theoretical justification for replacing a complicated object by a sufficiently close, more tractable one.

The remaining parts parallel classical probability: probability convergence implies weak convergence, and the two coincide when the limit is constant.

# 7. Almost Sure Representations - van der Vaart & Wellner §1.10, Theorem 1.10.4, pp. 58-59

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\mathbb D$ be an arbitrary net indexed by a nontrivial directed set. Let $X_\infty$ be Borel measurable and separable.

If $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X_\infty$, then there exists a probability space

$$
(\widetilde\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal A},\widetilde P)
$$

and maps $\widetilde X_\alpha:\widetilde\Omega\to\mathbb D$ such that

$$
\widetilde X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}\widetilde X_\infty
$$

and

$$
\widetilde E^*f(\widetilde X_\alpha)=E^*f(X_\alpha)
$$

for every bounded $f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb R$ and every $\alpha$, including $\alpha=\infty$.

*Textbook clarification: My notes change the name of the constructed maps between the existence line and the conclusion; the notation is made consistent here. Theorem 1.10.4, p. 59, gives almost uniform convergence, despite its title “a.s. representations.” “Nontrivial” means that the directed set permits a net of strictly positive real numbers tending to zero (§1.10, p. 58); it does not merely mean nonempty. The functions $f$ in the equality need only be bounded, not continuous or measurable.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The representation result allows a weakly convergent collection to be realized on another probability space where a stronger form of convergence holds, while retaining the indicated distributional information. It does not assert that the original maps themselves become almost uniformly convergent on their original spaces.
