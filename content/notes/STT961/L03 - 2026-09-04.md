# 1. Portmanteau Theorem — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Theorem 1.3.4

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Portmanteau Theorem

The following statements are equivalent:

**(i)**

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow L
$$

**(ii)**

$$
\liminf_\alpha P_\star (X_\alpha\in G)\geq L(G)
\qquad\text{for every open }G.
$$

**(iii)**

$$
\limsup_\alpha P^\star (X_\alpha\in F)\leq L(F)
\qquad\forall\text{ closed }F.
$$

**(iv)**

$$
\liminf_\alpha E_\star f(X_\alpha)\geq\int f\,dL
$$

for every lower semicontinuous $f$ bounded below.

**(v)**

$$
\limsup_\alpha E^\star f(X_\alpha)\leq\int f\,dL
$$

for every upper semicontinuous $f$ bounded above.

**(vi)**

$$
\lim_\alpha P^\star (X_\alpha\in B)
=
\lim_\alpha P_\star (X_\alpha\in B)
=
L(B)
$$

for every Borel set $B$ with

$$
L(\partial B)
=
L(\overline B-B^\circ)=0.
$$

**(vii)**

$$
\liminf_\alpha E_\star f(X_\alpha)\geq\int f\,dL
$$

for every bounded Lipschitz continuous $f\geq0$.


---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced the **Portmanteau theorem** as a major result connecting several equivalent ways to characterize weak convergence. She described it as a theorem assembled from results developed by different mathematicians, emphasizing its importance in probability theory.

The first condition is the definition of weak convergence introduced in the previous lecture. The second and third conditions express weak convergence through probabilities of open and closed sets. Open sets give a lower bound involving the limit inferior of inner probabilities, while closed sets give an upper bound involving the limit superior of outer probabilities. The professor observed that these two conditions are closely related through complements and the relationship between inner and outer probabilities.

The fourth and fifth conditions use lower and upper semicontinuous functions. The professor described these as generalizations of continuous functions and noted that replacing a function by its negative exchanges lower and upper semicontinuity, reverses inequalities, and interchanges inner and outer expectations.

The sixth condition concerns Borel sets whose boundaries have zero probability under the limiting measure. The boundary is the difference between the closure and the interior of the set. For such sets, both inner and outer probabilities converge to the limiting probability $L(B)$.

The seventh condition restricts attention to bounded, nonnegative Lipschitz continuous functions. A Lipschitz function satisfies a stronger regularity condition than ordinary continuity: differences between function values are bounded by a constant times the metric distance between their arguments. The professor emphasized that this condition permits a smaller class of functions while still characterizing weak convergence.

The professor connected these statements to classical convergence in distribution for real-valued random variables. In classical probability, distribution functions and their continuity points are central to defining convergence in distribution. On general metric spaces, however, distribution functions may not provide a natural description. Using expectations of bounded continuous functions gives a formulation that extends to arbitrary metric spaces.

The professor did not prove the Portmanteau theorem, noting that its proof involves substantial probability and analysis. She recommended consulting the textbook for its development.

# 2. Upper and Lower Semicontinuity — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Theorem 1.3.4

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Def.** $f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb R$ is upper semicont. if

$$
\{f\geq c\}
$$

is closed for every $c$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced **upper and lower semicontinuity** as weaker forms of ordinary continuity. These concepts are needed for conditions (iv) and (v) of the Portmanteau theorem.

A function $f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb R$ is upper semicontinuous if, for every real number $c$, its upper level set

$$
\{x\in\mathbb D:f(x)\geq c\}
$$

is closed. The professor described this as selecting a level $c$ and considering all points where the function takes a value at least that large.

She also gave the equivalent characterization

$$
\limsup_{x\to x_0}f(x)\leq f(x_0).
$$

This allows the limiting behavior of the function near $x_0$ to differ from its value at $x_0$, provided the limit superior does not exceed that value.

**Lower semicontinuity** is defined by applying upper semicontinuity to $-f$. Equivalently, the sets

$$
\{x\in\mathbb D:f(x)\leq c\}
$$

are closed for every $c$, or

$$
\liminf_{x\to x_0}f(x)\geq f(x_0).
$$

The professor emphasized that changing $f$ to $-f$ exchanges upper and lower semicontinuity. This same transformation explains the correspondence between conditions (iv) and (v) of the Portmanteau theorem, including the reversal of inequalities and the interchange of inner and outer expectations.

The professor did not provide a proof of these equivalences, treating them as familiar results from real analysis.

# 3. Continuous Mapping Theorem — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Theorem 1.3.6

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Cor. to Portmanteau Th.**

For every function $g$ continuous a.s. with $X$,

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\implies
g(X_\alpha)\rightsquigarrow g(X).
$$

**Continuous Mapping Th.**

Let $g:\mathbb D\to\mathbb E$ be continuous at $\forall$ point $x\in\mathbb D_0\subset\mathbb D$.

If $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$ and $X$ takes values in $\mathbb D_0$, then

$$
g(X_\alpha)\rightsquigarrow g(X).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced the **Continuous Mapping Theorem** as a natural consequence of the Portmanteau theorem. It extends the familiar result from classical probability that convergence in distribution is preserved when a continuous function is applied to the random variables.

The theorem considers a function $g$ mapping between two metric spaces, $\mathbb D$ and $\mathbb E$, which may have different metrics. The function does not need to be continuous everywhere on $\mathbb D$. It is sufficient for $g$ to be continuous at the points in a subset $\mathbb D_0$ where the limiting random element $X$ takes its values.

Thus, if $X_\alpha$ converges weakly to $X$, and $g$ is continuous at every point in the range of $X$, then $g(X_\alpha)$ converges weakly to $g(X)$.

The professor emphasized that the continuity requirement is imposed on the **limiting random element**, rather than requiring continuity throughout the entire domain. In particular, continuity almost surely with respect to the distribution of $X$ is sufficient.

The theorem will be used repeatedly in subsequent proofs. The professor noted that students have already encountered versions of the Continuous Mapping Theorem in classical probability, and this formulation extends the same principle to weak convergence in general metric spaces.

# 4. Asymptotic Measurability and Asymptotic Tightness — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Definition 1.3.7 and Lemma 1.3.8

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Def.** The net of maps $X_\alpha$ is asymp. measurable $\iff$

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0
$$

for $\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D)$.

**Def.** The net $X_\alpha$ is asymp. tight if for $\forall\varepsilon>0$

$\exists$ compact $K$:

$$
\liminf_\alpha P_\star (X_\alpha\in K^\delta)
\geq1-\varepsilon
\qquad\text{for }\forall\delta>0,
$$

where

$$
K^\delta=\text{$\delta$-enlargement of }K
=\{y\in\mathbb D:d(y,K)<\delta\}.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The distance from a point $y$ to the set $K$ is defined by*

$$
d(y,K)=\inf_{x\in K}d(y,x).
$$

*The same compact set $K$ must satisfy the inequality for every $\delta>0$.*

---

**Lemma.**

1. If $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$, then $X_\alpha$ is asymp. measurable.

2. If $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$, then

   $$
   X_\alpha\text{ is asymp. tight}
   \iff X\text{ is tight}.
   $$

**Remark:**

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists\text{ compact }K:
\quad P(X\in K)\geq1-\varepsilon.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced **asymptotic measurability** as a property of a net of potentially nonmeasurable random maps. For every bounded continuous function $f$, we compare the outer and inner expectations of $f(X_\alpha)$. If their difference converges to zero, the net is asymptotically measurable. Individual maps need not be measurable, but the discrepancy between their inner and outer expectations must vanish asymptotically.

The professor then introduced **asymptotic tightness**, which extends the earlier notion of tightness for a probability measure to a net of random elements. For every $\varepsilon>0$, we require a compact set $K$ such that the limit inferior of the inner probabilities of $X_\alpha$ belonging to a $\delta$-enlargement of $K$ is at least $1-\varepsilon$, for every $\delta>0$.

The professor illustrated $K^\delta$ by drawing a compact set surrounded by a larger region. The enlargement consists of all points whose distance from $K$ is less than $\delta$. She described this distance as the infimum of the metric distances between the point and elements of $K$, comparing the enlargement to a boundary or envelope surrounding the original set in Euclidean space.

The professor emphasized the connection among **weak convergence, asymptotic measurability, and asymptotic tightness**. The first result states that weak convergence implies asymptotic measurability. This is consistent with the definition of weak convergence, which requires convergence of outer expectations to expectations under the limiting law.

The second result states that, when $X_\alpha$ converges weakly to $X$, the net is asymptotically tight if and only if the limiting random element $X$ has a tight distribution. The professor noted that this is particularly useful when the limiting distribution is known to be tight, as frequently occurs with familiar Gaussian limits.

These results provide connections between properties of a converging net and properties of its limiting law. The professor indicated that they are useful when establishing more general weak convergence results.

# 5. Prohorov's Theorem — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Theorem 1.3.9

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Prohorov's Theorem

**(i)** If the net $X_\alpha$ is asymp. tight and asymp. measurable, then it has a subnet $X_{\alpha(\beta)}$ that converges in law to a tight Borel law.

**(ii)** If the sequence $X_n$ is asymp. tight and asymp. measurable, then it has a subsequence $X_{n_k}$ that converges in law to a tight Borel law.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced **Prohorov's theorem** as one of the central results of weak convergence theory. It connects the two properties introduced previously—asymptotic tightness and asymptotic measurability—to the existence of weakly convergent subnets or subsequences.

The first statement concerns a general net of random maps. If the net is asymptotically tight and asymptotically measurable, then there exists a subnet that converges weakly to a tight Borel probability law. The second statement gives the corresponding result for sequences, guaranteeing a weakly convergent subsequence under the same conditions.

The professor emphasized that **the theorem does not guarantee convergence of the entire net or sequence**. Instead, it establishes the existence of a convergent subnet or subsequence. This distinction is important because asymptotic tightness and asymptotic measurability alone are insufficient to conclude that the original net converges weakly.

Together with the preceding lemma, the theorem establishes an important relationship: weak convergence implies asymptotic measurability and, when the limiting law is tight, asymptotic tightness. Conversely, asymptotic tightness and asymptotic measurability guarantee the existence of a weakly convergent subnet or subsequence, but not necessarily convergence of the full collection.

The professor noted that this result is useful in theoretical justifications of statistical methods. Sometimes the existence of a convergent subsequence is sufficient for a particular argument, even when convergence of the entire sequence has not been established.

The professor identified Prohorov's theorem as an important result from classical probability theory that continues to play a central role in the more general framework of potentially nonmeasurable random elements.

# 6. Other Properties of Weak Convergence — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Theorem 1.3.10 and Lemma 1.3.12

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Other Properties of $\rightsquigarrow$

**1.** Let $\mathbb D_0\subset\mathbb D$ be an arbitrary set. Both $\mathbb D_0,\mathbb D$ are equipped with the same metric. Let $X_\alpha,X$ take values in $\mathbb D_0$.

Then

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\text{ as maps in }\mathbb D_0
\iff
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\text{ as maps in }\mathbb D.
$$

**2.** Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be finite Borel measures on $\mathbb D$.

If

$$
\int f\,dL_1=\int f\,dL_2
\qquad\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D),
$$

then

$$
L_1=L_2.
$$

**3.** Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be tight Borel prob. measures on $\mathbb D$.

If

$$
\int f\,dL_1=\int f\,dL_2
$$

for $\forall f$ in a vector lattice $\mathcal F\subset C_b(\mathbb D)$ that contains const. functions and separates points in $\mathbb D$,

then

$$
L_1=L_2.
$$

**Def.** Vector lattice $\mathcal F$ is a vector space closed under taking positive parts:

$$
f\in\mathcal F,\qquad f^+=f\vee0\in\mathcal F.
$$

**Def.** $\mathcal F$ separates points in $\mathbb D$ if

$$
\forall x\ne y\in\mathbb D,\quad
\exists f\in\mathcal F:
f(x)\ne f(y).
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced several additional properties of weak convergence, beginning with the observation that **weak convergence is preserved when we change the ambient metric space without changing the metric**. If both the random maps and their limit take values in a subset $\mathbb D_0\subset\mathbb D$, then weak convergence in the smaller space is equivalent to weak convergence in the larger space, provided the same metric is used. The professor noted that this is useful when working with restrictions or truncations of mathematical objects.

The second property concerns **uniqueness of probability measures**. If two finite Borel measures assign the same integral to every bounded continuous real-valued function, then the two measures must be identical. Thus, integrals against the entire class $C_b(\mathbb D)$ uniquely determine a finite Borel measure.

The third property shows that, for tight Borel probability measures, it is possible to replace $C_b(\mathbb D)$ with a smaller class of functions. Specifically, a vector lattice containing the constant functions and separating points is sufficient to determine the measure uniquely.

The professor defined a **vector lattice** as a vector space of functions that is closed under taking positive parts. If a function belongs to the lattice, then its positive part also belongs to the lattice. She related this structure to classical integration theory, in which functions are decomposed into positive and negative parts and more complicated integrable functions are constructed from simpler ones.

The professor also explained what it means for a class of functions to **separate points**. For any two distinct points $x$ and $y$ in the metric space, there must be at least one function $f$ in the class such that $f(x)\ne f(y)$. If every function assigns the same value to two points, that class cannot distinguish them.

The significance of the third property is that **we need not always work with every bounded continuous function**. Under the stated conditions, a smaller collection of functions contains enough information to identify a tight Borel probability measure. The professor connected this to the construction of integration theory using function classes with appropriate algebraic properties.

# 7. Asymptotic Measurability from a Separating Algebra — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Lemma 1.3.13

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**4.** Let the net $X_\alpha$ be asymp. tight and

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0
$$

$\forall f$ in subalgebra $\mathcal F\subset C_b(\mathbb D)$ that separates points in $\mathbb D$.

Then $X_\alpha$ is asymp. measurable.

**Def.** $\mathcal F$ is algebra if $\mathcal F$ is a vector space that is closed under product:

$$
f,g\in\mathcal F\implies fg\in\mathcal F.
$$

**(Ch. 1.3 is done)**

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced a final result concerning **asymptotic measurability**, building on the earlier definition and the properties of function classes discussed in the preceding topic.

Recall that asymptotic measurability requires the difference between outer and inner expectations to converge to zero for every bounded continuous function. This lemma shows that, when the net is already asymptotically tight, it is sufficient to verify the condition for a **smaller collection of functions** rather than all of $C_b(\mathbb D)$.

The smaller collection must be a subalgebra of $C_b(\mathbb D)$ that separates points in $\mathbb D$. The professor explained that an algebra is a vector space of functions that is also closed under multiplication. Thus, whenever $f$ and $g$ belong to the algebra, their product $fg$ must belong to it as well.

The professor distinguished an algebra from an ordinary vector space. A vector space is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, but products of its elements need not remain in the space. Requiring closure under products provides additional algebraic structure.

The requirement that $\mathcal F$ separates points means that for every pair of distinct points $x,y\in\mathbb D$, there exists at least one function $f\in\mathcal F$ satisfying $f(x)\ne f(y)$.

The professor emphasized that **asymptotic tightness allows measurability conditions to be checked on a smaller class of functions**. This can simplify proofs because verifying convergence of inner and outer expectations for every bounded continuous function may be difficult, whereas verifying it on a suitable algebra may be more manageable.

The professor concluded the discussion of Chapter 1.3 at this point, noting that the textbook contains additional definitions, theorems, and proofs that would not be covered in the lecture.

# 8. Spaces of Bounded Functions — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.5

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Ch. 1.5 Spaces of Bounded Functions

Let $T$ be an arbitrary set.

The space $\ell^\infty(T)$ is the set of all uniformly bounded real functions on $T$.

$$
z:T\to\mathbb R:
\quad
\|z\|_T=\sup_{t\in T}|z(t)|<\infty.
$$

$\ell^\infty(T)$ is a metric space wrt the uniform distance (metric)

$$
d(z_1,z_2)=\|z_1-z_2\|_T.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor began Chapter 1.5 by introducing **the space of bounded functions**, which will serve as a primary mathematical setting for studying empirical processes and weak convergence.

We begin with an arbitrary index set $T$. The professor emphasized that $T$ need not be an interval of real numbers or even a subset of Euclidean space. It can be a much more complicated mathematical object, including a collection of functions or points on a manifold.

The space $\ell^\infty(T)$ consists of all real-valued functions on $T$ that are uniformly bounded. A function belongs to this space when the supremum of its absolute values over the entire index set is finite.

The professor then defined the **supremum norm** and the corresponding uniform distance between two functions. The distance between $z_1$ and $z_2$ is determined by the largest absolute difference between their values over $T$.

She noted that this distance satisfies the properties of a metric: nonnegativity, symmetry, the triangle inequality, and the condition that zero distance implies the two functions agree everywhere on $T$. Consequently, $\ell^\infty(T)$ is a metric space under the uniform distance.

The professor emphasized that this function space will be particularly important throughout the course because it provides a setting in which entire functions, rather than individual real-valued random variables, can be treated as elements of a metric space.

# 9. Stochastic Processes and Sample Paths — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.5

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Def.** A stochastic process is an indexed collection

$$
\{X(t):t\in T\}
$$

of random variables defined on the same probability space

$$
(\Omega,\mathcal A,P).
$$

Every

$$
X(t):\Omega\to\mathbb R
$$

is measurable.

If every sample path

$$
t\mapsto X(t,\omega)
$$

is bounded, then the stochastic process yields a map

$$
X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T).
$$

*Textbook clarification: The individual coordinate maps $X(t)$ are measurable, but this does not necessarily imply that $X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T)$ is Borel measurable under the supremum metric. This distinction is important in the weak convergence theory developed in the course.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced **stochastic processes** as indexed collections of random variables defined on a common probability space. Each $X(t)$ must be a measurable random variable, and the index set $T$ can be arbitrary.

She emphasized that a stochastic process can be viewed from two perspectives. If we fix $t$, then $X(t,\omega)$ is a random variable whose value depends on $\omega$. If instead we fix $\omega$, then $t\mapsto X(t,\omega)$ is an ordinary function of the index $t$, called a **sample path**.

The professor noted that $T$ does not necessarily represent time. It may be a parameter set, a space of functions, or a more complicated mathematical structure. For example, the index set could itself be a collection of functions, allowing us to construct random variables indexed by other functions. She also mentioned geometric structures such as manifolds as possible index sets.

When every sample path is uniformly bounded, we can regard the entire stochastic process as a map from the underlying probability space into $\ell^\infty(T)$. In this interpretation, each outcome $\omega$ determines one bounded function on $T$.

The professor emphasized that **this function-valued perspective is central to the course**. Rather than studying convergence of individual random variables alone, we will study convergence of random functions as elements of a metric space.

She concluded the lecture at this point, explaining that the next class would continue the discussion of stochastic processes and connect these ideas to earlier probability coursework.
