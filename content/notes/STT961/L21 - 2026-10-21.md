# 1. The Multiplier Inequality - van der Vaart & Wellner, Lemma 2.9.1, p. 177

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.9. Multiplier Inequality

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac12\|\xi_1\|_1E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
&\leq E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\\
&\leq2(n_0-1)E^*\|Z_1\|_{\mathcal F}
E\left[\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|}{\sqrt n}\right]\\
&\quad+2\sqrt2\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}\max_{n_0\leq k\leq n}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{i=n_0}^k\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F},
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}=\int_0^\infty\sqrt{P(|\xi_1|>x)}\,dx.
$$

*Textbook clarification: This continues the previous lecture's statement. The assumptions are that the $Z_i$ are i.i.d. stochastic processes with $E^*\|Z_1\|_{\mathcal F}<\infty$, the $\varepsilon_i$ are independent Rademacher variables, the $\xi_i$ are i.i.d. mean-zero variables independent of the $Z_i$, and $1\leq n_0\leq n$. The auxiliary Rademacher sequence can be constructed independently of both other sequences. These are the conditions of Lemma 2.9.1, p. 177.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor returned to the multiplier inequality as an analogue of the earlier symmetrization lemma. The lower comparison is relatively direct, while the upper comparison needs additional work. The cutoff $n_0$ can be chosen anywhere between one and $n$; the inequality is not tied to one particular choice of that index.

# 2. Proof of the Lower Bound - van der Vaart & Wellner, proof of Lemma 2.9.1, pp. 177-178

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Define $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$, $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$, and $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ as mutually independent sequences on a larger probability space.

Let $\xi_i$ be symmetric. Then

$$
\xi_i\overset d=\varepsilon_i|\xi_i|.
$$

$$
E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i E_\xi|\xi_i|\,Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i|\xi_i|Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Since $E_\xi|\xi_i|=\|\xi_1\|_1$, the left inequality is proved for symmetric variables.

In general, introduce an independent copy $\eta_1,\ldots,\eta_n$ of $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$. Since $E\eta_i=0$,

$$
E|\xi_i|=E|\xi_i-E\eta_i|
\leq E|\xi_i-\eta_i|
\leq E|\xi_i|+E|\eta_i|.
$$

The differences $\xi_i-\eta_i$ are symmetric, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\xi_1\|_1E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
&\leq\|\xi_1-\eta_1\|_1
E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\\
&\leq E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n(\xi_i-\eta_i)Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\\
&\leq2E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide by $2\sqrt n$ to obtain the general lower bound.

*Correction: The final chain in my notes places the expectation of the symmetrized difference sum after the doubled multiplier norm with an equality, and reverses the Jensen comparison with $E|\xi_i-\eta_i|$. The corrected order above is the one used in the proof on p. 178: Jensen gives the lower comparison, while the triangle inequality gives the factor $2$ upper comparison.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor distinguished mean-zero weights from symmetric weights: mean zero alone does not imply symmetry. The proof starts with symmetry because an independent Rademacher sign times the magnitude has the same distribution as the original weight. The probability space can be enlarged to accommodate all the independent auxiliary variables needed for the argument.

For nonsymmetric weights, an independent copy supplies symmetric differences. The professor emphasized that this is the same “ghost sample” device used earlier in symmetrization. Rather than invent a separate proof, one proves the symmetric case first and then returns to it by doubling the collection of weights. The additional factor in the general inequality is the cost of this reduction.

# 3. Proof of the Upper Bound - van der Vaart & Wellner, proof of Lemma 2.9.1, pp. 178-179

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let the $\xi_i$ be symmetric. Denote by

$$
\widetilde\xi_1\geq\widetilde\xi_2\geq\cdots\geq\widetilde\xi_n
$$

the reversed order statistics of $|\xi_1|,\ldots,|\xi_n|$. Thus

$$
\widetilde\xi_1=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|,
\qquad\widetilde\xi_n=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|.
$$

By reordering the i.i.d. processes,

$$
E_{\varepsilon}E_Z^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i|\xi_i|Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
=E_{\varepsilon}E_Z^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i\widetilde\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
E^*\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq(n_0-1)E\widetilde\xi_1\,E^*\|Z_1\|_{\mathcal F}
+E^*\left\|\sum_{i=n_0}^n\varepsilon_i\widetilde\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Use the telescoping identity

$$
\widetilde\xi_i=\sum_{k=i}^n(\widetilde\xi_k-\widetilde\xi_{k+1}),
\qquad\widetilde\xi_{n+1}=0.
$$

The indices obey $n_0\leq i\leq k\leq n$. Switching the order of summation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^*\left\|\sum_{i=n_0}^n\varepsilon_i
\sum_{k=i}^n(\widetilde\xi_k-\widetilde\xi_{k+1})Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
&=E^*\left\|\sum_{k=n_0}^n
\sqrt k(\widetilde\xi_k-\widetilde\xi_{k+1})
\left(\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{i=n_0}^k\varepsilon_iZ_i\right)\right\|_{\mathcal F}\\
&\leq E\left[\sum_{k=n_0}^n\sqrt k(\widetilde\xi_k-\widetilde\xi_{k+1})\right]
\max_{n_0\leq k\leq n}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{i=n_0}^k\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Correction: My notes introduce $1/\sqrt k$ inside the sum after changing summation order but omit the compensating $\sqrt k$ outside it. Restored that factor, which is essential to the next bound (Lemma 2.9.1 proof, p. 178).*

Concentrate on the first factor:

$$
E\sum_{k=n_0}^n\sqrt k(\widetilde\xi_k-\widetilde\xi_{k+1})
=E\sum_{k=n_0}^n\int_{\widetilde\xi_{k+1}}^{\widetilde\xi_k}\sqrt k\,dt.
$$

*Correction: The integral endpoints are reversed in my notes. Since $\widetilde\xi_k\geq\widetilde\xi_{k+1}$, the integral runs from $\widetilde\xi_{k+1}$ to $\widetilde\xi_k$, as on p. 178.*

For $\widetilde\xi_{k+1}<t\leq\widetilde\xi_k$,

$$
k=\#\{i:|\xi_i|\geq t\}.
$$

Therefore, by Jensen's inequality, since the square root is concave,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\sum_{k=n_0}^n\sqrt k(\widetilde\xi_k-\widetilde\xi_{k+1})
&\leq\int_0^\infty E\sqrt{\#\{i:|\xi_i|\geq t\}}\,dt\\
&\leq\int_0^\infty\sqrt{E\#\{i:|\xi_i|\geq t\}}\,dt\\
&=\int_0^\infty\sqrt{nP(|\xi_1|\geq t)}\,dt
=\sqrt n\,\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For general, not necessarily symmetric $\xi_i$,

$$
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\xi_i-\eta_i)Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Repeat the symmetric proof to obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
&\leq(n_0-1)E^*\|Z_1\|_{\mathcal F}
E\left[\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i-\eta_i|}{\sqrt n}\right]\\
&\quad+\|\xi_1-\eta_1\|_{2,1}\max_{n_0\leq k\leq n}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{i=n_0}^k\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Use

$$
E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i-\eta_i|
\leq2E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|,
$$

$$
\|\xi_1-\eta_1\|_{2,1}\leq2\sqrt2\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}.
$$

This gives the upper bound.

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Sorting the magnitudes also entails reindexing the corresponding Rademacher variables and processes. The professor explicitly noted the abuse of notation: the symbols are reused after the reordering, although they no longer denote the identical indexed entries. Their i.i.d. structure makes the distributional comparison valid.

The largest $n_0-1$ weights are treated crudely by bounding each by the maximum. This produces the first term of the upper bound. The rest are handled through the telescoping representation. Reversing the order statistics makes successive differences nonnegative, which is what allows the normalized partial-sum expectations to be bounded by their maximum. The professor explicitly described dividing and multiplying by $\sqrt k$ in this step.

The second main trick rewrites differences of neighboring order statistics as lengths of adjacent intervals. On such an interval, the number of observations exceeding the integration variable is exactly the corresponding index. In response to a question, the professor explained that adding the interval integrals creates an integral over their union. Extending it to all of $[0,\infty)$ is an upper bound because the integrand is nonnegative. Jensen's inequality then moves the expectation inside the concave square root, giving the tail integral in the statement.

For nonsymmetric weights, the same proof is applied to differences with an independent copy. The usual triangle inequality handles the maximum term, but $\|\cdot\|_{2,1}$ is not itself a norm with the ordinary triangle inequality. This accounts for the extra $\sqrt2$ in the comparison. The professor suggested the tail-event argument for this bound as a possible homework problem, without assigning it definitively during the lecture.

The bounds are intentionally not sharp. The professor noted that bounded weights or a more careful treatment of the integration range can improve constants, but the purpose here is the comparison needed for the central limit theorem. The combination of a symmetric reduction, telescoping sums, and a counting interpretation of order statistics was the main proof technique to retain.

# 4. The Multiplier Central Limit Theorem - van der Vaart & Wellner, Theorem 2.9.2, p. 179

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.9. Multiplier CLT

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions. Let $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ be i.i.d. random variables with mean $0$, variance $1$, and

$$
\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}<\infty,
$$

independent of $X_1,\ldots,X_n$. Then the sequence

$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i(\delta_{X_i}-P)
$$

converges to a tight limit in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$ if and only if $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker. In that case the limit is a $P$-Brownian bridge.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The theorem confirms the earlier intuition: under the stated condition on the weights, the multiplier central limit theorem holds exactly when the ordinary empirical-process central limit theorem holds. In response to a question about $\delta_{X_i}$, the professor confirmed that it is the Dirac measure at the observation.

The limiting Brownian bridge is not meant to be the same random realization as the limit of the original empirical process. The professor announced that the following lecture would state a joint-convergence corollary in which the two processes converge together to independent Brownian bridges. That corollary was mentioned here but not proved.

# 5. Proof through Asymptotic Equicontinuity - van der Vaart & Wellner, proof of Theorem 2.9.2, pp. 179-180

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**1. Center the class.** Both processes

$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\delta_{X_i}-P),
\qquad
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i(\delta_{X_i}-P)
$$

can be indexed by $f-Pf$ instead of $f$. Without loss of generality,

$$
Pf=0\qquad\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

**2. Check marginal convergence.**

**3. Envelope tails.** Use the minimal centered envelope

$$
F(x)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|f(x)-Pf|,
$$

which becomes $F(x)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|f(x)|$ after the centering in step 1. If $\mathcal F$ is Donsker, then

$$
P^*(F>x)=o(x^{-2})\qquad(x\to\infty).
$$

If the multiplier processes converge to a tight limit, then

$$
P^*(|\xi_1|F>x)=o(x^{-2})\qquad(x\to\infty).
$$

**4. Integrability.** In either case,

$$
P^*F<\infty.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The notes put a two-way arrow between the envelope tail displays and between $P^*F<\infty$ and $P^*(|\xi_1|F)<\infty$. The proof on p. 179 invokes Lemma 2.3.9 separately under each tight-convergence hypothesis to obtain the tail condition needed in that direction. Independence and $0<E|\xi_1|<\infty$ then give the required envelope integrability. Here $F$ is this minimal centered envelope, not an arbitrary larger envelope; the tightness conclusion does not control the tails of an arbitrarily enlarged envelope.*

**5. Asymptotic equicontinuity.** With $Z_i=\delta_{X_i}-P$, compare

$$
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\to0
$$

and

$$
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\to0
\qquad(\delta_n\downarrow0).
$$

The multiplier inequality will help. Since $\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}<\infty$,

$$
E\left[\frac1{\sqrt n}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|\right]\to0.
$$

Use the multiplier inequality, first for fixed $n_0$ and $\delta>0$. The term

$$
2(n_0-1)E^*\|Z_1\|_{\mathcal F}
E\left[\frac1{\sqrt n}\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|\right]
$$

vanishes as $n\to\infty$. Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac12\|\xi_1\|_1\limsup_{n\to\infty}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}\\
&\leq2\sqrt2\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}
\sup_{k\geq n_0}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{i=n_0}^k\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Textbook clarification: The notes use the shrinking index $\delta_n$ directly inside the intermediate maximum. The proof on p. 180 first establishes this bound for every fixed $n_0$ and $\delta>0$ and then passes to the equicontinuity limits. This order avoids interchanging a changing function class and a supremum over partial sums without justification. The notation $\mathcal F_\delta$ denotes the local difference class used in the preceding equicontinuity criteria.*

Use the symmetrization inequalities to remove the Rademacher variables $\varepsilon_i$. The resulting mean equicontinuity conditions are equivalent:

$$
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\to0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\to0.
$$

*Textbook clarification: Theorem 2.9.2's proof uses Lemma 2.3.6 to compare the symmetrized and unsymmetrized means, and Lemma 2.3.11 to pass between the mean and probability versions of asymptotic equicontinuity (p. 180).*
