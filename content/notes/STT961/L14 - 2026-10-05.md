# 1. Donsker Theorem Under Uniform Entropy - §2.5.1, Theorem 2.5.2, p. 127

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall

$$
\mathbb G_n=\sqrt n(P_n-P)\rightsquigarrow G
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F).
$$

**Theorem (without bracketing).** Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions with envelope $F$ satisfying the uniform entropy bound

$$
\int_0^\infty\sup_Q
\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q))}\,d\epsilon<\infty.
$$

The supremum is over all finitely discrete probability measures $Q$ on $(\mathcal X,\mathcal A)$ with

$$
\|F\|_{Q,2}^2=\int F^2\,dQ>0.
$$

Let

$$
\mathcal F_\delta=
\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta\},
\qquad
\mathcal F_\infty^2=\{h^2:h\in\mathcal F_\infty\}.
$$

Suppose $\mathcal F_\delta$ and $\mathcal F_\infty^2$ are $P$-measurable for every $\delta>0$. If $P^\star F^2<\infty$, then $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.

*Correction: The first handwritten page leaves the entropy integral, the condition on $Q$, and the difference-class definition incomplete. The transcript explicitly supplies the finiteness, finitely discrete probability measures, two measurability conditions, square-integrable envelope, and Donsker conclusion. The completed statement agrees with Theorem 2.5.2, p. 127. The squared class means squares of individual differences, not arbitrary products of two different differences.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The goal is convergence to a tight Borel-measurable random element in the bounded-function space. The professor grouped the theorem's requirements as an entropy condition, two measurability conditions, and an envelope condition. The measure $Q$ in the supremum ranges over finitely supported probability measures; it is not the fixed population measure alone.

The proof must establish asymptotic equicontinuity for every sequence $\delta_n\downarrow0$, as well as total boundedness of the class in $L_2(P)$. The nonbracketing Donsker proof was expected to be easier than the bracketing proof, reversing the relative difficulty of the two Glivenko-Cantelli arguments.

# 2. Symmetrization and the Random Entropy Integral - §2.5.1, Proof of Theorem 2.5.2, pp. 127-128

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

For $x>0$, Markov's inequality and the first symmetrization lemma give

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^\star (\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}>x)
&\le\frac{E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}}{x}\\
&\le\frac2x E_XE_\epsilon
\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For fixed $(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$, combine the previous Rademacher and sub-Gaussian bounds:

$$
E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\le K\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(P_n))}\,d\epsilon.
$$

Here

$$
\|f\|_{L_2(P_n)}=\sqrt{\int f^2\,dP_n}
=\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nf^2(X_i)\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Define

$$
\theta_n=\left(\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\|g\|_{L_2(P_n)}^2\right)^{1/2}
=\left(\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}P_ng^2\right)^{1/2}.
$$

For $\epsilon>\theta_n$, the class fits into one ball centered at zero, so the entropy integrand vanishes. Thus the integral may stop at $\theta_n$.

*Textbook clarification: $\theta_n$ is a radius about zero, not necessarily the diameter; the diameter is at most $2\theta_n$. The first probability bound and the integral in my notes were unfinished and have been completed using the transcript and (2.5.3), p. 128. The assumed measurability permits the conditional-expectation step.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Conditioning on the observations turns the symmetrized process into a Rademacher process with coefficients $f(X_i)/\sqrt n$. Its natural semimetric is the empirical $L_2$ distance. Although the entropy integral is initially written to infinity, only a finite random range contributes: once one ball covers the whole class, the logarithm of its covering number is zero.

To use the theorem's hypothesis, the proof must replace the shrinking difference class by the original class and normalize the radius by the envelope norm. The professor adjusted the order of the calculation during the lecture: first change variables using the empirical envelope norm, then bound by the supremum over $Q$, because $P_n$ is one of the finitely discrete measures allowed in that supremum.

# 3. Covering the Difference Class - §2.5.1, Proof of Theorem 2.5.2, p. 128

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

For every relevant $Q$,

$$
N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(Q))
\le N(\epsilon,\mathcal F_\infty,L_2(Q))
\le N^2(\epsilon/2,\mathcal F,L_2(Q)).
$$

Construct an $\epsilon/2$-net of $\mathcal F$ with centers $g_1,\ldots,g_N$. If $f,g\in\mathcal F$, choose $g_k,g_{k'}$ so that

$$
\int(g_k-f)^2\,dQ<(\epsilon/2)^2,
\qquad
\int(g_{k'}-g)^2\,dQ<(\epsilon/2)^2.
$$

The pairwise differences $h_{k,k'}=g_k-g_{k'}$ give a net for $\mathcal F_\infty$, since

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int[h_{k,k'}-(f-g)]^2\,dQ
&=\int[(g_k-f)-(g_{k'}-g)]^2\,dQ\\
&\le2\int(g_k-f)^2\,dQ+2\int(g_{k'}-g)^2\,dQ\\
&<2(\epsilon/2)^2+2(\epsilon/2)^2=\epsilon^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

There are at most $N^2$ such centers.

*Correction: My notes attempt to split a single center $h_k$ into two halves and finish with “maybe” a sum of half-centers. The transcript also shows this construction being reconsidered. The correct construction uses all **differences** of two centers of an $\epsilon/2$-net of $\mathcal F$. The displayed expansion with two plus signs would approximate $f+g$, not $f-g$. This is the covering-number inequality used in Theorem 2.5.2, p. 128.*

After changing variables and enlarging the constant,

$$
E_\epsilon\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_if(X_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\le K\|F\|_{P_n,2}
\int_0^{\theta_n/\|F\|_{P_n,2}}
\sup_Q\sqrt{\log N(u\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q))}\,du.
$$

It is enough to show $\theta_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }0$.

*Textbook clarification: The upper-limit formula can be obtained with an additional factor 2 in the substitution and then enlarged to the displayed bound. If the empirical envelope norm is zero, the conditional process is zero and division is unnecessary. In the nondegenerate case the normalization stays away from zero asymptotically, and the envelope assumption provides the integrability needed to take expectation of this bound; the textbook uses Cauchy-Schwarz and dominated convergence (p. 128). A nonmeasurable envelope is handled through measurable majorants, rather than asserting an ordinary strong law for it without qualification.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The first covering inequality follows from inclusion of classes. For the second, pair every center of one net with every center of the other. This may not be the smallest possible net, but an upper bound is all that is needed. Squaring the covering number contributes only a constant after taking its logarithm and square root.

The entropy assumption makes the normalized integrand integrable. Once the upper limit tends to zero, the integral becomes small. The task has therefore been reduced from an empirical-process supremum to the random radius $\theta_n$.

# 4. A Glivenko-Cantelli Reduction for Squared Differences - §2.5.1, Proof of Theorem 2.5.2, pp. 128-129

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_n^2
&=\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}P_ng^2\\
&\le\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|(P_n-P)g^2|
+\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}Pg^2\\
&\le\|(P_n-P)g^2\|_{\mathcal F_\infty}+\delta_n^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

It suffices to show that $\mathcal F_\infty^2$ is a Glivenko-Cantelli class. Apply the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem without bracketing. The new envelope is

$$
(2F)^2=4F^2,
$$

which is integrable by assumption. The class is $P$-measurable by assumption.

For $g_1,g_2\in\mathcal F_\infty$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_n|g_1^2-g_2^2|
&=P_n(|g_1-g_2|\,|g_1+g_2|)\\
&\le4P_n(|g_1-g_2|F)\\
&\le4\sqrt{P_n(g_1-g_2)^2}\sqrt{P_nF^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, up to a fixed change of radius,

$$
N(4\epsilon\|F\|_{P_n,2}^2,\mathcal F_\infty^2,L_1(P_n))
\le N(\epsilon\|F\|_{P_n,2},\mathcal F_\infty,L_2(P_n)).
$$

The uniform entropy hypothesis bounds the right side by a fixed finite number at each fixed positive normalized radius. Its logarithm divided by $n$ tends to zero. Therefore the Glivenko-Cantelli theorem applies, giving

$$
\|(P_n-P)g^2\|_{\mathcal F_\infty}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0,
\qquad \theta_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

*Correction: My notes leave the envelope exponent uncertain and put an unsquared envelope norm in the $L_1$ covering radius. The squared-difference class has envelope $4F^2$, and the normalized $L_1$ radius is proportional to $\epsilon\|F\|_{P_n,2}^2$. This follows from the displayed Cauchy-Schwarz bound and matches the proof on pp. 128-129. If covering centers are required to lie in the difference class before squaring them, selecting representatives changes only a fixed radius constant. The integrable envelope makes the empirical normalization bounded in probability, as required to pass from normalized radii to the fixed-radius condition of Theorem 2.4.3.*

The last thing to prove is that $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded in $L_2(P)$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The term involving $Pg^2$ is small directly from the definition of the shrinking difference class. The other term is a uniform law of large numbers for a larger class, namely the squares of all differences. Each difference is bounded by twice the original envelope, so its square is bounded by $4F^2$.

The professor derived the covering comparison by factoring a difference of squares and applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the empirical average. This changes an $L_1$ covering problem for squared differences into an $L_2$ covering problem for differences, which the original entropy condition controls.

The key link is that a uniform law of large numbers for the larger squared-difference class can establish asymptotic equicontinuity for the original empirical process. The total-boundedness step was explicitly deferred to the next lecture; it is not inserted here as if it had already been completed.
