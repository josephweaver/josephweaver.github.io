# 1. The Increment Bound to Be Proved - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, Theorem 2.2.4, p. 98

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Let $\psi(0)=0$, with $\psi$ convex, nondecreasing, and nonzero, and suppose

$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(cxy)}<\infty
\quad\text{for some }c>0.
$$

Let $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ be a separable stochastic process satisfying

$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_\psi\leq C d(s,t),\qquad s,t\in T,
$$

where $d$ is a semimetric on $T$.

Then, for every $\eta,\delta>0$,

$$
\left\|\sup_{d(s,t)\leq\delta}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
\leq K\left[
\int_0^\eta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+\delta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
$$

$K$ depends on $\psi$ and $C$ only.

The integral is labeled “Dudley's integral.”

*Textbook clarification: The nonzero condition on $\psi$ and separability of the process are present in Theorem 2.2.4, p. 98, and in the preceding lecture, but are not repeated in this day's abbreviated handwritten statement. They are retained here.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor explained that the left-hand side is the type of supremum that will arise in asymptotic equicontinuity. Norm control can be converted into probability control by a Markov-type argument. The integral was called Dudley's integral in the lecture, although the textbook also uses the description “covering integral.”

The proof matters as a technique in its own right. It will relate increments on an infinite index set to finitely many increments at successive resolutions, with a balance between their sizes and their number.

# 2. Nested Separated Sets and Chains - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, proof of Theorem 2.2.4, pp. 98-99

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Proof.** Without loss of generality, assume the packing numbers and the integral are finite; otherwise the upper bound is infinite.

Construct a sieve

$$
T_0\subset T_1\subset T_2\subset\cdots\subset T
$$

such that each $T_j$ is a maximal set with

$$
d(s,t)>\frac\eta{2^j}
\qquad\text{for distinct }s,t\in T_j.
$$

“Maximal”: no point can be added.

[The diagram shows a coarser set of black points and a finer set adding red points, while retaining the black points.]

$$
\#T_j\leq D\left(\frac\eta{2^j},d\right).
$$

Link every $t_{j+1}\in T_{j+1}$ to a unique chosen point $t_j\in T_j$ such that

$$
d(t_j,t_{j+1})\leq\frac\eta{2^j}.
$$

**Question.** Why does such $t_j$ exist?

Suppose no such $t_j$ exists. Then

$$
d(t_j,t_{j+1})>\frac\eta{2^j}
\qquad\text{for every }t_j\in T_j.
$$

We could add $t_{j+1}$ to $T_j$, contradicting maximality.

Now link every $t_{k+1}\in T_{k+1}$ back through

$$
t_{k+1},t_k,t_{k-1},\ldots,t_1,t_0,
\qquad t_0\in T_0.
$$

This is a **chain**; the method is called **chaining**.

*Textbook clarification: “Unique” means that one admissible parent is selected and fixed for each point. Maximality gives existence, not uniqueness of the nearby point itself. This is the linking construction in the proof on p. 99.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor distinguished a maximal separated set from an arbitrary separated subset: once it has been built, no additional point can be inserted while preserving the separation requirement. As the scale is halved, the next set can contain more points, but must contain the previous set.

One first builds these increasingly fine sets and then traces links backward to the coarsest set. The powers of two are a convenient way to organize the scales. The contradiction argument ensures that the chains can always be constructed.

# 3. Telescoping and the Covering Integral - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, equation (2.2.6), p. 99

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Take arbitrary $s_{k+1},t_{k+1}\in T_{k+1}$, with corresponding chains ending at $s_0,t_0$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|(X_{s_{k+1}}-X_{s_0})-(X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_0})\right|
&=\left|\sum_{j=0}^k(X_{s_{j+1}}-X_{s_j})
-\sum_{j=0}^k(X_{t_{j+1}}-X_{t_j})\right|\\
&\leq2\sum_{j=0}^k\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal L_j}|X_u-X_v|,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathcal L_j$ consists of the selected links from $T_{j+1}$ to $T_j$.

**Remark.** For every such link,

$$
\|X_u-X_v\|_\psi\leq Cd(u,v)\leq\frac{C\eta}{2^j}.
$$

The number of possible links is at most

$$
\#T_{j+1}\leq D\left(\frac\eta{2^{j+1}},d\right).
$$

Take the maximum over $s,t\in T_{k+1}$ and the Orlicz norm. By the triangle inequality and the finite maximal inequality,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left\|\max_{s,t\in T_{k+1}}
\left|(X_s-X_{s_0})-(X_t-X_{t_0})\right|\right\|_\psi\\
&\quad\leq2\sum_{j=0}^k
\left\|\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal L_j}|X_u-X_v|\right\|_\psi\\
&\quad\leq2K_\psi\sum_{j=0}^k
\psi^{-1}\left(D\left(\frac\eta{2^{j+1}},d\right)\right)
\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal L_j}\|X_u-X_v\|_\psi\\
&\quad\leq2K_\psi C\sum_{j=0}^k
\psi^{-1}\left(D\left(\frac\eta{2^{j+1}},d\right)\right)\frac\eta{2^j}\\
&\quad\leq K_0\int_0^\eta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $K_0$ depends only on $\psi$ and $C$.

*Textbook clarification: The notes write a particular multiple of $KC$ at the integral step, while constants are being renamed during the argument. Equation (2.2.6), p. 99, gives the bound with a constant depending only on $\psi,C$. With $K_\psi$ fixed as in the displayed finite-maximal-inequality step above, one may take $K_0=8K_\psi C$: on each interval $[\eta2^{-j-2},\eta2^{-j-1}]$, the integrand is at least its value at the right endpoint. This identifies the bound without treating the dyadic sum as an exact integral.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The telescoping decomposition inserts all the intermediate links in each chain. This turns a large increment into a sum of smaller increments. At level $j$, the maximal inequality pays for the number of links, while the increment hypothesis bounds the norm of each link by its length.

The professor emphasized that the finite maximal inequality is being used repeatedly, not applied once to an infinite collection. The dyadic sum produces the integral term, but this alone does not finish the theorem because the starting points of the two chains may be different in $T_0$.

# 4. Controlling Chain Endpoints - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, proof of Theorem 2.2.4, pp. 99-100

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

For pairs of endpoints $(s_0,t_0)\in T_0^2$ that arise from pairs $s,t\in T_{k+1}$ with $d(s,t)\leq\delta$, choose one representative pair

$$
(u_{s_0,t_0},v_{s_0,t_0})\in T_{k+1}^2
$$

with those endpoints and

$$
d(u_{s_0,t_0},v_{s_0,t_0})\leq\delta.
$$

The number of such endpoint pairs is at most

$$
(\#T_0)^2\leq D(\eta,d)^2.
$$

*Correction: My notes say “for all $(s_0,t_0)\in T_0^2$” and then “find $k$” and nearby descendants. This is only valid for endpoint pairs that actually occur from nearby starting points, and $k$ is fixed at this stage. The representative-pair construction above follows the proof on p. 99; it does not assert that every two coarse points have arbitrarily close descendants.*

Telescope again:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|X_{s_0}-X_{t_0}|
&\leq\left|(X_{s_0}-X_{u_{s_0,t_0}})
-(X_{t_0}-X_{v_{s_0,t_0}})\right|\\
&\qquad+|X_{u_{s_0,t_0}}-X_{v_{s_0,t_0}}|.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Correction: An intermediate handwritten bound controls the coarse-endpoint increment by the chain-integral term alone. The selected representative increment $X_{u_{s_0,t_0}}-X_{v_{s_0,t_0}}$ must also be retained, as in the decomposition above and the proof on pp. 99-100.*

The first term is controlled by the previous chain bound. For arbitrary nearby $s,t\in T_{k+1}$,

$$
X_s-X_t=(X_s-X_{s_0})-(X_t-X_{t_0})+(X_{s_0}-X_{t_0}).
$$

Combining the two decompositions gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\max_{\substack{s,t\in T_{k+1}\\d(s,t)\leq\delta}}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
&\leq2K_0\int_0^\eta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon\\
&\qquad+\left\|\max_{(s_0,t_0)}
|X_{u_{s_0,t_0}}-X_{v_{s_0,t_0}}|\right\|_\psi.
\end{aligned}
$$

The final maximum contains at most $D(\eta,d)^2$ terms. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\max_{(s_0,t_0)}
|X_{u_{s_0,t_0}}-X_{v_{s_0,t_0}}|\right\|_\psi
&\leq K_\psi\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\max_{(s_0,t_0)}\|X_{u_{s_0,t_0}}-X_{v_{s_0,t_0}}\|_\psi\\
&\leq K_\psi C\delta\,\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

*Correction: The handwritten last bound drops the Orlicz norm from the left-hand side and uses labels that also denote arbitrary descendants. Lemma 2.2.2 bounds the norm of the maximum over the selected representative pairs, not a pointwise random increment. The corrected form is the final maximal-inequality step on p. 100.*

Let $k\to\infty$ and combine constants. This gives

$$
\left\|\sup_{d(s,t)\leq\delta}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
\leq K\left[
\int_0^\eta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+\delta\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
$$

*Textbook clarification: Passing from the finite sets to the process supremum uses the separability assumption retained in the theorem; it is not an assertion that an arbitrary uncountable $T$ is literally the union of the finite sets. See Theorem 2.2.4 and its separability footnote, p. 98, and the concluding passage on p. 100.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The second term in the theorem comes from selecting only one nearby starting pair for each relevant pair of coarse endpoints. The square in the packing number reflects pairs of endpoints. The same finite maximal inequality then bounds the selected increments, each of which has norm at most $C\delta$.

The professor emphasized the separate roles of the two positive parameters. The scale $\eta$ organizes the nested sets and their cardinalities, while $\delta$ controls the closeness of the pairs whose increments are being bounded. Chaining combines the geometry of the index set with the size of the process increments.

This does not eliminate the difficulty of an infinite-dimensional index set. It expresses that difficulty through the packing numbers and the integral. If the integral diverges, the displayed inequality may give no useful finite control. The value of the method is that it makes the relevant complexity explicit.

# 5. The Corollary and a Bound on the Process - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.2, Corollary 2.2.5 and following discussion, pp. 98, 100

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Proof of corollary.** Take

$$
\eta=\operatorname{diam}T.
$$

Then $T_0=\{t_0\}$, so all chains have the same endpoint. Hence

$$
\left\|\sup_{s,t\in T}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
\leq K\int_0^{\operatorname{diam}T}
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

Alternatively, take $\eta=\delta=\operatorname{diam}T$ in the theorem. Then

$$
D(\eta,d)=1,
$$

and the second term can be absorbed into the integral, with a change in the constant.

**Note.** For every $t_0\in T$,

$$
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|\right\|_\psi
\leq\|X_{t_0}\|_\psi
+K\int_0^{\operatorname{diam}T}
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

With a single point in the initial set, the discrepancy between coarse endpoints disappears, which makes the corollary simpler than the general local-increment result. The alternative proof uses the theorem and absorbs its remaining diameter term into the integral.

For the supremum of the process itself, one also needs its behavior at one selected point. Subtracting and adding $X_{t_0}$ leaves an increment term that chaining controls. Depending on the application, this form may be more convenient than the direct increment inequality.

The professor concluded that the repeated telescoping construction, with increasingly fine sets and chains traced back to a coarse set, is the chaining technique that will be used in subsequent arguments.
