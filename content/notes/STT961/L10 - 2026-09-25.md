# 1. Hoeffding's Inequality and Chernoff's Method - §2.2.1, Lemma 2.2.7, pp. 100-101

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall $X\sim N(0,1)$:

$$
P(|X|>x)\le 2e^{-x^2/2},\qquad x>0,
\qquad \|X\|_{\psi_2}=\sqrt{8/3}.
$$

*Correction: My notes say the tail bound holds for every real $x$. The threshold must be nonnegative (the displayed version uses $x>0$); for large negative $x$ the left side is 1 and the asserted upper bound is smaller than 1. This is the positive-threshold tail convention used in §2.2.1, pp. 100-101.*

**Hoeffding's inequality.** Let $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ be constants and $\epsilon_1,\ldots,\epsilon_n$ independent Rademacher random variables. Then

$$
P\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\epsilon_k\right|>x\right)
\le 2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\|a\|^2}\right),\qquad x>0,
$$

where

$$
\|a\|^2=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k^2,
\qquad P(\epsilon_k=1)=P(\epsilon_k=-1)=\frac12.
$$

Consequently, by the lemma on Orlicz norms,

$$
\left\|\sum_{k=1}^n\epsilon_k a_k\right\|_{\psi_2}\le\sqrt6\,\|a\|.
$$

**Proof.** For a Rademacher variable $\epsilon$,

$$
Ee^{\lambda\epsilon}=\frac12 e^\lambda+\frac12 e^{-\lambda}
\le e^{\lambda^2/2}.\tag{A}
$$

For $\lambda>0$, by Markov's inequality and independence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left(\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\epsilon_k>x\right)
&=P\left(e^{\lambda\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\epsilon_k}>e^{\lambda x}\right)\\
&\le\frac{Ee^{\lambda\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\epsilon_k}}{e^{\lambda x}}
=\frac{\prod_{k=1}^n Ee^{\lambda a_k\epsilon_k}}{e^{\lambda x}}\\
&\le \exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\|a\|^2-\lambda x\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Chernoff's bounding method:** Find the smallest upper bound. Put

$$
g(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\|a\|^2-\lambda x,\qquad \lambda>0.
$$

$$
g'(\lambda)=\lambda\|a\|^2-x=0
\iff \lambda^*=\frac{x}{\|a\|^2},
\qquad g''(\lambda)=\|a\|^2>0.
$$

$$
g(\lambda^*)=
\frac{\|a\|^2}{2}\left(\frac{x}{\|a\|^2}\right)^2
-\frac{x}{\|a\|^2}x
=-\frac{x^2}{2\|a\|^2}.
$$

Thus

$$
P\left(\sum a_k\epsilon_k>x\right)\le e^{-x^2/(2\|a\|^2)},
\qquad
P\left(\sum a_k\epsilon_k<-x\right)\le e^{-x^2/(2\|a\|^2)}.
$$

Combine the two tails. When a bound is difficult to obtain directly, prove a whole class of bounds with a tuning parameter and then minimize.

*Textbook clarification: The optimization assumes $\|a\|>0$. If $a=0$, the sum is zero and the conclusion is immediate. Inequality (A) holds for every real argument, allowing coefficients of either sign (Lemma 2.2.7, pp. 100-101).*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The normal tail calculation and its Orlicz norm had already appeared in homework. The aim was to find a broader class with the same useful tail behavior. Rademacher sums provide a second important example.

The professor emphasized **Chernoff's bounding method** as a reusable proof technique: introduce a positive tuning parameter, obtain an entire family of valid bounds, and optimize over that parameter. A carefully tuned bound can supply the delicate probability control that a single rough inequality misses. The lower-tail argument is the same after absorbing a minus sign into the coefficients or the Rademacher variables. One should check the second derivative rather than assume that a stationary point is a minimum.

# 2. Sub-Gaussian Processes - §2.2.1, p. 101

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Definition.** A stochastic process $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ is sub-Gaussian with respect to a semimetric $d$ if

$$
P(|X_s-X_t|>x)\le 2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2d^2(s,t)}\right)
\qquad(s,t\in T,\ x>0).
$$

*Correction: My notes wrote $x<0$ below the definition. Changed this to $x>0$, as in §2.2.1, p. 101.*

**Example 1.** A centered Gaussian process is sub-Gaussian with

$$
d(s,t)=\sigma(X_s-X_t)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_s-X_t)}.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The Gaussian increment must have mean zero for its standard deviation alone to give the displayed tail bound. The example is read in the centered-process setting; an arbitrary nonconstant mean function cannot be omitted from this qualification (§2.2.1, pp. 100-101, normal-tail calculation).*

**Example 2.** The Rademacher process is sub-Gaussian:

$$
X_a=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k\epsilon_k,\qquad a\in\mathbb R^n,
$$

$$
d(a_1,a_2)=\|a_1-a_2\|_2
=\left(\sum_{k=1}^n(a_{1k}-a_{2k})^2\right)^{1/2}.
$$

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The semimetric describes the scale of the increments. For the Gaussian example it comes from the process itself, while for the Rademacher process it is ordinary Euclidean distance between coefficient vectors. These were identified as the two most important examples encountered in the literature. Rademacher processes also arise in statistical learning. The reason to single out sub-Gaussian processes is that their tails fit the preceding Orlicz-norm and chaining theory particularly well.

# 3. The Sub-Gaussian Entropy Integral - §2.2.1, Corollary 2.2.8, pp. 101-102

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Corollary.** Let $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ be a separable sub-Gaussian process. For every $\delta>0$,

$$
E\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_t-X_s|
\le K\int_0^\delta\sqrt{\log D(\epsilon,d)}\,d\epsilon,
$$

for a universal constant $K$. In particular, for every $t_0\in T$,

$$
E\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|
\le E|X_{t_0}|+K\int_0^\infty\sqrt{\log D(\epsilon,d)}\,d\epsilon.
$$

Apply the theorem from the previous lecture with

$$
\psi_2(x)=e^{x^2}-1,\qquad \eta=\delta,
\qquad \psi_2^{-1}(m)=\sqrt{\log(1+m)}.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi_2^{-1}\bigl(D^2(\delta,d)\bigr)
&=\sqrt{\log(1+D^2(\delta,d))}\\
&\le\sqrt{\log\bigl((1+D(\delta,d))^2\bigr)}\\
&=\sqrt2\sqrt{\log(1+D(\delta,d))}
\le C\sqrt{\log D(\delta,d)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Textbook clarification: The last comparison is used where $D\ge2$. The proof handles scales up to the diameter of $T$, where packing numbers are at least 2; beyond the diameter the increment supremum does not grow. The extra endpoint term from the general maximal inequality is absorbed into the integral after enlarging $K$ (Corollary 2.2.8, pp. 101-102). It is not valid to apply $\log(1+D)\le C\log D$ at $D=1$.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor asked where the extra term in the preceding general theorem had gone. The special form of $\psi_2^{-1}$ allows that term to be bounded by an entropy-integral contribution, at the cost of a larger constant. The constant is universal: it does not depend on the particular process or semimetric. Its dependence comes from the fixed Orlicz function and the constants in the increment bound.

# 4. Bernstein's Inequality and an Exponential-Moment Lemma - §2.2.2, Lemma 2.2.9, p. 102

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Bernstein's inequality.** For independent random variables $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ with $|Y_k|\le M$ and $EY_k=0$,

$$
P\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^nY_k\right|>x\right)
\le 2\exp\left\{-\frac{x^2/2}{V+Mx/3}\right\},\qquad x>0,
$$

where $V\ge\operatorname{Var}(\sum_{k=1}^nY_k)$.

**Lemma.** Let $Y$ be a random variable with $EY=0$, $|Y|\le M$, and $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\sigma^2$. Then, for every $\lambda>0$,

$$
Ee^{\lambda Y}
\le\exp\left\{\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M)\right\}.
$$

**Proof.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
Ee^{\lambda Y}
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}EY^k\\
&=1+0+\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\sigma^2
+\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}E[Y^{k-2}Y^2].
\end{aligned}
$$

Bound the final moments by $M^{k-2}\sigma^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Ee^{\lambda Y}
&\le 1+\frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2}
+\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda M)^k}{k!}\\
&=1+\frac{\lambda^2\sigma^2}{2}
+\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M-\frac{\lambda^2M^2}{2}\right)\\
&=1+\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M)\\
&\le\exp\left\{\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M)\right\},
\end{aligned}
$$

using $1+u\le e^u$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Sub-Gaussian bounds are not always available. Bernstein's inequality supplies a different form, with an additional term linear in the threshold in the denominator. It has Gaussian behavior at small thresholds and exponential behavior for large thresholds.

The proof follows the same structure as the Hoeffding argument: first establish a useful exponential-moment bound for one variable, then use independence and optimize. Boundedness provides every moment and justifies the exponential series. Expanding the series and bounding the higher powers by $M^{k-2}Y^2$ reduces them all to the variance. The final use of $1+u\le e^u$ prepares the expression for multiplication over independent variables.

# 5. Proof of Bernstein's Inequality - §2.2.2, Lemma 2.2.9, p. 102

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Put $S=\sum_{k=1}^n\sigma_k^2$, where $\sigma_k^2=\operatorname{Var}(Y_k)$. By Markov's inequality, independence, and the lemma,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left(\sum_{k=1}^nY_k>x\right)
&=P\left(e^{\lambda\sum Y_k}>e^{\lambda x}\right)\\
&\le e^{-\lambda x}Ee^{\lambda\sum Y_k}
=e^{-\lambda x}\prod_{k=1}^nEe^{\lambda Y_k}\\
&\le\exp\left\{-\lambda x+\frac{S}{M^2}(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M)\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Call the exponent $g(\lambda)$. Chernoff's bounding method gives

$$
g'(\lambda)=-x+\frac{S}{M^2}(Me^{\lambda M}-M),
\qquad g''(\lambda)=Se^{\lambda M}>0.
$$

$$
\frac{S}{M}(e^{\lambda M}-1)=x,
\qquad e^{\lambda M}=1+\frac{xM}{S},
\qquad \lambda^*=\frac1M\log\left(1+\frac{xM}{S}\right).
$$

Plugging back in,

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(\lambda^*)
&=-\frac{x}{M}\log\left(1+\frac{xM}{S}\right)
+\frac{S}{M^2}\left[\frac{xM}{S}-\log\left(1+\frac{xM}{S}\right)\right]\\
&=\frac{x}{M}-\left(\frac{x}{M}+\frac{S}{M^2}\right)
\log\left(1+\frac{xM}{S}\right)\\
&\le -\frac{x^2/2}{S+Mx/3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

To verify the last inequality, bring terms to one side:

$$
\frac{x}{M}+\frac{x^2/2}{S+Mx/3}
-\left(\frac{x}{M}+\frac{S}{M^2}\right)
\log\left(1+\frac{xM}{S}\right)\le0.
$$

Set $u=xM/S$, and write the expression as a positive constant times $h(u)$. Show $h(0)=h'(0)=0$ and $h''(u)<0$ for $u>0$. Then $h'$ decreases, and $h(u)\le0$.

*Textbook clarification: These divisions assume $M>0$ and $S>0$; if the total variance is zero, the centered sum is zero almost surely. Repeating the upper-tail argument for $-Y_k$ and adding the two tails gives the factor 2, and replacing $S$ by any $V\ge S$ weakens the bound to the statement of Lemma 2.2.9 (p. 102). The textbook states the lemma and refers elsewhere for its proof; the detailed moment and optimization calculation above is retained from the lecture.*
