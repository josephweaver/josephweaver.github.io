## 1. What Is a $P$-Measurable Class?

Before stating the next Glivenko–Cantelli theorem, the
professor introduces a measurability condition on the
function class.

### Individual measurability is not enough

Suppose every function $f\in\mathcal F$ is measurable.

For each fixed $f$, the weighted sum

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n e_i f(X_i)
$$

is then measurable.

However, the supremum

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|\sum_{i=1}^n e_i f(X_i)\right|
$$

need not be measurable when $\mathcal F$ is uncountable.

Thus we distinguish between measurability of each function
and measurability of the supremum over the class.

### Definition: $P$-measurable class

Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions
$f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$, where
$(\mathcal X,\mathcal A,P)$ is a probability space.

The class is **$P$-measurable** if, for every $n\geq1$
and every deterministic vector

$$
(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\in\mathbb R^n,
$$

the map

$$
\boxed{
(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
\longmapsto
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|\sum_{i=1}^n e_i f(x_i)\right|
}
$$

is measurable on the completion of

$$
(\mathcal X^n,\mathcal A^{\otimes n},P^{\otimes n}).
$$

Equivalently, the supremum above is

$$
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n e_i f(x_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The weights are arbitrary real numbers, not only
Rademacher signs.

### What does “completion” mean?

The completed probability space includes all subsets
of measurable sets having probability zero.

This permits measurability with respect to the completed
sigma-field, even when measurability with respect to the
original sigma-field is unavailable.

### Why this condition appears here

The upcoming proof uses the symmetrized supremum

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
\right|.
$$

We want to condition on the observations, apply bounds
over the signs, and then integrate over the observations.

Because the signs take finitely many possible vectors,
$P$-measurability supplies the measurability needed for
this interchange of expectations.

Outer expectations alone do not generally justify Fubini's
theorem. This class condition addresses that obstacle.

### A useful sufficient condition: countable approximation

Suppose $\mathcal F$ contains a countable subclass
$\mathcal G$ such that every $f\in\mathcal F$ is the
pointwise limit of a sequence from $\mathcal G$.

For every fixed sample and weight vector,

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|\sum_i e_i f(x_i)\right|
=
\sup_{g\in\mathcal G}
\left|\sum_i e_i g(x_i)\right|.
$$

The right side is a countable supremum of measurable
functions, so it is measurable.

Thus pointwise approximation by a countable subclass
is a convenient way to establish $P$-measurability.

### Check your understanding

What extra requirement does $P$-measurability impose?

It requires the supremum of every finite weighted sum
over the entire class to be measurable, beyond the
measurability of each individual function.


## 2. The Glivenko–Cantelli Theorem Without Bracketing

The second theorem uses covering numbers measured under
the empirical distribution $P_n$.

### Setup: an integrable envelope

Let $\mathcal F$ be a $P$-measurable class of measurable
functions with a nonnegative envelope $F$:

$$
|f(x)|\leq F(x)
\qquad
\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

Assume

$$
P^\star F<\infty.
$$

If the envelope is measurable, this is simply

$$
PF=E[F(X)]<\infty.
$$

Integrability allows us to control the contribution from
regions where the envelope is large.

### Truncate the entire class using the same envelope

For $M>0$, define

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal F_M
=
\{f\,\mathbf1\{F\leq M\}:f\in\mathcal F\}.
}
$$

Every function $g\in\mathcal F_M$ satisfies

$$
|g(x)|\leq M.
$$

This truncation sets all functions to zero wherever
the envelope exceeds $M$. It does not clip each
function separately at height $M$.

Using the same envelope gives a common tail region
for the whole class.

### The empirical $L_1$ distance

For two functions $g,h$, define

$$
\|g-h\|_{P_n,1}
=
P_n|g-h|
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n|g(X_i)-h(X_i)|.
$$

The covering number

$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))
$$

counts how many representative functions are needed
to approximate the truncated class at accuracy
$\varepsilon$ on the observed sample.

It is random because the distance depends on the data.

### Statement of Theorem 2.4.3

If, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and $M>0$,

$$
\boxed{
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))
=
o_{P^\star }(n),
}
$$

then

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}^{*},
$$

and

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0.
$$

In particular, $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Glivenko–Cantelli.

### What does $o_{P^\star }(n)$ mean?

Your handwritten notes ask this explicitly.

Write

$$
H_n=\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n)).
$$

The condition means

$$
\boxed{
\frac{H_n}{n}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
}
$$

Equivalently, for every $\eta>0$,

$$
P^\star\left(\frac{H_n}{n}>\eta\right)\longrightarrow0.
$$

The star allows for possible nonmeasurability of the
random covering number.

The entropy may increase with sample size, but its
growth must be negligible compared with $n$.

### Examples of allowable growth

For fixed $\varepsilon$ and $M$:

- If $N_n\leq n^k$ for a fixed $k$, then

  $$
  \frac{\log N_n}{n}
  \leq\frac{k\log n}{n}\longrightarrow0.
  $$

- If $N_n\leq e^{\sqrt n}$, then

  $$
  \frac{\log N_n}{n}
  \leq\frac1{\sqrt n}\longrightarrow0.
  $$

- If $N_n=e^{cn}$ for a fixed $c>0$, then

  $$
  \frac{\log N_n}{n}=c,
  $$

  so the entropy condition fails.

Thus the number of representatives can be very large.
What matters is the logarithm of that number.

### Why this particular growth condition?

The proof will produce a bound of the form

$$
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_M}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)
\right|
\leq
\varepsilon
+
CM\sqrt{
\frac{1+\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))}{n}
}.
$$

For fixed $\varepsilon$ and $M$, the entropy assumption
makes the second term converge to zero in outer probability.

We will then let the approximation error $\varepsilon$
decrease and remove the truncation.

### Check your understanding

Does the theorem require the covering number itself
to remain bounded?

No. It requires its logarithm, divided by $n$,
to converge to zero in outer probability.

## 3. Proof, Part I: Symmetrization and Truncation

We first prove convergence in mean:

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0.
$$

For clarity, the displayed argument uses a measurable
integrable envelope $F$. The general formulation uses
outer expectations and measurable majorants.

### Step 1: Apply symmetrization

Let $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$ be independent
Rademacher signs, independent of the observations.

The first symmetrization lemma gives

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E_{X,\varepsilon}
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
\right|.
$$

The class measurability condition permits us to write
the right-hand expectation as

$$
2E_XE_\varepsilon
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
\right|.
$$

Here $E_\varepsilon$ averages over the signs with
the observations held fixed.

### Step 2: Split each function into bounded and tail parts

Fix $M>0$. For every $f$,

$$
f
=
f\mathbf1\{F\leq M\}
+
f\mathbf1\{F>M\}.
$$

The first part belongs to $\mathcal F_M$.

Using the triangle inequality and taking suprema,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
\right|\\
&\quad\leq
\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_M}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)
\right|
+
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i
\varepsilon_i f(X_i)\mathbf1\{F(X_i)>M\}
\right|.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 3: Bound the tail uniformly using the envelope

Since $|\varepsilon_i|=1$ and $|f(X_i)|\leq F(X_i)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i
\varepsilon_i f(X_i)\mathbf1\{F(X_i)>M\}
\right|\\
&\qquad\leq
\frac1n\sum_i F(X_i)\mathbf1\{F(X_i)>M\}\\
&\qquad=
P_n\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$

This upper bound no longer depends on $f$ or the signs.

Taking expectations gives

$$
E_XP_n\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr)
=
P\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr).
$$

### Step 4: Combine the bounds

Define the conditional expected supremum

$$
A_{n,M}(X)
=
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_M}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)
\right|.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E_XA_{n,M}
+
2P\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr).
}
$$

The two terms have different roles:

- $A_{n,M}$ measures the complexity of the bounded class.
- The envelope tail measures the error from truncation.

### Step 5: Use integrability to remove the tail

Because $PF<\infty$,

$$
F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\longrightarrow0
\qquad P\text{-almost surely},
$$

and

$$
0\leq F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\leq F.
$$

Dominated convergence therefore gives

$$
P\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr)\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }M\to\infty.
$$

It remains to show that, for each fixed $M$,

$$
E_XA_{n,M}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.
$$

### The order of limits matters

The professor explicitly emphasized:

1. Hold $M$ fixed and let $n\to\infty$.
2. Then let $M\to\infty$.

The entropy assumption is stated for each fixed $M$.
It does not automatically justify choosing a growing
sequence $M=M_n$.

### Check your understanding

Why use an envelope rather than bound each function's
tail separately?

The envelope provides one bound that works simultaneously
for every function in the class.




## 4. Proof, Part II: Approximate by a Finite Empirical Cover

We now bound

$$
A_{n,M}(X)
=
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_M}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)
\right|.
$$

Fix the observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$.
The functions' sample values are now fixed coefficients;
only the Rademacher signs remain random.

### Step 1: Choose an empirical $L_1$ cover

Fix $\varepsilon>0$. Choose a finite collection
$\mathcal G$ of representative functions such that

$$
\forall g\in\mathcal F_M,\quad
\exists h\in\mathcal G:
P_n|g-h|<\varepsilon.
$$

Thus $\mathcal G$ is an $\varepsilon$-net under

$$
\|g-h\|_{P_n,1}
=
\frac1n\sum_i|g(X_i)-h(X_i)|.
$$

We may use centers bounded by $M$. If an ambient center
is not bounded, clipping it to $[-M,M]$ cannot increase
its distance from a function in $\mathcal F_M$.

Write

$$
N_n=N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n)).
$$

For this fixed sample, the bounded class has a finite
cover: its sample-value vectors lie in $[-M,M]^n$.

### Step 2: Add and subtract a representative

For $g\in\mathcal F_M$, choose an approximating $h\in\mathcal G$.
Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)\right|
&\leq
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i)\right|
+
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i[g(X_i)-h(X_i)]\right|\\
&\leq
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i)\right|
+
P_n|g-h|\\
&<
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i)\right|
+\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second inequality uses $|\varepsilon_i|=1$.

Taking the supremum gives

$$
\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_M}
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)\right|
\leq
\max_{h\in\mathcal G}
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i)\right|
+\varepsilon.
$$

### Step 3: Take expectation over the signs

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
A_{n,M}(X)
\leq
E_\varepsilon
\max_{h\in\mathcal G}
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i)\right|
+\varepsilon.
}
$$

We have replaced a supremum over the whole class by
a maximum over finitely many representatives, plus
a controlled approximation error.

### Step 4: Apply the maximal Orlicz inequality

For each representative, define

$$
Y_h=\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i).
$$

The $\psi_2$ maximal inequality, together with the
bound of the $L_1$ norm by the $\psi_2$ norm, gives

$$
E_\varepsilon\max_{h\in\mathcal G}|Y_h|
\leq
C\sqrt{\log(1+|\mathcal G|)}
\max_{h\in\mathcal G}\|Y_h\|_{\psi_2\mid X}.
$$

Here $\|\cdot\|_{\psi_2\mid X}$ is computed over the
signs with the observations fixed.

Using a cover of size $N_n$, and absorbing equivalent
logarithmic factors into the constant,

$$
\boxed{
A_{n,M}(X)
\leq
\varepsilon
+
C\sqrt{1+\log N_n}
\max_{h\in\mathcal G}\|Y_h\|_{\psi_2\mid X}.
}
$$

### Why use an $L_1(P_n)$ cover here?

The random signs give the direct bound

$$
\left|\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i(g-h)(X_i)\right|
\leq P_n|g-h|.
$$

Thus empirical $L_1$ approximation controls the
randomized approximation error for every sign vector.

The sub-Gaussian bound used next involves $P_nh^2$.
These two norms perform different jobs in the proof.

### Check your understanding

Does the finite cover have to be chosen independently
of the observations?

No. We choose it after fixing the sample.

For this conditional bound, the cover is only a proof
device; we do not need a measurable rule selecting its
centers as the sample varies.

## 5. Proof, Part III: Hoeffding Controls Each Representative

For a fixed sample and a representative $h$, recall

$$
Y_h=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i h(X_i).
$$

We will bound its conditional $\psi_2$ norm and substitute
that bound into the finite-maximum inequality.

### Step 1: Identify the Rademacher coefficients

Write

$$
a_i=\frac{h(X_i)}{n}.
$$

Then

$$
Y_h=\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_i.
$$

Conditionally on the observations, the coefficients
$a_i$ are fixed.

The Rademacher version of Hoeffding's inequality gives

$$
P_\varepsilon(|Y_h|>t\mid X)
\leq
2\exp\left\{
-\frac{t^2}{2\sum_i a_i^2}
\right\},
\qquad t>0,
$$

when $\sum_i a_i^2>0$. If all coefficients are zero,
then $Y_h=0$.

### Step 2: Convert the tail bound to an Orlicz bound

Recall the tail-to-Orlicz implication

$$
P(|Y|>t)\leq 2e^{-t^2/(2s^2)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\|Y\|_{\psi_2}\leq\sqrt6\,s.
$$

Applying it conditionally,

$$
\|Y_h\|_{\psi_2\mid X}
\leq
\sqrt6\left(\sum_i a_i^2\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Substituting the coefficients gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i a_i^2
&=
\frac1{n^2}\sum_i h(X_i)^2\\
&=
\frac1nP_nh^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\|Y_h\|_{\psi_2\mid X}
\leq
\sqrt{\frac6n}\,(P_nh^2)^{1/2}.
}
$$

### Step 3: Use the truncation bound

The representatives can be chosen bounded by $M$, so

$$
P_nh^2\leq M^2.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
\max_{h\in\mathcal G}\|Y_h\|_{\psi_2\mid X}
\leq
M\sqrt{\frac6n}.
}
$$

Each randomized average therefore has a sub-Gaussian
scale of order $M/\sqrt n$.

### Step 4: Substitute into the maximal inequality

From the previous section,

$$
A_{n,M}(X)
\leq
\varepsilon
+
C\sqrt{1+\log N_n}
\max_{h\in\mathcal G}\|Y_h\|_{\psi_2\mid X},
$$

where

$$
N_n=N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n)).
$$

Absorbing the numerical factor $\sqrt6$ into $C$,

$$
\boxed{
A_{n,M}(X)
\leq
\varepsilon
+
CM\sqrt{
\frac{1+\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))}{n}
}.
}
$$

This is the main conditional bound in the proof.

### Step 5: Apply the entropy assumption

For fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and $M>0$, the hypothesis gives

$$
\frac{\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))}{n}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Since $1/n\to0$, the second term in the bound converges
to zero in outer probability.

Thus, for every $\eta>0$,

$$
P^\star\bigl(A_{n,M}>\varepsilon+\eta\bigr)\longrightarrow0.
$$

Because this holds for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$,
we conclude

$$
\boxed{
A_{n,M}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
\qquad
\text{for each fixed }M.
}
$$

For example, to control $P^\star (A_{n,M}>t)$, choose
$\varepsilon=t/2$ and use the vanishing second term.

### A clarification of the handwritten limit

The bound does not show that $A_{n,M}$ converges to
$\varepsilon$. It shows that its excess above
$\varepsilon$ vanishes in probability.

Allowing $\varepsilon$ to be arbitrarily small gives
convergence to zero.

### Check your understanding

How do sample size and class complexity interact?

The bound combines

$$
\underbrace{\frac{M}{\sqrt n}}_{\text{scale of each average}}
\quad\text{with}\quad
\underbrace{\sqrt{1+\log N_n}}_{\text{cost of the maximum}}.
$$

The entropy condition ensures that their product
converges to zero for fixed $M$.



## 6. Completing the Proof: Convergence in Mean and Almost Surely

For each fixed $M$, we have shown that

$$
A_{n,M}
=
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{g\in\mathcal F_M}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)
\right|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

We now convert this into convergence of expectations.

### Step 1: Obtain a deterministic bound

Every $g\in\mathcal F_M$ satisfies $|g|\leq M$.
Therefore, for every sample and sign vector,

$$
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i g(X_i)
\right|
\leq
\frac1n\sum_i|g(X_i)|
\leq M.
$$

Taking the supremum and averaging over the signs gives

$$
\boxed{
0\leq A_{n,M}\leq M.
}
$$

This bound prevents rare, large values from contributing
substantially to the expectation.

### Step 2: Convert probability convergence to mean convergence

For any $\eta>0$,

$$
A_{n,M}
\leq
\eta+M\mathbf1\{A_{n,M}>\eta\}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
E_X^\star A_{n,M}
\leq
\eta+M P^\star (A_{n,M}>\eta).
$$

For fixed $M$, convergence in outer probability gives

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_X^\star A_{n,M}\leq\eta.
$$

Since $\eta>0$ is arbitrary,

$$
\boxed{
E_X^\star A_{n,M}\longrightarrow0
\qquad
\text{for every fixed }M.
}
$$

This is the bounded-convergence-in-probability argument
behind the lecture's dominated-convergence step.

### Step 3: Return to the original class

Recall the truncation bound:

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E_X^\star A_{n,M}
+
2P^\star\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr).
$$

First hold $M$ fixed and let $n\to\infty$. Then

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2P^\star\bigl(F\mathbf1\{F>M\}\bigr).
$$

Now let $M\to\infty$. Integrability of the envelope
makes the right side tend to zero.

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0.
}
$$

We have proved convergence in mean.

### Step 4: What establishes almost-sure convergence?

Convergence in mean alone does not generally imply
almost-sure convergence of the full sequence.

The textbook supplies an additional argument through
Lemma 2.4.5. On the canonical infinite product space,
suitable measurable versions of

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}^{*}
$$

form a reverse submartingale with respect to a decreasing
filtration based on permutation-symmetric functions.

The lemma establishes almost-sure convergence. Combined
with the mean convergence just proved, its limit must
be zero.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}^{*}.
}
$$

The professor referred to this additional lemma rather
than proving it during the lecture.

### Why the almost-sure limit must be zero

If the nonnegative measurable versions converge almost
surely to $L$, Fatou's lemma gives

$$
EL
\leq
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
0.
$$

Hence $L=0$ almost surely.

### Proof pattern to remember

1. Symmetrize the empirical error.
2. Truncate using an integrable envelope.
3. Approximate the bounded class by an empirical $L_1$ cover.
4. Apply a maximal inequality and Hoeffding's inequality.
5. Use the entropy condition and boundedness to get mean convergence.
6. Remove the truncation.
7. Invoke the reverse-submartingale lemma for almost-sure convergence.

### Check your understanding

Why must $M$ remain fixed when converting probability
convergence into mean convergence?

The bound $A_{n,M}\leq M$ then provides a common finite
bound. A growing $M=M_n$ would require additional control.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}^{*}.
}
$$

The professor referred to this additional lemma rather
than proving it during the lecture.

### Why the almost-sure limit must be zero

If the nonnegative measurable versions converge almost
surely to $L$, Fatou's lemma gives

$$
EL
\leq
\liminf_{n\to\infty}
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
0.
$$

Hence $L=0$ almost surely.

### Proof pattern to remember

1. Symmetrize the empirical error.
2. Truncate using an integrable envelope.
3. Approximate the bounded class by an empirical $L_1$ cover.
4. Apply a maximal inequality and Hoeffding's inequality.
5. Use the entropy condition and boundedness to get mean convergence.
6. Remove the truncation.
7. Invoke the reverse-submartingale lemma for almost-sure convergence.

### Check your understanding

Why must $M$ remain fixed when converting probability
convergence into mean convergence?

The bound $A_{n,M}\leq M$ then provides a common finite
bound. A growing $M=M_n$ would require additional control.



## 8. Comparing the Two Glivenko–Cantelli Theorems

Both theorems establish uniform convergence of empirical
averages, but they control the function class differently.

| Feature | With bracketing | Without bracketing |
|---|---|---|
| Approximation | Pointwise lower and upper functions | Nearby representative functions |
| Distance | Population $L_1(P)$ | Empirical $L_1(P_n)$ |
| Complexity condition | Finite bracketing numbers at every accuracy | Empirical log covering numbers are $o_{P^\star }(n)$ |
| Class used | $\mathcal F$ | Truncated class $\mathcal F_M$ |
| Main proof tools | Finite approximation and endpoint strong laws | Symmetrization, maximal inequality, and Hoeffding |
| Additional assumptions | Integrable bracket endpoints | $P$-measurability and an integrable envelope |

### Population versus empirical approximation

The bracketing condition is

$$
N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P))<\infty
\qquad
\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
$$

It measures approximation under the true distribution.

The empirical entropy condition is

$$
\frac{
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F_M,L_1(P_n))
}{n}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
$$

for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$ and $M>0$.

Its geometry depends on the observations.

The professor emphasized that some classes are easier
to handle with brackets, while others are easier to
handle through empirical covering numbers.

These are different proof routes. We should not infer
logical non-implication merely from their different forms.

### An empirical condition still requires an asymptotic proof

Although $L_1(P_n)$ is based on data, checking a covering
number for one observed sample does not establish the theorem.

We must show that the entropy condition holds as
$n\to\infty$, under sampling from $P$.

In the threshold-indicator example, the deterministic bound

$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P_n))\leq n+1
$$

does this for every possible sample.

## 9. Closing Preview: Donsker Theorems and Statistical Applications

The lecture concludes by introducing the next chapter.
The Donsker theorems themselves were left for the next class.

### Uniform LLN versus process CLT

The Glivenko–Cantelli property gives

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The Donsker property describes the scaled fluctuations:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb G_n=\sqrt n\,(P_n-P)
\rightsquigarrow
\mathbb G_P
\qquad
\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
}
$$

where $\mathbb G_P$ is a tight, centered Gaussian process.

Its covariance is

$$
\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(\mathbb G_Pf,\mathbb G_Pg\bigr)
=
P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
$$

The next chapter develops Donsker criteria using both
bracketing and covering-number methods.

### From process convergence to statistic convergence

Suppose a statistic can be written as

$$
T_n=\Psi(\mathbb G_n).
$$

If $\Psi$ is continuous at the limiting process
with probability one, the continuous mapping theorem gives

$$
\boxed{
T_n\rightsquigarrow\Psi(\mathbb G_P).
}
$$

One process convergence result can therefore provide
limiting distributions for many statistics constructed
from that process.

For example, a supremum statistic corresponds to

$$
\Psi(h)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|h(f)|.
$$

### Why the limiting distribution may remain difficult

Even when $\mathbb G_P$ is Gaussian, its covariance
usually depends on the unknown distribution $P$.

Also, a nonlinear functional such as
$\sup_f|\mathbb G_Pf|$ need not have a Gaussian distribution.

The professor explained that this motivates bootstrap
methods: we want to approximate the limiting law using
the observed data.

Bootstrap validity requires additional results; the
Donsker property alone is not a complete bootstrap proof.

### Check your understanding

If the limiting process is Gaussian, must every statistic
formed from it have a Gaussian limit?

No. Nonlinear functionals of Gaussian processes generally
have other distributions.
