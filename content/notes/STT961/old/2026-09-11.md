
The September 11 lecture develops the framework for convergence of entire random functions, especially toward Gaussian processes.    
A process supplies a useful distance between its indices through its random increments. Total boundedness gives finite approximations of the index set, while asymptotic equicontinuity controls the gaps between them. Together with finite-dimensional convergence, these establish weak convergence in $\ell^\infty(T)$.
The lecture also extends familiar modes of convergence to potentially nonmeasurable maps using outer probability and measurable covers. It introduces practical tools: continuous transformations preserve convergence under suitable conditions, negligible approximation errors preserve weak limits, and Skorokhod representation constructs copies on a new probability space with stronger convergence.
The central distinction is between convergence of distributions and closeness of realized random objects—weak convergence guarantees the former, but generally not the latter.

## 1. Semimetrics and Uniform Continuity in the pth Mean

Let $X=\{X(t):t\in T\}$ be a stochastic process. Each $t$ identifies
a random variable $X(t)$, while each outcome $\omega$ identifies a
sample path $t\mapsto X(t,\omega)$.

There are two different spaces to keep track of:

- $T$: the set indexing the process.
- $\ell^\infty(T)$: the space of bounded real-valued functions on $T$.

A semimetric $\rho(s,t)$ measures distance between indices.
The supremum norm measures distance between entire bounded paths:

$$
\|x-y\|_\infty=\sup_{t\in T}|x(t)-y(t)|.
$$

### A semimetric built from the process

For $p>0$, define

$$
\rho_p(s,t)
=
\left(E|X(s)-X(t)|^p\right)^{1/(p\vee 1)},
$$

where $p\vee 1=\max\{p,1\}$. We assume these expectations are finite.

Thus,

$$
\rho_p(s,t)=
\begin{cases}
\left(E|X(s)-X(t)|^p\right)^{1/p}, & p\geq 1,\\
E|X(s)-X(t)|^p, & 0<p<1.
\end{cases}
$$

This measures how different the random variables $X(s)$ and $X(t)$
are, rather than how far apart their labels are geometrically.

It is a semimetric because distinct indices can have zero distance:

$$
\rho_p(s,t)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X(s)=X(t)\text{ almost surely}.
$$

The indices $s$ and $t$ need not be equal.

### Uniform continuity in the pth mean

The process is uniformly $\rho$-continuous in the pth mean if,
for every pair of sequences $s_n,t_n\in T$,

$$
\rho(s_n,t_n)\to 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E|X(s_n)-X(t_n)|^p\to 0.
$$

Equivalently: for every $\epsilon>0$, there is a $\delta>0$ such that

$$
\rho(s,t)<\delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E|X(s)-X(t)|^p<\epsilon
$$

for all $s,t\in T$.

The word **uniformly** means that the same $\delta$ works throughout
$T$, rather than depending on a particular index.

### Mean continuity versus sample-path continuity

Mean continuity controls an expectation:

$$
E|X(s)-X(t)|^p.
$$

Sample-path continuity controls the realized function for a fixed
outcome $\omega$:

$$
|X(s,\omega)-X(t,\omega)|.
$$

These are different properties. A statement about expected increments
does not, by itself, establish continuity of almost every sample path.

With respect to its own semimetric $\rho_p$, the process is automatically
uniformly continuous in the pth mean: this follows directly from the
definition of $\rho_p$.

However, total boundedness of $(T,\rho_p)$ and continuity of sample paths
still require additional justification.

## 2. Tightness Gives a Useful Geometry on the Index Set

Let $X$ be a tight, Borel measurable random element of $\ell^\infty(T)$.

The lecture’s lemma says that there exists a semimetric $\rho$ on $T$
such that:

1. $(T,\rho)$ is totally bounded.
2. Almost every sample path of $X$ is uniformly $\rho$-continuous.

The important idea is that the process can supply a useful geometry
on its index set. We do not necessarily use the usual distance between
the labels in $T$.

### What does total boundedness mean?

For every $\delta>0$, finitely many $\rho$-balls of radius $\delta$
cover $T$.

Equivalently, there exist $t_1,\ldots,t_m\in T$ such that

$$
T=\bigcup_{j=1}^m B_\rho(t_j,\delta),
$$

where

$$
B_\rho(t_j,\delta)=\{t\in T:\rho(t,t_j)<\delta\}.
$$

Thus, at any fixed resolution, finitely many representative indices
are enough to approximate every index in $T$.

The number of representatives may increase as $\delta$ decreases.

**Total boundedness is stronger than boundedness.** A bounded set
need not admit a finite cover by arbitrarily small balls.

### What does uniform sample-path continuity mean?

There is a measurable set $A$ with $P(A)=1$ such that, for every
$\omega\in A$ and every $\epsilon>0$, there exists
$\delta=\delta(\omega,\epsilon)>0$ satisfying

$$
\rho(s,t)<\delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|X(s,\omega)-X(t,\omega)|<\epsilon
$$

for all $s,t\in T$.

The same $\delta$ works throughout $T$ for that particular path.
It may depend on the realized outcome $\omega$.

### Why do these two properties work well together?

Fix a sample path for which uniform continuity holds.

Given $\epsilon>0$, choose a suitable $\delta$ and a finite
$\delta$-cover with centers $t_1,\ldots,t_m$.

For every $t\in T$, some center $t_j$ satisfies

$$
\rho(t,t_j)<\delta,
$$

and therefore

$$
|X(t,\omega)-X(t_j,\omega)|<\epsilon.
$$

So finitely many coordinate values approximate the entire path
uniformly.

This explains why finite-dimensional distributions become useful:
they describe the process at finitely many representative indices.
Continuity controls what happens between those indices.

### Where does tightness enter?

Tightness means that, for every $\eta>0$, there is a compact set
$K_\eta\subseteq\ell^\infty(T)$ such that

$$
P(X\in K_\eta)\geq 1-\eta.
$$

Here, a compact set is a collection of entire functions, with distance
measured by the supremum norm.

The lemma connects this compactness in the space of paths to total
boundedness and continuity on the index set.

Keep the two levels separate:

- Tightness concerns the distribution of entire paths in
  $\ell^\infty(T)$.
- Total boundedness concerns the indices in $(T,\rho)$.

### Connection to weak convergence

Convergence of finite-dimensional distributions only controls
finitely many coordinates at a time.

To obtain weak convergence in $\ell^\infty(T)$, we also need control
over the entire process. Total boundedness and asymptotic
equicontinuity provide a way to obtain that control.

Continuity of the limiting process alone does not establish
equicontinuity of the approximating processes.

## 3. Gaussian Processes: Joint Normality, Mean, and Covariance

A stochastic process $X=\{X(t):t\in T\}$ is a **Gaussian process**
if every finite vector of its coordinates has a multivariate
normal distribution.

Specifically, for every $k\geq 1$ and every $t_1,\ldots,t_k\in T$,

$$
\bigl(X(t_1),\ldots,X(t_k)\bigr)
$$

has a multivariate normal distribution. Degenerate normal
distributions are allowed.

The index set $T$ need not represent time. It can index locations,
functions, or other objects.

### Individual normality is not enough

Requiring each $X(t)$ to be normally distributed only describes
the one-dimensional marginals.

A Gaussian process requires **joint normality** at every finite
collection of indices.

Equivalently, every finite linear combination

$$
\sum_{j=1}^k a_jX(t_j)
$$

must be normally distributed for every choice of real coefficients
$a_1,\ldots,a_k$.

### Mean and covariance functions

Define the mean function

$$
m(t)=E[X(t)]
$$

and the covariance function

$$
K(s,t)
=
\operatorname{Cov}(X(s),X(t)).
$$

For a finite collection $t_1,\ldots,t_k$, these determine the
mean vector and covariance matrix:

$$
\boldsymbol m=
\begin{pmatrix}
m(t_1)\\
\vdots\\
m(t_k)
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma_{ij}=K(t_i,t_j).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\bigl(X(t_1),\ldots,X(t_k)\bigr)
\sim N_k(\boldsymbol m,\Sigma).
$$

Because a multivariate normal distribution is determined by its
mean and covariance, $m$ and $K$ determine all finite-dimensional
distributions of a Gaussian process.

This does not automatically establish bounded or continuous
sample paths.

### The process-induced semimetric

For $p=2$, the semimetric from the previous section is

$$
\rho_2(s,t)
=
\left(E|X(s)-X(t)|^2\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Using the identity $E[Z^2]=\operatorname{Var}(Z)+(EZ)^2$ gives

$$
\rho_2(s,t)^2
=
K(s,s)+K(t,t)-2K(s,t)
+
\bigl(m(s)-m(t)\bigr)^2.
$$

If the process is centered, meaning $m(t)=0$ for every $t$, this
simplifies to

$$
\rho_2(s,t)^2
=
K(s,s)+K(t,t)-2K(s,t).
$$

Thus the covariance structure supplies a geometry on $T$:
two indices are close when their process values have a small
mean-square difference.

### Example: a random straight line

Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and define

$$
X(t)=tZ,\qquad t\in[0,1].
$$

Every finite vector is

$$
\bigl(X(t_1),\ldots,X(t_k)\bigr)
=
Z(t_1,\ldots,t_k),
$$

so the process is Gaussian, although its finite covariance
matrices can be singular.

Its mean and covariance are

$$
m(t)=0,
\qquad
K(s,t)=st.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\rho_2(s,t)
=
\sqrt{s^2+t^2-2st}
=
|s-t|.
$$

In this example, the process-induced distance agrees with the
usual distance on $[0,1]$.

Each realized sample path is a straight line with random slope $Z$.

## 4. Weak Convergence to a Gaussian Process

Let $X_\alpha$ be random maps taking values in $\ell^\infty(T)$,
and let $X$ be a Gaussian process indexed by $T$.

The lecture gives three conditions that characterize convergence
to a tight, Borel measurable version of $X$:

1. Convergence of finite-dimensional distributions.
2. Asymptotic equicontinuity in probability.
3. Total boundedness of the index set under a suitable semimetric.

Use the process-induced semimetric

$$
\rho_p(s,t)
=
\left(E|X(s)-X(t)|^p\right)^{1/(p\vee 1)},
\qquad p>0.
$$

Under the Gaussian setting of the corollary, the conditions can
be checked for some $p>0$; the conclusion then gives the corresponding
conditions for every $p>0$.

### Condition 1: finite-dimensional convergence

For every $k\geq 1$ and every $t_1,\ldots,t_k\in T$,

$$
\bigl(X_\alpha(t_1),\ldots,X_\alpha(t_k)\bigr)
\rightsquigarrow
\bigl(X(t_1),\ldots,X(t_k)\bigr)
$$

in $\mathbb R^k$.

This is joint convergence at every fixed finite collection of indices.

Checking each coordinate separately is insufficient: we must also
capture the dependence between coordinates.

In applications, a multivariate central limit theorem often
establishes this condition.

### Condition 2: asymptotic equicontinuity

For every $\epsilon>0$,

$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}
\limsup_\alpha
P^\star\left(
\sup_{\rho_p(s,t)<\delta}
|X_\alpha(s)-X_\alpha(t)|>\epsilon
\right)
=0.
$$

Read the expression from the inside outward:

- Consider all pairs of nearby indices.
- Find the largest increment between those pairs.
- Measure the outer probability that it exceeds $\epsilon$.
- Examine the eventual behavior as $\alpha$ increases.
- Finally, shrink the neighborhood size $\delta$ toward zero.

Thus, nearby indices eventually produce similar process values,
uniformly over the index set, with high probability.

Outer probability $P^\star $ is used because the supremum need not
be measurable.

### Condition 3: total boundedness

For every $\delta>0$, finitely many $\rho_p$-balls of radius
$\delta$ cover $T$.

This lets us reduce the index set to finitely many representative
points at any fixed resolution.

### How the conditions fit together

Total boundedness gives a finite collection of representative indices.

Finite-dimensional convergence controls the process at those indices.

Asymptotic equicontinuity controls the differences between each
index and its nearby representative.

Together, these conditions control the entire random function,
rather than only a finite collection of its values.

The conclusion is that $X$ has a version that is a tight,
Borel measurable random element of $\ell^\infty(T)$ and

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(T).
$$

### What is a version?

A version $\widetilde X$ of $X$ satisfies

$$
P\bigl(\widetilde X(t)=X(t)\bigr)=1
$$

for each fixed $t\in T$.

The exceptional null set may depend on $t$.

For an uncountable index set, this does not automatically imply
that the two processes agree simultaneously at every index
on one event of probability one.

Choosing a suitable version lets us obtain the required
measurability and sample-path properties.

## 5. Convergence in Outer Probability

The lecture now moves from $\ell^\infty(T)$ to an arbitrary
metric space $(\mathbb D,d)$.

Let

$$
X_\alpha,X:\Omega\to\mathbb D
$$

be maps defined on the same probability space. These maps need
not be measurable.

### Start with ordinary convergence in probability

For measurable real-valued random variables, convergence in
probability means

$$
P(|X_n-X|>\epsilon)\to 0
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0.
$$

In a metric space, replace absolute difference by distance:

$$
P(d(X_n,X)>\epsilon)\to 0.
$$

But if the maps are not measurable, the event

$$
\{\omega:d(X_n(\omega),X(\omega))>\epsilon\}
$$

may not belong to the probability space's sigma-algebra.
Its ordinary probability may therefore be undefined.

### Outer probability handles this problem

For any set $A\subseteq\Omega$, define

$$
P^\star (A)
=
\inf\{P(B):A\subseteq B,\ B\text{ measurable}\}.
$$

We surround $A$ with measurable supersets and take the smallest
possible probability.

If $A$ is measurable, then

$$
P^\star (A)=P(A).
$$

Thus outer probability extends ordinary probability to events
that may not be measurable.

### Definition

We say that $X_\alpha$ converges to $X$ in outer probability if

$$
P^\star\bigl(d(X_\alpha,X)>\epsilon\bigr)\to 0
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0.
$$

Write

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }X.
$$

The interpretation is familiar: the probability of a discrepancy
larger than any fixed tolerance tends to zero.

Using outer probability makes that statement meaningful even
when the discrepancy event is not measurable.

### The measurable-cover formulation

Let

$$
Z_\alpha=d(X_\alpha,X).
$$

A measurable cover $Z_\alpha^\star $ is a measurable majorant of
$Z_\alpha$ that is minimal up to almost-sure equality.

The star here belongs to the random quantity $Z_\alpha$.
It is different from the star in the notation $P^\star $.

The lecture expresses outer-probability convergence equivalently as

$$
\bigl(d(X_\alpha,X)\bigr)^\star
\xrightarrow{P}0.
$$

The cover is measurable, so ordinary convergence in probability
can be applied to it.

### Example in $\ell^\infty(T)$

With the supremum metric,

$$
d(X_n,X)
=
\|X_n-X\|_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in T}|X_n(t)-X(t)|.
$$

Therefore,

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }X
$$

means

$$
P^\star\left(
\sup_{t\in T}|X_n(t)-X(t)|>\epsilon
\right)\to 0
$$

for every $\epsilon>0$.

This controls the discrepancy uniformly over all indices.

By comparison, convergence at each fixed index only requires

$$
P^\star (|X_n(t)-X(t)|>\epsilon)\to 0
$$

separately for each $t$. That is a weaker requirement.

## 6. Almost Uniform Convergence

Let $X_\alpha,X:\Omega\to\mathbb D$, where $(\mathbb D,d)$
is a metric space.

We say that $X_\alpha$ converges **almost uniformly** to $X$ if,
for every $\eta>0$, there exists a measurable set $A\subseteq\Omega$
such that

$$
P(A)\geq 1-\eta
$$

and

$$
\sup_{\omega\in A}
d\bigl(X_\alpha(\omega),X(\omega)\bigr)\to 0.
$$

Write

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X.
$$

The idea is to discard a set of arbitrarily small probability
and obtain uniform convergence on what remains.

### What is uniform here?

Uniformity is over outcomes $\omega\in A$.

For every tolerance $\epsilon>0$, there exists $\alpha_0$ such that

$$
d\bigl(X_\alpha(\omega),X(\omega)\bigr)<\epsilon
$$

for every $\alpha\geq\alpha_0$ and every $\omega\in A$.

The same $\alpha_0$ works for all outcomes in $A$.

In pointwise convergence, the required starting point may depend
on $\omega$.

The set $A$ may depend on the discarded probability $\eta$, but
it must work for the entire convergence statement, not just one
particular $\alpha$.

### Example: convergence that fails to be uniform everywhere

Let $\Omega=[0,1]$ with uniform probability, and define

$$
X_n(\omega)=\omega^n,
\qquad
X(\omega)=0.
$$

For every $\omega<1$,

$$
X_n(\omega)\to 0.
$$

At $\omega=1$, however, $X_n(1)=1$ for every $n$.

Moreover,

$$
\sup_{\omega\in[0,1]}|X_n(\omega)|=1,
$$

so convergence is not uniform on all of $\Omega$.

For any $0<\eta<1$, choose

$$
A=[0,1-\eta].
$$

Then $P(A)=1-\eta$ and

$$
\sup_{\omega\in A}|X_n(\omega)|
=
(1-\eta)^n\to 0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}0.
$$

### Almost uniform convergence implies outer-probability convergence

Fix $\epsilon>0$ and choose a set $A$ with $P(A)\geq1-\eta$
on which convergence is uniform.

Eventually, the discrepancy is below $\epsilon$ throughout $A$.
Consequently,

$$
\{d(X_\alpha,X)>\epsilon\}\subseteq A^c,
$$

and hence

$$
P^\star\bigl(d(X_\alpha,X)>\epsilon\bigr)
\leq P(A^c)\leq\eta.
$$

Because $\eta$ can be arbitrarily small,

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }X.
$$

### Two different kinds of uniformity

If $\mathbb D=\ell^\infty(T)$, then

$$
d(X_n(\omega),X(\omega))
=
\sup_{t\in T}|X_n(t,\omega)-X(t,\omega)|.
$$

Almost uniform convergence becomes

$$
\sup_{\omega\in A}\sup_{t\in T}
|X_n(t,\omega)-X(t,\omega)|\to 0.
$$

There are two layers:

- The metric controls all indices $t$ within a realized path.
- Almost uniform convergence controls all outcomes $\omega$ in
  a high-probability set.

## 7. Almost Sure and Outer Almost Sure Convergence

Let $X_\alpha,X:\Omega\to\mathbb D$, where $(\mathbb D,d)$
is a metric space, and write

$$
Z_\alpha(\omega)=d(X_\alpha(\omega),X(\omega)).
$$

The lecture distinguishes convergence of the actual distances
from convergence of their measurable covers.

### Almost sure convergence

We say that $X_\alpha$ converges almost surely to $X$ if

$$
P_\star\left(
\left\{\omega:Z_\alpha(\omega)\to 0\right\}
\right)=1.
$$

Write

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X.
$$

Here $P_\star $ is inner probability:

$$
P_\star (A)
=
\sup\{P(B):B\subseteq A,\ B\text{ measurable}\}.
$$

Thus, there is a measurable set of probability one on which
the actual distances converge to zero.

Inner probability is useful because the convergence event
itself may not be measurable.

### Why inner probability?

To say that convergence holds almost surely, we need a
measurable set of probability one contained in the convergence event.

Outer probability one would only say that every measurable
superset has probability one. That does not guarantee a
measurable probability-one set inside the event.

Recall the complementary relationship

$$
P_\star (A)=1-P^\star (A^c).
$$

Therefore, the almost sure condition can also be written

$$
P^\star\left(
\left\{\omega:Z_\alpha(\omega)
\text{ does not converge to }0\right\}
\right)=0.
$$

### Outer almost sure convergence

Let $Z_\alpha^\star $ denote a measurable cover of $Z_\alpha$.

We say that $X_\alpha$ converges outer almost surely to $X$ if,
for some choices of versions of these covers,

$$
Z_\alpha^\star\to 0
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

Write

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}^\star }X.
$$

Compare the definitions:

- Almost sure convergence concerns the actual distances $Z_\alpha$.
- Outer almost sure convergence concerns measurable covers
  $Z_\alpha^\star $.

The word “outer” here refers to the covers. It does not mean
that the convergence event merely has outer probability one.

### Why specify versions of the covers?

A measurable cover is determined only up to almost-sure equality.

For a sequence, countably many exceptional null sets can be
combined into one null set. Consequently, changing versions
does not change almost sure convergence of the sequence.

For a general net, the index set can be uncountable.
An uncountable union of null sets need not be null, so the
choice of versions requires more care.

### The familiar measurable case

For a sequence whose distances $Z_n$ are measurable, we may
take

$$
Z_n^\star =Z_n.
$$

Then both definitions reduce to the usual condition

$$
d(X_n(\omega),X(\omega))\to 0
$$

on a measurable set of probability one.

The distinction matters when measurability is absent or when
working with general nets.

### Almost sure does not mean uniform over outcomes

In almost sure convergence, the starting point needed to obtain
an error below $\epsilon$ may depend on $\omega$.

Almost uniform convergence instead requires one starting point
that works for every outcome in a chosen high-probability set.

For measurable sequences on a probability space, Egorov's theorem
connects these notions: almost sure convergence implies almost
uniform convergence. We should not apply that sequence theorem
automatically to arbitrary nets.

## 8. Relationships Between Modes of Convergence

The lecture now states several useful relationships.

Assume the limiting map $X$ is Borel measurable. Pay attention
to whether a result concerns a sequence $X_n$ or a general net
$X_\alpha$.

### Outer almost sure convergence implies outer-probability convergence

For a sequence,

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}^\star }X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }X.
$$

The measurable covers of the distances converge almost surely,
so they also converge in ordinary probability.

This gives outer-probability convergence of the original distances.

### The subsequence characterization

For a sequence,

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }X
$$

if and only if every subsequence has a further subsequence
converging outer almost surely to $X$.

Symbolically, for every subsequence $(X_{n_j})$, there is a
further subsequence $(X_{n_{j_k}})$ such that

$$
X_{n_{j_k}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}^\star }X.
$$

This does not say that the entire original sequence converges
outer almost surely.

It says that every subsequence contains a further subsequence
with this stronger convergence.

### Egorov's theorem for sequences

The lecture gives

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}^\star }X
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X.
$$

For the forward direction, apply Egorov's theorem to measurable
covers of the distances.

For the reverse direction, almost uniform convergence supplies
uniform control on measurable sets whose probabilities are
arbitrarily close to one. This yields outer almost sure convergence
for the sequence.

The sequence restriction matters: do not automatically extend
this equivalence to arbitrary nets.

### Almost uniform convergence controls the entire tail

For a general net, define the tail discrepancy

$$
R_\alpha(\omega)
=
\sup_{\beta\geq\alpha}
d(X_\beta(\omega),X(\omega)).
$$

The lecture's characterization is

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
R_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Compare this with ordinary outer-probability convergence:

$$
d(X_\alpha,X)\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

The tail supremum controls every later index simultaneously,
rather than only the current index.

Since

$$
d(X_\alpha,X)\leq R_\alpha,
$$

we obtain

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }X.
$$

### Example: convergence in probability without almost sure convergence

Let $B_n$ be independent events with

$$
P(B_n)=\frac1n,
$$

and set $X_n=\mathbf 1_{B_n}$.

For $0<\epsilon<1$,

$$
P(|X_n|>\epsilon)=\frac1n\to 0.
$$

Thus $X_n\to 0$ in probability.

However,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(B_n)=\infty.
$$

By independence and the second Borel–Cantelli lemma, $B_n$
occurs infinitely often almost surely.

Consequently, $X_n$ does not converge to zero almost surely,
and therefore does not converge almost uniformly to zero.

The subsequence characterization still applies: every subsequence
has a further subsequence that converges almost surely to zero.

## 9. Continuous Mapping for Outer-Probability and Almost Uniform Convergence

Suppose $X_\alpha$ converges to $X$ in a metric space
$(\mathbb D,d)$.

We often want convergence of a statistic or transformation
$g(X_\alpha)$, where

$$
g:\mathbb D\to\mathbb E
$$

and $(\mathbb E,e)$ is another metric space.

The continuous mapping theorem lets us transfer convergence
through $g$.

### Assumptions

Let $\mathbb D_0\subseteq\mathbb D$ be a Borel set such that:

1. $g$ is continuous at every point of $\mathbb D_0$.
2. $X$ is Borel measurable.
3. $P(X\in\mathbb D_0)=1$.

Continuity at $x\in\mathbb D_0$ means continuity as a map on
$\mathbb D$: inputs approaching $x$ need not lie in $\mathbb D_0$.

The map does not need to be continuous everywhere. It needs
to be continuous at the possible limiting values, except on
a set of probability zero.

### The conclusions

Under these assumptions,

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g(X_\alpha)\xrightarrow{P^\star }g(X),
$$

and

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g(X_\alpha)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}g(X).
$$

These are the two forms stated in this part of the lecture.

### Example: squaring

Let $\mathbb D=\mathbb E=\mathbb R$ and $g(x)=x^2$.

Since $g$ is continuous everywhere,

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X_n^2\xrightarrow{P^\star }X^2.
$$

The function $g(x)=x^2$ is not uniformly continuous on all
of $\mathbb R$. Global uniform continuity is not required.

### Example: taking a supremum

For bounded functions on a nonempty index set $T$, define

$$
g(x)=\sup_{t\in T}x(t).
$$

For any $x,y\in\ell^\infty(T)$,

$$
\left|
\sup_{t\in T}x(t)-\sup_{t\in T}y(t)
\right|
\leq
\|x-y\|_\infty.
$$

Thus $g$ is continuous in the supremum norm, and

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\sup_{t\in T}X_n(t)
\xrightarrow{P^\star }
\sup_{t\in T}X(t).
$$

This is useful when a statistic depends on the largest value
of an entire process.

### Why continuity at the limit matters

Consider

$$
g(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x>0\}}.
$$

This function is discontinuous at zero.

If $X_n=1/n$ and $X=0$, then $X_n\to X$, but

$$
g(X_n)=1,
\qquad
g(X)=0.
$$

Therefore, convergence does not pass through this transformation.

If instead $P(X=0)=0$, the discontinuity is avoided almost surely,
and the theorem applies.

## 10. Negligible Perturbations and Weak Convergence

The next lemma connects convergence in outer probability
to weak convergence.

Let $X_\alpha,Y_\alpha$ take values in the same metric space
$(\mathbb D,d)$ and be defined on the same probability space
for each $\alpha$.

Assume the limiting random element $X$ is Borel measurable.

### A nearby process has the same weak limit

If

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
$$

and

$$
d(X_\alpha,Y_\alpha)\xrightarrow{P^\star }0,
$$

then

$$
Y_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X.
$$

Thus, an approximation error that vanishes in outer probability
does not change the weak limit.

The closeness condition compares the two random elements
on their common probability space. Having similar marginal
distributions alone does not establish this condition.

### Why this is useful

Suppose $Y_\alpha$ is the complicated process we actually
want to study.

Instead of proving its weak convergence directly:

1. Construct a simpler approximation $X_\alpha$.
2. Prove $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$.
3. Show $d(X_\alpha,Y_\alpha)\xrightarrow{P^\star }0$.

The lemma then transfers the weak limit to $Y_\alpha$.

The transcript emphasizes this strategy: replace a difficult
object with a nearby surrogate whose convergence is easier
to establish.

### Example in $\ell^\infty(T)$

Suppose

$$
Y_n(t)=X_n(t)+R_n(t),
$$

where

$$
X_n\rightsquigarrow X
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(T)
$$

and

$$
\|R_n\|_\infty
=
\sup_{t\in T}|R_n(t)|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Since

$$
\|Y_n-X_n\|_\infty=\|R_n\|_\infty,
$$

we obtain

$$
Y_n\rightsquigarrow X.
$$

The remainder must vanish in the metric of the space.
Here that requires uniform control over $T$, rather than
only convergence at each fixed index.

### Outer-probability convergence implies weak convergence

The lemma also states

$$
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }X
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X.
$$

Outer-probability convergence controls the distance between
the actual random elements.

Weak convergence controls their limiting distributions.
It does not generally imply that the random elements become
close on their original probability space.

### A constant limit gives an equivalence

For a fixed, nonrandom $c\in\mathbb D$,

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow c
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{P^\star }c.
$$

Here weak convergence means convergence to the point-mass
distribution concentrated at $c$.

There is no randomness in the limiting value, so convergence
of the distributions forces concentration near that value.

### Why the converse fails for a random limit

Let $Z$ and $W$ be independent $N(0,1)$ variables, and set

$$
X_n=Z,
\qquad
X=W.
$$

Every $X_n$ has the same distribution as $X$, so

$$
X_n\rightsquigarrow X.
$$

However, for any fixed $\epsilon>0$,

$$
P(|X_n-X|>\epsilon)
=
P(|Z-W|>\epsilon)>0,
$$

and this probability does not decrease with $n$.

Therefore, $X_n$ does not converge in probability to $X$.

## 11. Skorokhod Representation: Realizing Weak Convergence on a New Space

Weak convergence concerns distributions. It does not generally
give convergence of the original random elements to one another.

The next result says that, under suitable assumptions, we can
construct representatives on a new probability space that
converge in a stronger sense.

### The setting

Let

$$
X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\mathbb D
$$

be random maps indexed by a nontrivial directed set.

Suppose

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X_\infty,
$$

where $X_\infty$ is Borel measurable and separable.

Here separability means that the limit lies almost surely in
a separable subset of $\mathbb D$: a subset with a countable
dense set.

The original maps may live on different probability spaces.

### The representation result

There exists a new probability space

$$
(\widetilde\Omega,\widetilde{\mathcal A},\widetilde P)
$$

and maps $\widetilde X_\alpha,\widetilde X_\infty$ into
$\mathbb D$ such that

$$
\widetilde X_\alpha
\xrightarrow{\mathrm{au}}
\widetilde X_\infty.
$$

The construction preserves the marginal information. In the
lecture's general formulation,

$$
\widetilde E^\star f(\widetilde X_\alpha)
=
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
$$

for every bounded function $f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb R$, including
the limiting index $\alpha=\infty$.

For ordinary Borel measurable random elements, this gives
representatives with the same individual distributions as
the original random elements.

### What changes?

The construction changes how the random elements are arranged
together on a common probability space.

It preserves each individual distribution, but it need not
preserve dependence between different indices.

This arrangement is called a coupling.

Thus, the result does not claim that the original $X_\alpha$
converges almost uniformly to the original $X_\infty$.

It constructs new representatives for which that convergence holds.

### Example: equal distributions, different couplings

Suppose $X_n$ and $X$ are independent standard normal variables.

Since their distributions are identical,

$$
X_n\rightsquigarrow X,
$$

but their differences do not converge to zero in probability.

On a new probability space, take one standard normal variable $Z$
and define

$$
\widetilde X_n=Z,
\qquad
\widetilde X=Z.
$$

Each representative has the required standard normal distribution,
and

$$
|\widetilde X_n-\widetilde X|=0
$$

for every $n$.

The marginal distributions are preserved; the dependence
between the representatives has changed.

### Connection to the familiar sequence theorem

For sequences of Borel measurable random elements in the
usual separable metric-space setting, Skorokhod representation
is commonly stated as

$$
\widetilde X_n\to\widetilde X
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

The lecture uses almost uniform convergence to formulate
the result for the more general setting of nets and potentially
nonmeasurable maps.

### Why this helps in proofs

On the new space, convergence is expressed through distances
between realized objects.

That can make deterministic continuity arguments easier
to apply. Distributional conclusions can then be transferred
back to the original objects because the relevant marginal
information was preserved.
