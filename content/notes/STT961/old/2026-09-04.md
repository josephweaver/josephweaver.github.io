## Portmanteau Theorem

The **Portmanteau Theorem** gives several equivalent ways to characterize
weak convergence.

The professor described it as a **"suitcase theorem"**: many different
results are packed together into one theorem.

Suppose $X_\alpha$ is a net of random elements in a metric space
$\mathbb{D}$ and $L$ is a Borel probability law.

The following statements are equivalent.

### 1. Weak convergence

$$
X_\alpha \rightsquigarrow L.
$$

By definition, this means that for every bounded continuous
$f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}$,

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
\longrightarrow
\int f\,dL.
$$

---

### 2. Open sets

For every open set $G\subseteq\mathbb{D}$,

$$
\liminf_\alpha
P_\star (X_\alpha\in G)
\geq
L(G).
$$

**Interpretation:** probability cannot suddenly disappear from an open
set in the limit.

Notice the combination

$$
\boxed{\text{open set} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \liminf,\ \geq}
$$

---

### 3. Closed sets

For every closed set $F\subseteq\mathbb{D}$,

$$
\limsup_\alpha
P^\star (X_\alpha\in F)
\leq
L(F).
$$

**Interpretation:** a closed set cannot suddenly acquire extra probability
in the limit.

Notice the complementary combination

$$
\boxed{\text{closed set} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \limsup,\ \leq}
$$

The open-set and closed-set statements are closely related through the
relationship between inner and outer probability.

---

### 4. Lower semicontinuous functions

For every lower semicontinuous function $f$ that is **bounded below**,

$$
\liminf_\alpha E_\star f(X_\alpha)
\geq
\int f\,dL.
$$

---

### 5. Upper semicontinuous functions

For every upper semicontinuous function $f$ that is **bounded above**,

$$
\limsup_\alpha E^\star f(X_\alpha)
\leq
\int f\,dL.
$$

Thus there is a useful pattern:

| Type                          | Limit       | Inequality |
| ----------------------------- | ----------- | ---------- |
| Open / lower semicontinuous   | $\liminf$ | $\geq$   |
| Closed / upper semicontinuous | $\limsup$ | $\leq$   |

---

### 6. Continuity sets

Let $B$ be a Borel set whose boundary has zero probability under $L$:

$$
L(\partial B)=0.
$$

Since

$$
\partial B=\overline B-B^\circ,
$$

this means that the limiting distribution puts no probability mass on
the boundary of $B$.

Then

$$
P_\star (X_\alpha\in B)
\longrightarrow L(B)
$$

and

$$
P^\star (X_\alpha\in B)
\longrightarrow L(B).
$$

Such a set $B$ is often called an **$L$-continuity set**.

### 7. Bounded Lipschitz functions

For every bounded, Lipschitz-continuous, nonnegative function $f$,

$$
\liminf_\alpha E_\star f(X_\alpha)
\geq
\int f\,dL.
$$

This condition is especially useful technically: Lipschitz functions
can be constructed to approximate indicators of open sets.

### Connection to ordinary probability

For ordinary measurable real-valued random variables, inner and outer
probabilities agree with ordinary probability.

Then the Portmanteau theorem reduces to familiar statements about
convergence in distribution.

For example, choosing sets such as

$$
(-\infty,x]
$$

connects these statements to convergence of CDFs at continuity points
of the limiting distribution.

So the metric-space theory is a generalization of the familiar idea

$$
F_n(x)\to F(x)
$$

at continuity points of $F$.

### Big picture

Do **not** memorize the Portmanteau theorem as seven unrelated facts.

Remember the structure:

$$
\boxed{
\text{weak convergence}
\iff
\text{open/closed set inequalities}
\iff
\text{semicontinuous function inequalities}
\iff
\text{convergence on continuity sets}
}
$$

It gives us different tools for proving the **same weak convergence**,
depending on which representation is easiest in a particular problem.

## Upper and Lower Semicontinuity

The Portmanteau theorem uses **upper semicontinuous** and
**lower semicontinuous** functions.

These are weaker notions than ordinary continuity: instead of requiring
the function to approach $f(x)$ from both directions, we only control
one direction.

### Upper semicontinuity

Let

$$
f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{R}.
$$

The function $f$ is **upper semicontinuous** if, for every $c\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$
\{x\in\mathbb{D}:f(x)\geq c\}
$$

is closed.

An equivalent sequential/limit characterization is

$$
\limsup_{y\to x} f(y)\leq f(x).
$$

So nearby values of $f$ cannot approach a value strictly **above**
$f(x)$.

Intuitively, an upper semicontinuous function is allowed to have a
**jump upward at the point**, but not a jump downward at the point.

---

### Lower semicontinuity

Lower semicontinuity can be obtained by applying upper semicontinuity
to $-f$.

Thus $f$ is **lower semicontinuous** if

$$
\{x\in\mathbb{D}:f(x)\leq c\}
$$

is closed for every $c\in\mathbb{R}$.

Equivalently,

$$
\liminf_{y\to x}f(y)\geq f(x).
$$

So nearby values cannot approach a value strictly **below** $f(x)$.

Intuitively, a lower semicontinuous function is allowed to have a
**jump downward at the point**, but not a jump upward at the point.

---

### Relationship with continuity

For an ordinary continuous function,

$$
\lim_{y\to x}f(y)=f(x),
$$

so both inequalities hold:

$$
\limsup_{y\to x}f(y)\leq f(x)
$$

and

$$
\liminf_{y\to x}f(y)\geq f(x).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
f\text{ continuous}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
f\text{ is both upper and lower semicontinuous}.
}
$$

Semicontinuity is therefore "almost continuity," but only from one
direction.

### Why this appears in Portmanteau

The directions line up exactly with the Portmanteau inequalities:

$$
\text{lower semicontinuous}
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\liminf
$$

and

$$
\text{upper semicontinuous}
\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad
\limsup.
$$

In particular,

$$
\liminf_\alpha E_\star f(X_\alpha)
\geq
\int f\,dL
$$

for suitable lower semicontinuous $f$, while

$$
\limsup_\alpha E^\star f(X_\alpha)
\leq
\int f\,dL
$$

for suitable upper semicontinuous $f$.

$$
\boxed{\text{LOWER semicontinuous} \leftrightarrow \liminf\geq}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{UPPER semicontinuous} \leftrightarrow \limsup\leq}
$$

## Continuous Mapping Theorem

A natural corollary of the Portmanteau Theorem is the
**Continuous Mapping Theorem**.

Suppose

$$
X_\alpha \rightsquigarrow X.
$$

If $g$ is continuous almost surely with respect to the limiting
random element $X$, then

$$
g(X_\alpha)\rightsquigarrow g(X).
$$

So weak convergence is preserved when we apply an appropriate
continuous transformation.

---

### More precise metric-space statement

Let

$$
g:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{E},
$$

where $\mathbb{D}$ and $\mathbb{E}$ are metric spaces.

Let

$$
\mathbb{D}_0\subseteq\mathbb{D},
$$

and suppose $g$ is continuous at every point

$$
a\in\mathbb{D}_0.
$$

If

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
$$

and $X$ takes values in $\mathbb{D}_0$, then

$$
\boxed{
g(X_\alpha)\rightsquigarrow g(X)
}
$$

### Important point

We do **not** require $g$ to be continuous everywhere in
$\mathbb{D}$.

We only need continuity at the points in $\mathbb{D}_0$, where the
limiting random element $X$ takes its values.

Schematically,

$$
X_\alpha
\rightsquigarrow
X
\qquad
\overset{g}{\Longrightarrow}
\qquad
g(X_\alpha)
\rightsquigarrow
g(X).
$$

The professor emphasized that this is a theorem we will use repeatedly
in later proofs.

### Connection to familiar probability

This is the same basic continuous mapping principle used in ordinary
convergence-in-distribution arguments:

if a random quantity converges weakly, then applying a continuous
transformation preserves that weak convergence.

$$

$$

and $g$ has some points where it is discontinuous.

Do we automatically lose the Continuous Mapping Theorem?

What matters about the values that $X$ can actually take?

## Asymptotic Measurability

Recall that our random maps $X_\alpha$ need not themselves be measurable.

Because of this, we have been working with **outer expectation**
$E^\star $ and **inner expectation** $E_\star $.

A net of maps $X_\alpha$ is called **asymptotically measurable** if

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\longrightarrow 0
$$

for every

$$
f\in C_b(\mathbb{D}),
$$

where $C_b(\mathbb{D})$ is the collection of bounded continuous
functions on $\mathbb{D}$.

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha\text{ asymptotically measurable}
\iff
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0
\quad
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
}
$$

### Why this definition?

For a measurable random variable,

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)=E_\star f(X_\alpha)=E f(X_\alpha).
$$

For a potentially nonmeasurable map, there may instead be a gap:

$$
E_\star f(X_\alpha)
\leq
E^\star f(X_\alpha).
$$

Asymptotic measurability says that this gap becomes negligible as
$\alpha$ progresses:

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0.
$$

So $X_\alpha$ does **not necessarily become measurable**.

Rather, its possible nonmeasurability becomes asymptotically irrelevant
when tested using the bounded continuous functions that define weak
convergence.

### Why $C_b(\mathbb D)$?

Weak convergence itself is defined using bounded continuous functions:

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
$$

when the corresponding expectations converge for every

$$
f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

Therefore the same class of test functions appears in the definition of
asymptotic measurability.

### About nets

The professor again emphasized:

> When you see a **net**, think of a sequence as the important special
> case.

Thus, for a sequence $X_n$, the definition becomes simply

$$
E^\star f(X_n)-E_\star f(X_n)\to0
\qquad
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

$$

for each $n$?

## Asymptotic Tightness

Let $X_\alpha$ be a net of maps taking values in the metric space
$\mathbb{D}$.

We say that $X_\alpha$ is **asymptotically tight** if, for every

$$
\epsilon>0,
$$

there exists a compact set

$$
K\subseteq\mathbb{D}
$$

such that

$$
\liminf_\alpha
P_\star (X_\alpha\in K^\delta)
\geq
1-\epsilon
$$

for every

$$
\delta>0.
$$

Here,

$$
K^\delta
=
\{y\in\mathbb{D}:d(y,K)<\delta\}
$$

is called the **$\delta$-enlargement** of $K$.

---

### Distance from a point to a set

For a point $y$ and a set $K$,

$$
d(y,K)
=
\inf_{x\in K} d(y,x).
$$

So we look at the distance from $y$ to every point in $K$ and take the
smallest possible distance.

Therefore,

$$
K^\delta
=
\left\{
y:
\inf_{x\in K}d(y,x)<\delta
\right\}.
$$

---

### Geometric picture

In $\mathbb{R}^2$, imagine an irregular compact set $K$.

Then $K^\delta$ consists of $K$ together with all points lying less
than $\delta$ away from it.

You can picture it as a **$\delta$-thick envelope around $K$**:

$$
\boxed{
K
\subset
K^\delta
}
$$

The professor drew exactly this sort of picture in the notes.

---

### What the definition is saying

For every tolerance $\epsilon>0$, we can find one compact set $K$
such that, asymptotically,

$$
X_\alpha
$$

lies inside **every $\delta$-enlargement of $K$** with probability at
least approximately

$$
1-\epsilon.
$$

That is,

$$
\boxed{
\liminf_\alpha
P_\star (X_\alpha\in K^\delta)
\geq
1-\epsilon
\qquad
\forall\delta>0.
}
$$

The use of inner probability $P_\star $ is again because the maps
$X_\alpha$ are not assumed to be measurable.

---

### Compare with ordinary tightness

Ordinary tightness of a probability law $L$ means that for every
$\epsilon>0$, we can find a compact set $K$ such that

$$
L(K)\geq1-\epsilon.
$$

Asymptotic tightness is the analogous idea for an entire net
$X_\alpha$.

The basic idea is still:

> almost all of the probability can be confined near a compact set.

## Weak Convergence, Asymptotic Measurability, and Tightness

The previous definitions are closely connected.

Suppose

$$
X_\alpha \rightsquigarrow X.
$$

Then we have two useful results.

---

### Lemma 1: Weak convergence implies asymptotic measurability

If

$$
X_\alpha \rightsquigarrow X,
$$

then

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha \text{ is asymptotically measurable}.
}
$$

Recall that asymptotic measurability means

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0
$$

for every

$$
f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

This makes intuitive sense because weak convergence says that the
expectations of bounded continuous functions are converging to the
expectation under the limiting law.

The difference between the outer and inner expectations therefore
vanishes asymptotically.

---

### A useful way to see this

Recall that

$$
E_\star f(X_\alpha)
=
-E^\star [-f(X_\alpha)].
$$

Since $f\in C_b(\mathbb D)$ implies

$$
-f\in C_b(\mathbb D),
$$

weak convergence gives convergence for both $f$ and $-f$.

Thus both

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
$$

and

$$
E_\star f(X_\alpha)
$$

approach the same limiting quantity,

$$
\int f\,dL.
$$

Hence

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0.
$$

---

## Lemma 2: Asymptotic tightness versus tightness of the limit

Again suppose

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha \text{ is asymptotically tight}
\iff
X \text{ has a tight law}.
}
$$

Recall that the law of $X$ is **tight** if, for every $\epsilon>0$,
there exists a compact set $K\subseteq\mathbb D$ such that

$$
P(X\in K)\geq1-\epsilon.
$$

Equivalently, if $L$ is the law of $X$,

$$
L(K)\geq1-\epsilon.
$$

---

### Why this is useful

Asymptotic tightness is a property of the entire collection

$$
X_\alpha,
$$

while ordinary tightness is a property of the limiting probability law.

Once we already know that

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X,
$$

the lemma tells us that these two notions are equivalent.

So rather than directly checking the more complicated condition

$$
\liminf_\alpha
P_\star (X_\alpha\in K^\delta)\geq1-\epsilon,
$$

we can often inspect the limiting law $X$ instead.

In many applications the limiting process is Gaussian and can be shown
to possess a tight Borel law. Once tightness of that limiting law is
established, asymptotic tightness of $X_\alpha$ follows from the lemma.

Therefore, once convergence to such a limit has been established,
asymptotic tightness follows automatically.

---

### Big picture

Under weak convergence,

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X,
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha \text{ is asymptotically measurable}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha \text{ asymptotically tight}
\iff
X \text{ tight}.
}
$$

These results connect the three ideas:

$$
\text{weak convergence}
\qquad
\text{measurability}
\qquad
\text{tightness}.
$$

## Prokhorov's Theorem

Prokhorov's Theorem connects **tightness** with the existence of
weakly convergent subsequences.

### General version for nets

Suppose the net $X_\alpha$ is

1. asymptotically tight, and
2. asymptotically measurable.

Then there exists a subnet

$$
X_{\alpha(\beta)}
$$

that converges weakly to a **tight Borel probability law**.

Schematically,

$$
\boxed{
\text{asymptotically tight}
+
\text{asymptotically measurable}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{subsequential weak convergence}
}
$$

The limiting law is itself a tight Borel law.

---

### Sequence version

For a sequence $X_n$, the corresponding statement says that under
these conditions there exists a subsequence

$$
X_{n_k}
$$

that converges weakly to a tight Borel law.

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
X_n\text{ asymptotically tight and measurable}
\Longrightarrow
\exists\,X_{n_k}\rightsquigarrow L.
}
$$

> **Important:** The net and sequence versions are separate parts of
> Prokhorov's theorem; neither implies the other. In particular, the
> existence of a convergent subnet of a sequence does not by itself give
> the convergent subsequence asserted in the sequence version.

---

### What the theorem does **not** say

The important distinction is that

$$
\text{asymptotic tightness}
+
\text{asymptotic measurability}
$$

does **not** imply that the entire sequence $X_n$ weakly converges.

Instead, it guarantees that somewhere inside the sequence there is a
weakly convergent subsequence.

So

$$
X_n
\not\Rightarrow
\text{one common limit},
$$

but Prokhorov tells us we can find

$$
X_{n_k}\rightsquigarrow L.
$$

---

### Connection with the previous lemmas

Previously we learned that

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
$$

implies asymptotic measurability.

Also, when $X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X$,

$$
X_\alpha\text{ asymptotically tight}
\iff
X\text{ is tight}.
$$

Therefore weak convergence to a tight law gives us

$$
\text{asymptotic measurability}
+
\text{asymptotic tightness}.
$$

But the converse is weaker:

$$
\text{asymptotic measurability}
+
\text{asymptotic tightness}
$$

only guarantees the existence of a convergent **subsequence/subnet**.

This is why the professor emphasized that weak convergence is a
stronger statement.

---

### Intuition

Think of tightness as preventing the probability distributions from
"escaping to infinity."

If the sequence cannot escape, Prokhorov's theorem says that we can
extract some subsequence whose distributions settle down to a
probability law.

It does **not** say that every part of the original sequence settles
down to the same law.

### Simple conceptual example

Imagine a sequence whose distributions alternate between two different
well-behaved probability laws:

$$
L_1,L_2,L_1,L_2,\ldots
$$

The full sequence may fail to converge.

But we can extract the odd subsequence

$$
L_1,L_1,L_1,\ldots
$$

or the even subsequence

$$
L_2,L_2,L_2,\ldots
$$

each of which converges.

This illustrates why **existence of a convergent subsequence** is
weaker than convergence of the whole sequence.

---

### Big picture

The practical structure is

$$
\boxed{
\text{control where the probability can go}
\Longrightarrow
\text{extract a weakly convergent subsequence}.
}
$$

Prokhorov's theorem is therefore a major tool for proving the
**existence of weak limits**.

## Other Properties of Weak Convergence

### 1. Restricting the State Space

Let

$$
\mathbb D_0\subseteq\mathbb D
$$

be an arbitrary subset.

Equip both $\mathbb D_0$ and $\mathbb D$ with the **same metric**
(the metric on $\mathbb D_0$ is the restriction of the metric on
$\mathbb D$).

Suppose that both

$$
X_\alpha
\quad\text{and}\quad
X
$$

take values in $\mathbb D_0$.

Then

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\text{ as maps into }\mathbb D_0
\iff
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\text{ as maps into }\mathbb D.
}
$$

### Interpretation

If all of the random elements actually live inside $\mathbb D_0$,
then enlarging the ambient space from

$$
\mathbb D_0
$$

to

$$
\mathbb D
$$

does not change whether weak convergence holds.

For example, schematically,

$$
\mathbb D_0\subset\mathbb D,
$$

and

$$
X_\alpha,X\in\mathbb D_0.
$$

We can study

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
$$

either in the smaller space $\mathbb D_0$ or in the larger space
$\mathbb D$.

The answer is the same, provided the metric is the same.

### Why this is useful

Sometimes the natural mathematical setting is a large function space
$\mathbb D$, while the random objects under consideration actually
belong to a smaller subset $\mathbb D_0$.

This result lets us move between those viewpoints without changing
the weak-convergence statement.

## Bounded Continuous Functions Determine the Measure

Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be finite Borel measures on $\mathbb D$.

Suppose that

$$
\int f\,dL_1
=
\int f\,dL_2
$$

for every

$$
f\in C_b(\mathbb D),
$$

where $C_b(\mathbb D)$ is the set of bounded continuous functions on
$\mathbb D$.

Then

$$
\boxed{
L_1=L_2.
}
$$

### Interpretation

To show that two finite Borel measures are the same, it is enough to
show that they give the same integral for every bounded continuous
test function.

That is,

$$
\left[
\int f\,dL_1
=
\int f\,dL_2
\quad
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D)
\right]
\Longrightarrow
L_1=L_2.
$$

So the collection $C_b(\mathbb D)$ is rich enough to uniquely identify
a finite Borel measure.

### Why this matters for weak convergence

Weak convergence is defined using exactly these bounded continuous
functions.

If

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow L,
$$

we study quantities of the form

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
\to
\int f\,dL,
\qquad
f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

This result tells us that the values

$$
\int f\,dL,
\qquad f\in C_b(\mathbb D),
$$

uniquely determine the limiting measure $L$.

So there cannot be two different finite Borel measures that agree on
all bounded continuous test functions.

### Big picture

Bounded continuous functions serve two roles:

1. They are used to **define weak convergence**.
2. They are rich enough to **uniquely identify the limiting measure**.

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
C_b(\mathbb D)
\text{ is convergence-determining and measure-determining.}
}
$$

## Measure-Determining Vector Lattices

Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be **tight Borel probability measures** on
$\mathbb D$.

Suppose

$$
\mathcal F\subseteq C_b(\mathbb D)
$$

is a **vector lattice** such that:

1. $\mathcal F$ contains the constant functions, and
2. $\mathcal F$ separates points in $\mathbb D$.

If

$$
\int f\,dL_1
=
\int f\,dL_2
\qquad
\forall f\in\mathcal F,
$$

then

$$
\boxed{L_1=L_2.}
$$

---

### What is a vector lattice?

A **vector lattice** $\mathcal F$ is a vector space of functions that is
also closed under taking positive parts.

If

$$
f\in\mathcal F,
$$

then

$$
f^+
=
f\vee0
=
\max(f,0)
$$

must also belong to $\mathcal F$:

$$
\boxed{
f\in\mathcal F
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
f^+\in\mathcal F.
}
$$

Because $\mathcal F$ is already a vector space, this gives us substantial
freedom to construct new functions from functions already in
$\mathcal F$.

---

### What does "separates points" mean?

The class $\mathcal F$ **separates points** in $\mathbb D$ if, whenever

$$
x\neq y,
$$

there exists some

$$
f\in\mathcal F
$$

such that

$$
f(x)\neq f(y).
$$

In symbols,

$$
\boxed{
x\neq y
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\exists f\in\mathcal F:
f(x)\neq f(y).
}
$$

So although $\mathcal F$ may be much smaller than $C_b(\mathbb D)$,
it still contains enough functions to distinguish any two different
points in the space.

---

### Compare with the previous result

Previously we had

$$
\int f\,dL_1
=
\int f\,dL_2
\qquad
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D)
$$

implying

$$
L_1=L_2.
$$

Now we learn that it may be enough to check only

$$
f\in\mathcal F,
$$

provided $\mathcal F$ has enough structure:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\mathcal F\text{ is a vector lattice}\\
\mathcal F\text{ contains constants}\\
\mathcal F\text{ separates points}
\end{array}
\quad
\Longrightarrow
\quad
\mathcal F\text{ can determine a tight Borel probability measure.}
}
$$

### Why this matters

This gives us a strategy for proving that two probability laws are the
same without checking **every** bounded continuous function.

Instead, we may be able to find a smaller, convenient family of test
functions that is still rich enough to characterize the law.

points $x\neq y$ such that

$$

\qquad
\forall f\in\mathcal F.
$$

Does $\mathcal F$ separate points?

Why might such a class be too small to uniquely identify a probability
law?

## A Criterion for Asymptotic Measurability

Let $X_\alpha$ be an **asymptotically tight** net.

Suppose

$$
\mathcal F\subseteq C_b(\mathbb D)
$$

is a subalgebra that **separates points** in $\mathbb D$.

Assume that

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\longrightarrow 0
\qquad
\forall f\in\mathcal F.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha \text{ is asymptotically measurable}.
}
$$

---

### Why this is useful

Recall that asymptotic measurability is defined by requiring

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0
$$

for **every**

$$
f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

This result says that, if $X_\alpha$ is already asymptotically tight,
we do not necessarily need to verify this for all of $C_b(\mathbb D)$.

It is enough to verify it on a sufficiently rich subalgebra
$\mathcal F$ that separates points.

Schematically,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
X_\alpha\text{ asymptotically tight}\\[2mm]
+
\\[2mm]
E^\star f(X_\alpha)-E_\star f(X_\alpha)\to0
\quad\forall f\in\mathcal F
\end{array}
\Longrightarrow
X_\alpha\text{ asymptotically measurable}.
}
$$

---

## What is an algebra of functions?

A collection $\mathcal F$ is an **algebra** if

1. $\mathcal F$ is a vector space, and
2. it is closed under multiplication.

That is, if

$$
f,g\in\mathcal F,
$$

then

$$
fg\in\mathcal F.
$$

So

$$
\boxed{
f,g\in\mathcal F
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
fg\in\mathcal F.
}
$$

---

### Separating points

As before, $\mathcal F$ **separates points** if for any

$$
x\neq y
$$

there exists some $f\in\mathcal F$ such that

$$
f(x)\neq f(y).
$$

This ensures that $\mathcal F$ contains enough functions to distinguish
different points of the state space.

---

### Compare with the previous result

The previous theorem used a **vector lattice** to determine a probability
measure.

Here we use a **subalgebra** to establish asymptotic measurability.

So keep the two structures separate:

| Result                                  | Function class |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| Determine a tight Borel probability law | Vector lattice |
| Establish asymptotic measurability      | Subalgebra     |

Both require a class rich enough to **separate points**.

### Big picture

Instead of checking an enormous collection of test functions,

$$
C_b(\mathbb D),
$$

we can sometimes work with a much smaller, structured collection

$$
\mathcal F.
$$

The combination of

$$
\text{tightness}
+
\text{a rich enough test-function class}
$$

lets us extend information from $\mathcal F$ to all of
$C_b(\mathbb D)$.

### Check your understanding

Why is the conclusion of this theorem stronger than the assumption?

In particular, compare

$$

\qquad
\forall f\in\mathcal F
$$

with the definition of asymptotic measurability, which requires the same
statement for

$$
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

## Chapter 1.5 — Spaces of Bounded Functions

We now begin working with the function space

$$
\ell^\infty(T),
$$

where $T$ is an **arbitrary set**.

There is no assumption yet that $T$ is an interval, a subset of
$\mathbb R$, or even itself a metric space.

It can be essentially anything.

---

### Definition of $\ell^\infty(T)$

Consider real-valued functions

$$
z:T\to\mathbb R.
$$

The space $\ell^\infty(T)$ consists of all functions $z$ that are
bounded uniformly over $t\in T$:

$$
\boxed{
\ell^\infty(T)
=
\left\{
z:T\to\mathbb R:
\sup_{t\in T}|z(t)|<\infty
\right\}.
}
$$

So for each $z\in\ell^\infty(T)$,

$$
|z(t)|
$$

cannot become arbitrarily large as $t$ ranges over $T$.

---

## The Supremum Norm

For

$$
z\in\ell^\infty(T),
$$

define

$$
\boxed{
\|z\|_T
=
\sup_{t\in T}|z(t)|.
}
$$

This is called the **supremum norm** or **uniform norm**.

It measures the largest absolute value attained, or approached, by the
function anywhere on $T$.

### Example

Suppose

$$
T=[0,1]
$$

and

$$
z(t)=t^2.
$$

Then

$$
\|z\|_T
=
\sup_{0\leq t\leq1}t^2
=
1.
$$

Notice that the supremum norm is a property of the **function** $z$,
not simply of the set $T$.

---

## Uniform Distance Between Functions

The supremum norm gives us a metric on $\ell^\infty(T)$.

For two functions

$$
z_1,z_2\in\ell^\infty(T),
$$

define

$$
d(z_1,z_2)
=
\|z_1-z_2\|_T.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
d(z_1,z_2)
=
\sup_{t\in T}
|z_1(t)-z_2(t)|.
}
$$

This is called the **uniform distance**.

It asks:

> What is the largest difference between these two functions anywhere
> on $T$?

Thus two functions are close in $\ell^\infty(T)$ only if they are close
**everywhere on $T$**.

---

### Geometric interpretation

For ordinary vectors, distance measures how far apart two points are.

Here, our "points" are entire functions:

$$
z_1,\ z_2.
$$

The distance between those points is

$$
\sup_{t\in T}|z_1(t)-z_2(t)|.
$$

So we have turned a collection of functions into a metric space:

$$
\boxed{
(\ell^\infty(T),\|\cdot\|_T).
}
$$

This allows us to apply the weak-convergence machinery developed for
general metric spaces to **random functions**.

---

### Why this matters for the course

So far we have discussed weak convergence abstractly in a metric space
$\mathbb D$.

Now one of the main choices for that metric space will be

$$
\mathbb D=\ell^\infty(T).
$$

Instead of studying convergence of ordinary random variables,

$$
X_n\in\mathbb R,
$$

we will eventually study convergence of entire random functions,

$$
X_n\in\ell^\infty(T).
$$

This is the bridge toward stochastic processes and empirical processes.

$$

\qquad\text{and}\qquad

$$

on $T=[0,1]$.

What does

$$
\|z_1-z_2\|_T
=
\sup_{0\leq t\leq1}|t-t^2|
$$

mean geometrically?

In particular, are we measuring the difference between the two functions
at one particular $t$, or looking for their **largest difference over
all $t$**?

## Stochastic Processes and Sample Paths

A **stochastic process** is a collection

$$
\{X(t):t\in T\}
$$

of random variables defined on the same probability space.

For each fixed

$$
t\in T,
$$

we have a random variable

$$
X(t):\Omega\to\mathbb R.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\text{fix }t
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
X(t,\cdot)\text{ is a random variable.}
}
$$

---

## Fixing $\omega$ Instead

We can also hold the outcome

$$
\omega\in\Omega
$$

fixed and allow $t$ to vary.

Then we obtain the function

$$
t\mapsto X(t,\omega).
$$

This function is called a **sample path**.

So,

$$
\boxed{
\text{fix }\omega
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
t\mapsto X(t,\omega)
\text{ is a sample path.}
}
$$

---

## Two Views of the Same Object

The stochastic process can therefore be viewed in two complementary ways.

### Fix $t$

$$
\omega\mapsto X(t,\omega)
$$

is a random variable.

### Fix $\omega$

$$
t\mapsto X(t,\omega)
$$

is a function on $T$.

This distinction is important because we are moving from ordinary random
variables toward **random functions**.

---

## Connection with $\ell^\infty(T)$

If every sample path

$$
t\mapsto X(t,\omega)
$$

is bounded, then each sample path belongs to

$$
\ell^\infty(T).
$$

In that case, we can regard the entire stochastic process as a map

$$
X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T),
$$

where

$$
\omega\mapsto X(\cdot,\omega).
$$

So instead of thinking of $X$ only as a collection of scalar random
variables, we can think of $X$ as one random object whose value is an
entire function.

Schematically,

$$
\boxed{
\omega
\longmapsto
\big[t\mapsto X(t,\omega)\big].
}
$$

### Big picture

Previously,

$$
X:\Omega\to\mathbb R
$$

was a random variable.

Now,

$$
X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T)
$$

can be viewed as a **random function**.

This is the viewpoint needed for studying weak convergence of
stochastic processes.

## Viewing a Stochastic Process as an $\ell^\infty(T)$-Valued Map

Suppose

$$
\{X(t):t\in T\}
$$

is a stochastic process.

For each fixed outcome

$$
\omega\in\Omega,
$$

the sample path is the function

$$
t\mapsto X(t,\omega).
$$

If **every sample path is bounded**, then

$$
\sup_{t\in T}|X(t,\omega)|<\infty
$$

for every $\omega$.

Therefore,

$$
X(\cdot,\omega)\in\ell^\infty(T).
$$

Thus the entire stochastic process can be regarded as a map

$$
\boxed{
X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T)
}
$$

defined by

$$
\boxed{
\omega\mapsto X(\cdot,\omega).
}
$$

---

### What changed?

Originally, for every fixed $t$,

$$
X(t):\Omega\to\mathbb R
$$

is a random variable.

But when we collect all values of $t$ together, one outcome $\omega$
produces an entire function:

$$
X(\cdot,\omega)
=
\{X(t,\omega):t\in T\}.
$$

So instead of

$$
\omega\mapsto\text{a number},
$$

we now have

$$
\omega\mapsto\text{a function}.
$$

That function is one point in the metric space

$$
\ell^\infty(T).
$$

---

### The important distinction

Bounded sample paths establish that

$$
X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T)
$$

is a **well-defined map**.

They do **not**, by themselves, establish that this map is measurable.

This distinction is important because $\ell^\infty(T)$ can be a very
large function space, and measurability of the whole random function is
more subtle than measurability of each individual coordinate

$$
X(t):\Omega\to\mathbb R.
$$

This is one reason the course has introduced outer probability,
outer expectation, and asymptotic measurability.

---

### Big picture

We now have the chain

$$
\boxed{
\text{stochastic process}
\longrightarrow
\text{sample paths}
\longrightarrow
\ell^\infty(T)\text{-valued map}
}
$$

provided the sample paths are bounded.

This allows the general weak-convergence framework

$$
X_n\rightsquigarrow X
\quad\text{in a metric space }\mathbb D
$$

to be applied with

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb D=\ell^\infty(T).
}
$$

In other words, weak convergence can now describe convergence of
**entire stochastic processes**, rather than only scalar random
variables.
