## 1. Metric Spaces, Semimetrics, and $C_b(\mathbb D)$

The lecture begins by recalling the metric-space framework that will be used for weak convergence.

Let $(\mathbb D,d)$ be a **metric space**, where

$$
d:\mathbb D\times\mathbb D\to\mathbb R.
$$

For $a,b,c\in\mathbb D$, the metric $d$ satisfies

$$
d(a,b)\ge 0,
$$

$$
d(a,b)=0 \iff a=b,
$$

$$
d(a,b)=d(b,a),
$$

and the **triangle inequality**

$$
d(a,c)\le d(a,b)+d(b,c).
$$

The professor also noted that we may work with a **semimetric**, where distinct points can have distance zero. Thus,

$$
d(a,b)=0
$$

does not necessarily imply $a=b$.

> **Clarification.**
> A semimetric behaves like a metric except that different elements may be considered distance zero apart.

### Bounded Continuous Functions

For a metric space $\mathbb D$, define

$$
C_b(\mathbb D)
=
\left\{
f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb R:
f \text{ is continuous and bounded}
\right\}.
$$

Thus, for $f\in C_b(\mathbb D)$,

$$
\sup_{x\in\mathbb D}|f(x)|<\infty.
$$

The class $C_b(\mathbb D)$ will later serve as the collection of **test functions** used in the definition of weak convergence.

## 2. Borel $\sigma$-Fields, Open Sets, and Continuous Maps

We next recall the connection between the metric on $\mathbb D$, open sets, continuity, and Borel measurability.

### Open Sets

A set $O\subseteq \mathbb D$ is **open** if for every $x\in O$, there exists some $\varepsilon>0$ such that the $\varepsilon$-ball around $x$ is contained in $O$:

$$
x\in O
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\exists\,\varepsilon>0
\text{ such that }
B_\varepsilon(x)\subseteq O.
$$

Thus every point in an open set has a neighborhood that remains inside the set.

### Borel $\sigma$-Field

The **Borel $\sigma$-field** on $\mathbb D$, denoted $\mathcal B(\mathbb D)$, is the smallest $\sigma$-field containing all open subsets of $\mathbb D$.

The metric therefore determines the open sets, and the open sets generate the Borel $\sigma$-field.

### Continuous Maps Between Metric Spaces

Suppose

$$
\psi:(\mathbb D,d)\to(\mathbb E,\rho)
$$

is a map between metric spaces.

The professor used the open-set characterization of continuity:

$$
\psi \text{ is continuous}
\iff
\psi^{-1}(A)\text{ is open in }\mathbb D
$$

for every open set $A\subseteq\mathbb E$.

Here,

$$
\psi^{-1}(A)
=
\{x\in\mathbb D:\psi(x)\in A\}.
$$

> **Clarification.**
> This is the metric-space version of the familiar idea that points that are close in the domain should remain close after applying $\psi$. It is useful here because it connects continuity directly to the sets generating the Borel $\sigma$-field.

### Continuity Implies Borel Measurability

Because the Borel $\sigma$-field is generated by open sets, and a continuous map pulls open sets back to open sets,

$$
\boxed{
\psi \text{ continuous}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\psi \text{ is Borel measurable}.
}
$$

The professor emphasized that this makes Borel measurability particularly natural in the metric-space setting: both **continuity** and the **Borel $\sigma$-field** are built from open sets.

## 3. The Borel $\sigma$-Field Is Generated by $C_b(\mathbb D)$

The professor next gave a lemma connecting the Borel $\sigma$-field directly to the bounded continuous functions $C_b(\mathbb D)$.

> [!lemma] Lemma — Borel $\sigma$-Field Generated by $C_b(\mathbb D)$
> The Borel $\sigma$-field on a metric space $\mathbb D$ is the **smallest $\sigma$-field** that makes every
>
> $$
> f\in C_b(\mathbb D)
> $$
>
> measurable with respect to the Borel sets on $\mathbb R$.

Equivalently, if $\mathcal A$ is a $\sigma$-field on $\mathbb D$ for which every bounded continuous function

$$
f:\mathbb D\to\mathbb R
$$

is measurable, then necessarily

$$
\mathcal B(\mathbb D)\subseteq \mathcal A.
$$

The professor emphasized that this fits naturally with the previous discussion: both the Borel $\sigma$-field and continuity are built from **open sets**.

This is important because weak convergence will be defined by looking at the behavior of

$$
f(X_\alpha),
\qquad f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

Thus $C_b(\mathbb D)$ is not an arbitrary collection of test functions; it is closely tied to the measurable structure of the metric space itself.

## 4. Borel Measures and Compact Sets

The lecture next introduced Borel measures and reviewed compactness in metric spaces.

### Borel Measure

A **Borel measure** $\mu$ on $\mathbb D$ is a measure defined on the Borel $\sigma$-field such that

$$
\mu(K)<\infty
$$

for every compact set $K\subseteq\mathbb D$.

The role of compact sets will become important when defining **tightness**.

### Compact Sets

A set $K\subseteq\mathbb D$ is **compact** if every open cover of $K$ has a finite subcover.

That is, if

$$
K\subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha\in A} O_\alpha,
$$

where each $O_\alpha$ is open, then there exist finitely many indices

$$
\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m
$$

such that

$$
K\subseteq
O_{\alpha_1}\cup\cdots\cup O_{\alpha_m}.
$$

The professor emphasized the idea that even if the original open cover is very large, compactness says that only **finitely many** of those open sets are actually needed to cover $K$.

### Total Boundedness

A set is **totally bounded** if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, it can be covered by finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls.

Thus, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exist

$$
x_1,\ldots,x_m
$$

such that

$$
K\subseteq
\bigcup_{i=1}^m B_\varepsilon(x_i).
$$

### Completeness

A metric space is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence has a limit in the space.

A sequence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $N$ such that

$$
d(x_n,x_m)<\varepsilon
$$

whenever $n,m\ge N$.

Completeness requires the resulting limit to remain inside the space under consideration.

### Compactness in Metric Spaces

The professor recalled the metric-space characterization

$$
\boxed{
K\text{ compact}
\iff
K\text{ complete and totally bounded}.
}
$$

She noted that in familiar finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, compactness is closely associated with the familiar **closed and bounded** condition. In more general function spaces, however, compactness can behave less intuitively, which is why the complete-and-totally-bounded characterization is useful.

> **Lecture remark.**
> Much of our usual intuition comes from working in "nice" finite-dimensional spaces. In function spaces and spaces equipped with less familiar metrics or semimetrics, compactness requires more care.

## 5. Tight Borel Probability Measures

The professor next introduced **tightness**, which describes whether essentially all of the probability mass can be captured inside compact subsets of the state space.

Let $L$ be a Borel probability measure on $\mathbb D$.

> [!definition] Definition — Tight Borel Probability Measure
> The measure $L$ is **tight** if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a compact set $K\subseteq\mathbb D$ such that
>
> $$
> L(K)\ge 1-\varepsilon.
> $$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\forall \varepsilon>0,\quad
\exists\text{ compact }K
\quad\text{such that}\quad
L(K)\ge 1-\varepsilon.
}
$$

The idea is that however small an amount of probability we are willing to leave out, we can find a **compact set containing the rest of the probability mass**.

### Tight Random Maps

Suppose

$$
X:\Omega\to\mathbb D
$$

is a Borel measurable map.

Its distribution, or law, is

$$
\mathcal L(X)=P\circ X^{-1}.
$$

> [!definition] Definition — Tight Random Map
> The random map $X$ is called **tight** if its law
>
> $$
> P\circ X^{-1}=\mathcal L(X)
> $$
>
> is a tight Borel probability measure.

Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a compact $K\subseteq\mathbb D$ such that

$$
P(X\in K)\ge 1-\varepsilon.
$$

> **Clarification.**
> Tightness is therefore a property of where the distribution of $X$ places its mass. It says that the random element cannot place a substantial amount of probability arbitrarily far outside every compact subset of $\mathbb D$.

## 6. Separability and Its Relationship to Tightness

The professor next introduced **separability** for probability measures and random maps.

### Separable Sets

A subset $D_0\subseteq \mathbb D$ is **separable** if it contains a **countable dense subset**.

That is, there exists a countable collection

$$
\{x_1,x_2,\ldots\}\subseteq D_0
$$

whose closure contains $D_0$.

> **Clarification.**
> "Dense" and "closed" are different ideas.
>
> - Dense means every point of the space can be approximated arbitrarily closely by points from the dense subset.
> - Closed means limits of convergent sequences from the set remain in the set.
>
> The lecture discussion moved between these ideas, so it is useful to keep the distinction explicit.

### Separable Probability Measures and Random Maps

> [!definition] Definition — Separable Measure / Random Map
> A Borel probability measure $L$, or a Borel measurable random map $X$, is called **separable** if there exists a **separable measurable set**
>
> $$
> D_0\subseteq \mathbb D
> $$
>
> having probability $1$.

For a measure $L$,

$$
L(D_0)=1.
$$

For a random map $X$,

$$
P(X\in D_0)=1.
$$

Thus, even if the full space $\mathbb D$ is very large or nonseparable, the random object may effectively live inside a much smaller separable subset.

### Tightness and Countable Unions of Compact Sets

The professor remarked that tightness can equivalently be expressed by saying that the probability is concentrated on a **countable union of compact sets**.

If $L$ is tight, we can choose compact sets $K_j$ so that

$$
L(K_j)\ge 1-\frac{1}{j}
$$

(or another sequence of errors tending to zero). Then

$$
L\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}K_j\right)=1.
$$

Similarly, for a tight random map $X$,

$$
P\left(
X\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}K_j
\right)=1.
$$

Since compact metric spaces are separable, a countable union of compact sets is separable. Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\text{tightness} \Longrightarrow \text{separability}.}
$$

### Complete Metric Spaces

The professor then noted that on a **complete metric space**, the converse also holds.

> [!lemma] Lemma — Separability and Tightness on Complete Metric Spaces
> On a complete metric space, separability of a Borel probability measure implies tightness.

Hence, on a complete metric space,

$$
\boxed{
\text{separability}
\iff
\text{tightness}.
}
$$

The completeness assumption matters because a set that is both

$$
\text{complete}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\text{totally bounded}
$$

is compact.

This connects the earlier metric-space concepts directly to probability: separability gives a countable way to approximate where the probability mass lives, while completeness allows those approximations to be converted into compact sets.

## 7. Weak Convergence of Arbitrary Maps

The professor next introduced the notion of convergence that will be used throughout the course.

Let

$$
(\Omega_\alpha,\mathcal A_\alpha,P_\alpha)
$$

be a net of probability spaces, and let

$$
X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\mathbb D
$$

be **arbitrary maps** into the metric space $\mathbb D$.

The maps $X_\alpha$ are not assumed to be Borel measurable. This is why the outer expectation $E^\star $, introduced in the previous lecture, appears in the definition.

> [!definition] Definition — Weak Convergence
> We say that $X_\alpha$ **converges weakly** to a Borel measure $L$ if
>
> $$
> E^\star f(X_\alpha)
> \longrightarrow
> \int_{\mathbb D} f\,dL
> \qquad
> \text{for every }f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
> $$
>
> We write
>
> $$
> X_\alpha \rightsquigarrow L.
> $$

The two sides have slightly different roles:

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
$$

uses an **outer expectation** because the composition

$$
f\circ X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\mathbb R
$$

may fail to be measurable, whereas

$$
\int_{\mathbb D} f\,dL
$$

is an ordinary integral with respect to the Borel measure $L$.

### Connection to Classical Convergence in Distribution

The professor connected this definition to the usual convergence-in-distribution theory from probability.

For ordinary measurable real-valued random variables, we are used to statements such as

$$
X_n \xrightarrow{d} X,
$$

which can be characterized using distribution functions at continuity points or, under the usual assumptions, using characteristic functions.

Here the perspective is more general: instead of using a particular representation such as the CDF or characteristic function, we test convergence against **every bounded continuous function**

$$
f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

Thus the basic object is

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
\longrightarrow
\int f\,dL.
$$

This formulation works even when $\mathbb D$ is a complicated metric or function space and when $X_\alpha$ itself is not measurable.

> **Clarification.**
> The role of $f\in C_b(\mathbb D)$ is to turn the possibly complicated object $X_\alpha$ into a real-valued quantity that we can compare across $\alpha$. Weak convergence says that **all of these bounded continuous probes have the correct limiting expectations**.

## 8. Nets and Directed Sets

The professor paused after defining weak convergence to explain why the sequence is indexed by $\alpha$ rather than simply by

$$
n=1,2,3,\ldots
$$

The collection

$$
\{X_\alpha:\alpha\in A\}
$$

is called a **net**.

A sequence is a special case of a net, where the index set is

$$
A=\mathbb N
$$

with the usual ordering.

### Directed Sets

For a general net, the index set $A$ is a **directed set** equipped with an order, which we may write as

$$
\preceq.
$$

The defining property is that for every pair

$$
\alpha_1,\alpha_2\in A,
$$

there exists some

$$
\alpha_3\in A
$$

such that

$$
\alpha_1\preceq \alpha_3
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\alpha_2\preceq \alpha_3.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\forall \alpha_1,\alpha_2\in A,\quad
\exists \alpha_3\in A
\text{ with }
\alpha_1\preceq\alpha_3,\;
\alpha_2\preceq\alpha_3.
}
$$

Importantly, $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ themselves do **not** have to be comparable.

The professor suggested thinking of a graph-like structure: two nodes might not be ordered relative to one another, but there can still be a later or deeper node that comes after both.

### The Order Need Not Mean “Numerically Larger”

The professor emphasized that the ordering in a directed set does not have to mean ordinary numerical size.

For example, with functions or other complicated objects, it may not make sense to say that one object is simply “larger” than another. Instead, the ordering can represent some feature or property that we care about.

In her words, the order can represent a feature that we value and that gives the objects a meaningful direction.

Thus the point of the ordering is not necessarily

$$
\text{smaller} \longrightarrow \text{larger},
$$

but rather

$$
\text{earlier/less developed}
\longrightarrow
\text{later/more developed}
$$

according to whatever structure is relevant to the problem.

> **Clarification.**
> The essential feature is that no matter which two indices we start from, we can always move far enough forward in the net to reach an index that comes after both. This is what allows a notion of “eventually” and therefore a notion of convergence.

### Sequences as a Special Case

For an ordinary sequence,

$$
A=\mathbb N,
$$

and given $n_1,n_2\in\mathbb N$, we can simply take

$$
n_3=\max(n_1,n_2).
$$

Then

$$
n_1\le n_3,
\qquad
n_2\le n_3.
$$

So every sequence is automatically a net, but nets allow more general indexing structures than

$$
1,2,3,\ldots
$$

This is why the weak-convergence theory is written using $X_\alpha$: all of the sequence results are included as a special case.

## 9. Connection with Classical Convergence in Distribution

After introducing nets, the professor returned to the question of how this definition of weak convergence relates to the **classical convergence in distribution** from probability theory.

For real-valued random variables, we are familiar with statements such as

$$
X_n \xrightarrow{d} X.
$$

Classically, one way to characterize this is through the cumulative distribution functions:

$$
F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)
$$

at every continuity point $x$ of $F_X$.

The professor also recalled the corresponding characterization using **characteristic functions**:

$$
\varphi_{X_n}(t)
=
E\left[e^{itX_n}\right]
\longrightarrow
E\left[e^{itX}\right]
=
\varphi_X(t),
$$

for every $t$.

The important observation is that

$$
x\mapsto e^{itx}
$$

is a bounded continuous function. Thus characteristic functions test convergence using a particular family of functions from $C_b(\mathbb R)$.

### Why Use $C_b(\mathbb D)$ as the Definition?

The definition adopted in this course is

$$
E^\star f(X_\alpha)
\longrightarrow
\int f\,dL
\qquad
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

The professor's motivation was that this gives a formulation that works directly on an **arbitrary metric space** $\mathbb D$.

In $\mathbb R$, we can talk naturally about things such as

- cumulative distribution functions,
- continuity points of those distribution functions,
- and characteristic functions.

But once the random objects live in more complicated spaces—especially function spaces—there may be no useful analogue of a one-dimensional distribution function.

Using bounded continuous functions avoids having to construct such an analogue.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Weak convergence is defined directly through }
C_b(\mathbb D).
}
$$

So the definition is consistent with the classical theory, but is formulated in a way that continues to make sense when the state space is no longer $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb R^k$.

> **Clarification.**
> The lecture was not claiming that convergence of an arbitrary small collection of bounded continuous functions is always enough. In classical settings, special theorems tell us that particular classes—such as characteristic functions—determine the distribution. The general definition avoids relying on such a special representation.

## 10. Weak Convergence of Borel Measures

At the end of the lecture, the professor gave the corresponding definition of weak convergence when the objects themselves are **Borel measures** rather than random maps.

Let

$$
\{L_\alpha:\alpha\in A\}
$$

be a net of Borel measures on the metric space $\mathbb D$, and let $L$ be another Borel measure on $\mathbb D$.

> [!definition] Definition — Weak Convergence of Borel Measures
> We say that
>
> $$
> L_\alpha \rightsquigarrow L
> $$
>
> if
>
> $$
> \int_{\mathbb D} f\,dL_\alpha
> \longrightarrow
> \int_{\mathbb D} f\,dL
> \qquad
> \text{for every }f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
> $$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
L_\alpha \rightsquigarrow L
\iff
\int f\,dL_\alpha
\to
\int f\,dL
\quad
\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
}
$$

The professor described this as essentially the same idea as the previous definition: instead of studying a net of random elements $X_\alpha$, we directly study a net of Borel measures $L_\alpha$.

> **Clarification.**
> Notice that there is no outer expectation $E^\star $ in this definition. The objects $L_\alpha$ are already Borel measures, and every
>
> $$
> f\in C_b(\mathbb D)
> $$
>
> is Borel measurable, so the ordinary integrals
>
> $$
> \int f\,dL_\alpha
> $$
>
> are well defined.

This gives the two closely related forms of weak convergence introduced in the lecture:

$$
X_\alpha \rightsquigarrow L
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
E^\star f(X_\alpha)\to\int f\,dL,
$$

for arbitrary maps $X_\alpha$, and

$$
L_\alpha \rightsquigarrow L
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\int f\,dL_\alpha\to\int f\,dL,
$$

when the objects are Borel measures themselves.
