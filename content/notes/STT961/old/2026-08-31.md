# Arbitrary Maps and the Measurability Problem

Let

$$
(\Omega,\mathcal A,P)
$$

be an arbitrary probability space, and consider a map

$$
T:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}
      =[-\infty,\infty].
$$

The important point is that **we are not assuming that $T$ is measurable**.

Normally, if $T$ is a random variable, we can write

$$
ET=\int_\Omega T\,dP.
$$

But this requires $T$ to be measurable. If $T$ is an arbitrary map, the ordinary expectation may not be defined.

This creates a problem because weak-convergence and empirical-process arguments will sometimes involve objects for which measurability is difficult or inconvenient to establish.

The strategy introduced in this lecture is therefore:

> Instead of requiring $T$ itself to be measurable, approximate it using measurable functions.

This leads to the **outer expectation**

$$
E^\star T,
$$

which will be defined using measurable functions lying above $T$.

### Main idea

Ordinary expectation:

$$
T\text{ measurable}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
ET.
$$

More general situation:

$$
T\text{ arbitrary}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E^\star T.
$$

Thus outer expectation lets us continue doing integration-like probability calculations even when measurability is not immediately available.

### Why this matters later

This is not merely a technical redefinition of expectation.

The lecture immediately uses this machinery to develop a version of **Fubini's theorem for potentially non-measurable maps**

$$
T:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}.
$$

So the measurability issue is being handled now because it will matter when expectations are taken over empirical-process objects later in the course.

# Outer Expectation

Let

$$
(\Omega,\mathcal A,P)
$$

be a probability space and let

$$
T:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}
$$

be an arbitrary map. We do **not** require $T$ itself to be measurable.

## Definition

The **outer expectation** (or outer integral) of $T$ with respect to $P$ is

$$
E^\star T
=
\inf
\left\{
EU:
U\geq T,\;
U:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}\text{ is measurable},
\;
EU\text{ exists}
\right\}.
$$

So we consider every measurable function $U$ that lies above $T$, compute its ordinary expectation, and take the infimum.

## Picture to keep in mind

Think of $T$ as possibly being too badly behaved to integrate directly.

We instead put a **measurable cover** over it:

$$
T(\omega)\leq U(\omega)
\qquad\text{for every }\omega.
$$

Among all such measurable covers, $E^\star T$ asks for the smallest achievable expectation:

$$
T
\quad\underset{\text{covered by}}{\leq}\quad
U
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
EU
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\inf_U EU.
$$

The infimum is important: there does not initially have to be a single measurable $U$ that actually attains the smallest value.

## If $T$ is measurable

If $T$ is already measurable and $ET$ exists, we can choose

$$
U=T.
$$

Hence

$$
E^\star T\leq ET.
$$

But every admissible $U$ satisfies $U\geq T$, so monotonicity of expectation gives

$$
EU\geq ET.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{E^\star T=ET}
$$

when $T$ is measurable.

Thus outer expectation extends ordinary expectation rather than replacing it.

## Key distinction

The definition does **not** make $T$ measurable.

Instead, it defines an expectation-like quantity for $T$ by comparing $T$ with measurable functions.

# Outer Probability

Outer probability applies the same basic idea as outer expectation, but now to **sets** rather than arbitrary real-valued maps.

Let

$$
(\Omega,\mathcal A,P)
$$

be a probability space, and let

$$
B\subseteq \Omega
$$

be an arbitrary set.

The important point is that we do **not** require

$$
B\in\mathcal A.
$$

Therefore $P(B)$ may not be defined.

## Definition

The **outer probability** of $B$ is

$$
P^\star (B)
=
\inf
\left\{
P(A):
B\subseteq A,\;
A\in\mathcal A
\right\}.
$$

We cover $B$ from above by measurable sets $A$, compute their probabilities, and take the smallest possible value in the sense of an infimum.

## Connection to outer expectation

The constructions are parallel.

For a possibly nonmeasurable function:

$$
T\leq U,
\qquad U\text{ measurable},
$$

and

$$
E^\star T
=
\inf_{U\geq T}EU.
$$

For a possibly nonmeasurable set:

$$
B\subseteq A,
\qquad A\in\mathcal A,
$$

and

$$
P^\star (B)
=
\inf_{A\supseteq B}P(A).
$$

So both constructions have the form

$$
\boxed{\text{cover from above by something measurable, then take an infimum}.}
$$

## If $B$ is measurable

Suppose

$$
B\in\mathcal A.
$$

Then $A=B$ itself is an admissible measurable cover.

Therefore

$$
P^\star (B)\leq P(B).
$$

On the other hand, every admissible $A$ satisfies

$$
B\subseteq A,
$$

so monotonicity of probability gives

$$
P(B)\leq P(A).
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{P^\star (B)=P(B)}.
$$

Thus outer probability extends ordinary probability to arbitrary subsets of $\Omega$.

## Another useful connection

Since probability of a set can be written using its indicator,

$$
P(A)=E[\mathbf 1_A],
$$

outer probability is closely related to outer expectation.

Informally,

$$
P^\star (B)
$$

plays the role of the probability of $B$ even when $B$ itself is not measurable.

# Inner Expectation and Inner Probability

After defining outer expectation and outer probability, the lecture introduced the corresponding **inner** versions.

## Inner expectation

The inner expectation of an arbitrary map

$$
T:\Omega\to\overline{\mathbb R}
$$

is defined by

$$
\boxed{
E_\star T=-E^\star (-T)
}
$$

where $E^\star $ is the outer expectation.

The idea is that outer expectation approximates $T$ **from above**, while inner expectation corresponds to approximating $T$ **from below**.

Recall

$$
E^\star T
=
\inf\{EU:U\ge T,\ U\text{ measurable}\}.
$$

Now apply this definition to $-T$:

$$
E^\star (-T)
=
\inf\{EU:U\ge -T,\ U\text{ measurable}\}.
$$

Multiplying the inequality by $-1$ reverses it:

$$
-U\le T.
$$

Therefore

$$
-E^\star (-T)
$$

corresponds to taking the largest expectation obtainable from measurable functions lying below $T$.

Informally,

$$
\boxed{
E_\star T
=
\sup\{EV:V\le T,\ V\text{ measurable}\}.
}
$$

So we have the picture

$$
E_\star T
\qquad\text{from below}
$$

and

$$
E^\star T
\qquad\text{from above}.
$$

When $T$ is measurable and its expectation exists,

$$
E_\star T=ET=E^\star T.
$$

---

## Inner probability

For an arbitrary set $B\subseteq\Omega$, the lecture defined

$$
\boxed{
P_\star (B)
=
1-P^\star (\Omega\setminus B).
}
$$

So instead of covering $B$ from above, we look at the complement and use outer probability there.

The parallel is

$$
P^\star (B)
$$

for an outer approximation of $B$, and

$$
P_\star (B)
$$

for an inner approximation.

If $B$ is measurable, then

$$
P_\star (B)=P(B)=P^\star (B).
$$

## The important structural picture

For functions:

$$
\boxed{
E_\star T\le E^\star T
}
$$

and, when ordinary expectation is available,

$$
E_\star T=ET=E^\star T.
$$

For sets:

$$
\boxed{
P_\star (B)\le P^\star (B)
}
$$

and, when $B$ is measurable,

$$
P_\star (B)=P(B)=P^\star (B).
$$

Thus outer quantities approximate from above and inner quantities approximate from below.

# Minimal Measurable Majorant

Recall that outer expectation was defined by searching over all measurable functions lying above an arbitrary map $T$:

$$
E^\star T
=
\inf\{EU:U\ge T,\ U\text{ measurable}\}.
$$

A natural question is:

> Is there actually a "best" measurable function lying above $T$?

The lecture's next lemma says yes.

## Lemma: measurable cover

## What "majorant" means

A majorant is simply a function lying above another function:

$$
U\ge T.
$$

So $T^\star $ is:

1. measurable,
2. above $T$, and
3. no larger, almost surely, than any other measurable function that lies above $T$.

Schematically,

$$
T
\le
T^\star
\le
U
\qquad\text{a.s.}
$$

for every measurable majorant $U$.

## Connection to outer expectation

Previously,

$$
E^\star T
=
\inf_{U\ge T}EU.
$$

The lemma tells us that the measurable envelope $T^\star $ realizes this outer expectation:

$$
\boxed{
E^\star T=ET^\star
}
$$

provided the relevant expectation exists.

So instead of thinking only about an infimum over many possible measurable covers, we can work with the particular measurable cover $T^\star $.

## Why "minimal" rather than "minimum"?

The comparison

$$
T^\star\le U
$$

holds **almost surely**.

Therefore $T^\star $ is not necessarily a unique pointwise function. Two versions may differ on a set of probability zero.

What is unique is its almost-sure equivalence class.

That is why the lecture notes mention that there is a whole equivalence class of almost-surely equal functions representing the minimal measurable majorant.

## Maximal measurable minorant

The corresponding measurable approximation from below is

$$
\boxed{
T_\star =-(-T)^\star .
}
$$

This is called the **maximal measurable minorant**.

Thus we have

$$
T_\star
\le
T
\le
T^\star ,
$$

with $T_\star $ providing the measurable approximation from below and $T^\star $ the measurable approximation from above.

If $T$ itself is measurable, then up to almost-sure equality,

$$
T_\star =T=T^\star .
$$

# Classical Fubini Theorem

The lecture next moves from a single probability space to a **product probability space**.

Let

$$
(\Omega_1,\mathcal A_1,P_1)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(\Omega_2,\mathcal A_2,P_2)
$$

be probability spaces, and consider a map

$$
T:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}.
$$

We now have two coordinates,

$$
(\omega_1,\omega_2)\in\Omega_1\times\Omega_2,
$$

and can ask whether integrating over the two variables separately gives the same result as integrating over the product space.

## Classical measurable case

For a measurable function $f$, Fubini's theorem allows us to replace an integral over the product space by iterated integrals:

$$
\int_{A\times B}f(x,y)\,d(P_1\times P_2)(x,y)
=
\int_A
\left[
\int_B f(x,y)\,dP_2(y)
\right]
dP_1(x).
$$

In expectation notation, the basic idea is

$$
\boxed{
E_{1,2}T
=
E_1[E_2T].
}
$$

That is:

1. Fix $\omega_1$.
2. Integrate over $\omega_2$.
3. The result is now a function of $\omega_1$.
4. Integrate that function over $\omega_1$.

## Thinking in sections

For fixed $\omega_1$, define the section

$$
T_{\omega_1}(\omega_2)
=
T(\omega_1,\omega_2).
$$

Then

$$
E_2T(\omega_1,\cdot)
$$

means that we hold $\omega_1$ fixed and integrate over the second probability space.

This produces the map

$$
\omega_1
\longmapsto
E_2[T(\omega_1,\cdot)].
$$

We can then take its expectation with respect to $P_1$.

So Fubini lets us move between

$$
\boxed{\text{one integral on the product space}}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\text{two successive one-coordinate integrals}.}
$$

## Why this appears here

Everything works cleanly in the classical situation because measurability allows the relevant integrals and sections to behave properly.

But the lecture has deliberately allowed

$$
T:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}
$$

to be an **arbitrary map**.

The next question is therefore:

> What survives from Fubini's theorem when $T$ is not necessarily measurable?

This is where outer expectation $E^\star $ becomes useful.

# Fubini with Outer and Inner Expectations

For an arbitrary map

$$
T:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R},
$$

we no longer assume that $T$ is measurable.

Therefore classical Fubini's equality

$$
ET=E_1E_2T
$$

cannot simply be used.

Instead, the lecture gives a chain of inequalities involving inner and outer expectations:

$$
\boxed{
E_\star T
\le
E_{1*}E_{2*}T
\le
E_1^\star E_2^\star T
\le
E^\star T.
}
$$

Here $E^\star $ and $E_\star $ refer to outer and inner expectation on the
product space, while $E_1^\star ,E_{1*}$ and $E_2^\star ,E_{2*}$ refer to
outer and inner expectations with respect to the individual
coordinates.

## First integrate over the second coordinate

Fix

$$
\omega_1\in\Omega_1.
$$

Then consider the section

$$
\omega_2\mapsto T(\omega_1,\omega_2).
$$

Because this section need not be measurable, we use its outer
expectation:

$$
(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1)
=
\inf
\left\{
\int_{\Omega_2}U(\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2):
\begin{array}{l}
U:\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}
\text{ measurable},\\
U(\omega_2)\ge T(\omega_1,\omega_2)
\end{array}
\right\}.
$$

Thus

$$
E_2^\star T
$$

is no longer a number: it is a map

$$
\omega_1\mapsto(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1).
$$

## Then integrate over the first coordinate

There is another possible measurability problem:

$$
\omega_1\mapsto(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1)
$$

need not itself be measurable.

So we again use outer expectation:

$$
\boxed{
E_1^\star E_2^\star T.
}
$$

This means:

1. Fix $\omega_1$.
2. Take the outer expectation over $\omega_2$.
3. Regard the result as a function of $\omega_1$.
4. Take the outer expectation of that function over $\omega_1$.

## The important inequality

The part of the theorem emphasized in the lecture proof is

$$
\boxed{
E_1^\star E_2^\star T\le E^\star T.
}
$$

So iterating outer expectations cannot exceed the outer expectation
computed directly on the product space.

This replaces the familiar Fubini equality

$$
E_1E_2T=ET
$$

with an inequality when measurability is not available.

## Why equality can fail

In the measurable setting, the two routes agree:

$$
\text{product integral}
=
\text{iterated integral}.
$$

In the nonmeasurable setting, taking a measurable cover at one stage
and then another measurable cover at the next stage is not necessarily
the same operation as taking one measurable cover directly on the
product space.

Therefore we obtain inequalities rather than automatically obtaining
equality.

## The full picture

The theorem sandwiches the iterated quantities between the inner and
outer expectations on the product space:

$$
E_\star T
\le
\underbrace{E_{1*}E_{2*}T}_{\text{iterated inner}}
\le
\underbrace{E_1^\star E_2^\star T}_{\text{iterated outer}}
\le
E^\star T.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
\text{inner product expectation}
\le
\text{iterated expectations}
\le
\text{outer product expectation}.
}
$$

# Proof of the Outer-Fubini Upper Bound

The lecture proves the inequality

$$
\boxed{
E_1^\star E_2^\star T\le E^\star T.
}
$$

The strategy is to use the **minimal measurable majorant** $T^\star $ of $T$.

## Step 1: Replace $T$ by its measurable majorant

Assume

$$
E^\star T<\infty.
$$

By the measurable-majorant lemma, there exists a measurable map

$$
T^\star :\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\overline{\mathbb R}
$$

such that

$$
T^\star\ge T
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
E^\star T=ET^\star .
}
$$

Thus $T^\star $ is a measurable function on the full product space that
covers the possibly nonmeasurable $T$.

## Step 2: Fix $\omega_1$

For almost every $\omega_1$, consider the section

$$
\omega_2\mapsto T^\star (\omega_1,\omega_2).
$$

Because $T^\star $ is measurable on the product space, this section is
measurable in $\omega_2$.

Also,

$$
T^\star (\omega_1,\omega_2)
\ge
T(\omega_1,\omega_2).
$$

Therefore the section $T^\star (\omega_1,\cdot)$ is a measurable
majorant of

$$
T_{\omega_1}(\omega_2)=T(\omega_1,\omega_2).
$$

## Step 3: Compare with the outer expectation over $\Omega_2$

Recall that

$$
(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1)
$$

is the infimum of the expectations of all measurable majorants of
$T_{\omega_1}$.

Since $T^\star (\omega_1,\cdot)$ is one such majorant,

$$
\boxed{
(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1)
\le
\int_{\Omega_2}
T^\star (\omega_1,\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2)
}
$$

for almost every $\omega_1$.

Define

$$
g(\omega_1)
=
\int_{\Omega_2}
T^\star (\omega_1,\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2).
$$

Then

$$
E_2^\star T\le g
\qquad\text{a.s.}
$$

and $g$ is measurable.

Thus $g$ serves, up to a null-set modification if necessary, as a
measurable majorant of $E_2^\star T$.

## Step 4: Apply the outer expectation over $\Omega_1$

Since outer expectation is the infimum over measurable majorants,

$$
E_1^\star E_2^\star T
\le
E_1g.
$$

But

$$
E_1g
=
\int_{\Omega_1}
\left[
\int_{\Omega_2}
T^\star (\omega_1,\omega_2)\,dP_2(\omega_2)
\right]dP_1(\omega_1).
$$

Because $T^\star $ is measurable, classical Fubini applies, giving

$$
E_1g=ET^\star .
$$

Finally,

$$
ET^\star =E^\star T.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{
E_1^\star E_2^\star T
\le
E^\star T.
}
$$

## Proof structure to remember

The entire argument can be summarized as

$$
T
\le
T^\star
$$

which implies, section by section,

$$
E_2^\star T
\le
E_2T^\star ,
$$

and therefore

$$
E_1^\star E_2^\star T
\le
E_1E_2T^\star
=
ET^\star
=
E^\star T.
$$

So the proof is essentially:

$$
\boxed{
\text{measurable cover}
\rightarrow
\text{sectionwise bound}
\rightarrow
\text{ordinary Fubini}.
}
$$

> first we bound the iterated outer expectations $T$ by the expectation of the minimal measureable majorant $T^\star $. Then the itereated expectation collapses by classical Fubini. Then by the minimal measureable marjorant lemma, $ET^\star =E^\star T$

# Recovering Equality: A Separable-Metric-Space Condition

The previous theorem only guaranteed

$$
E_1^\star E_2^\star T\le E^\star T.
$$

The lecture next gives additional conditions under which we recover an
**equality**.

> **THEOREM — Equality under separability**
>
> Let
>
> $$
> (\Omega_1,\mathcal A_1)
> $$
>
> be a separable metric space equipped with its Borel
> $\sigma$-algebra.
>
> Suppose
>
> $$
> T:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to\mathbb R
> $$
>
> satisfies, for sufficiently close $\omega_1,\omega_1'$,
>
> $$
> |T(\omega_1,\omega_2)-T(\omega_1',\omega_2)|
> \le
> d(\omega_1,\omega_1')H(\omega_2),
> $$
>
> where
>
> $$
> E_2^\star H<\infty.
> $$
>
> Then, whenever $E^\star T<\infty$,
>
> $$
> \boxed{
> E^\star T=E_1E_2^\star T.
> }
> $$

## What is new?

The earlier theorem allowed a completely arbitrary map $T$.

Here we impose additional structure on how $T$ changes with
$\omega_1$:

$$
|T(\omega_1,\omega_2)-T(\omega_1',\omega_2)|
\le
d(\omega_1,\omega_1')H(\omega_2).
$$

For a fixed $\omega_2$, this says that changing $\omega_1$ by a
small metric distance can only change $T$ by a controlled amount.

The function

$$
H(\omega_2)
$$

controls how large that change may be, and the condition

$$
E_2^\star H<\infty
$$

ensures that this control is sufficiently well behaved with respect to
the second probability space.

## Why separability appears

A metric space $(\Omega_1,d)$ is **separable** if it contains a
countable dense subset.

That is, there exists

$$
D=\{\omega_1^{(1)},\omega_1^{(2)},\ldots\}
\subseteq\Omega_1
$$

such that every point of $\Omega_1$ can be approximated arbitrarily
closely by points in $D$.

The theorem combines:

$$
\boxed{\text{countable approximation from separability}}
$$

with

$$
\boxed{\text{control of $T$ under small changes in $\omega_1$}}.
$$

Together these conditions are sufficient for the iterated
outer-expectation construction to behave well enough that the outer
Fubini inequality becomes an equality.

## Important consequence

Notice that the theorem writes

$$
E_1E_2^\star T
$$

rather than

$$
E_1^\star E_2^\star T.
$$

So under these extra assumptions, the function

$$
\omega_1\mapsto(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1)
$$

can be handled using the ordinary expectation with respect to
$P_1$.

This removes the second outer-expectation operation.

Thus we move from the general result

$$
E_1^\star E_2^\star T\le E^\star T
$$

to

$$
\boxed{
E_1E_2^\star T=E^\star T.
}
$$

## Intuition

The problem before was that after integrating out $\omega_2$,

$$
\omega_1\mapsto(E_2^\star T)(\omega_1)
$$

might behave too badly to be measurable.

The new assumptions prevent arbitrary behavior in the
$\omega_1$-direction:

$$
d(\omega_1,\omega_1')\text{ small}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T(\omega_1,\omega_2)
\text{ and }
T(\omega_1',\omega_2)
\text{ are close}.
$$

Separability then lets us describe the space using a countable dense
subset rather than needing to control an arbitrary uncountable family
of unrelated points.
