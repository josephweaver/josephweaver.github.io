## 1. Controlling the First Piece: Failure at the Starting Level

The lecture continues the proof of the Donsker theorem using weak $L_2$ bracketing and ordinary $L_2(P)$ covering.

Recall the stopped chaining decomposition:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f-\pi_{q_0}f
={}&(f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f\\
&+\sum_{q>q_0}(f-\pi_qf)B_qf\\
&+\sum_{q>q_0}
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f.
\end{aligned}
$$

We will control the empirical-process contribution of each piece separately.

### Step 1: Identify the initial failure term

Define
$$
h_{n,f}
=
(f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f,
$$
where
$$
B_{q_0}f
=
\mathbf1\{\Delta_{q_0}f>\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}\}.
$$

This term records failure of the truncation test at the starting level.

For now, $q_0$ is fixed, so $a_{q_0}>0$ is a fixed constant.

### Step 2: Bound it by the envelope’s tail

Both $f$ and its representative $\pi_{q_0}f$ belong to $\mathcal F$. Therefore,
$$
|f-\pi_{q_0}f|\le2F.
$$

Also, the cell oscillation satisfies
$$
\Delta_{q_0}f\le2F,
$$
using compatible measurable majorants when needed.

Consequently,
$$
\{\Delta_{q_0}f>\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}\}
\subseteq
\{2F>\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}\}.
$$

Thus, uniformly over $f$,
$$
\boxed{
|h_{n,f}|
\le
2F\,\mathbf1\{2F>\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}\}.
}
$$

The initial failure term can occur only where the overall envelope is large.

### Step 3: Use a direct empirical-process bound

For any function $h$,
$$
|\mathbb G_nh|
=
\sqrt n\,|(P_n-P)h|
\le
\sqrt n\bigl(P_n|h|+P|h|\bigr).
$$

If every member of a class is bounded in absolute value by a common nonnegative function $H$, then
$$
\sup_h|\mathbb G_nh|
\le
\sqrt n(P_nH+PH).
$$

Taking expectations gives, with outer expectations where required,
$$
E^\star\sup_h|\mathbb G_nh|
\le
2\sqrt n\,P^\star H.
$$

Apply this with
$$
H=
2F\,\mathbf1\{2F>\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}\}.
$$

We obtain
$$
\boxed{
E^\star\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
|\mathbb G_nh_{n,f}|
\le
4\sqrt n\,
P^\star\!\left[
F\,\mathbf1\left\{
F>\frac{\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}}2
\right\}
\right].
}
$$

No entropy bound is needed for this piece.

### Step 4: Convert the envelope tail assumption into a tail-mean bound

The theorem assumes
$$
P^\star (F>t)=o(t^{-2}).
$$

Problem 2.5.6 in the textbook shows that this implies
$$
\boxed{
P^\star [F\mathbf1\{F>t\}]
=
o(t^{-1}).
}
$$

For a measurable nonnegative envelope, the reason is the identity
$$
P[F\mathbf1\{F>t\}]
=
tP(F>t)+\int_t^\infty P(F>s)\,ds.
$$

Given any $\eta>0$, for sufficiently large $t$,
$$
P(F>s)\le\frac{\eta}{s^2}
\qquad\text{for all }s\ge t.
$$

Hence
$$
P[F\mathbf1\{F>t\}]
\le
\frac{\eta}{t}
+
\int_t^\infty\frac{\eta}{s^2}\,ds
=
\frac{2\eta}{t}.
$$

Since $\eta$ is arbitrary, the tail mean is $o(t^{-1})$.

### Step 5: Let the sample size grow

Set
$$
t_n=\frac{\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}}2.
$$

For fixed $q_0$,
$$
t_n\to\infty,
\qquad
\frac{\sqrt n}{t_n}=\frac2{a_{q_0}}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\sqrt n\,P^\star [F\mathbf1\{F>t_n\}]
=
\frac2{a_{q_0}}
\left(t_nP^\star [F\mathbf1\{F>t_n\}]\right)
\longrightarrow0.
$$

It follows that
$$
\boxed{
E^\star\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\mathbb G_n\bigl((f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f\bigr)
\right|
\longrightarrow0
}
$$
for every fixed starting level $q_0$.

By Markov’s inequality, this contribution also converges to zero in outer probability.

### Why the little-$o$ condition matters

A bound
$$
P(F>t)=O(t^{-2})
$$
would give only
$$
P[F\mathbf1\{F>t\}]=O(t^{-1}).
$$

At $t\asymp\sqrt n$, multiplying by $\sqrt n$ would then produce an $O(1)$ bound.

The stronger assumption $o(t^{-2})$ makes this contribution vanish.

## 2. Later Failure Terms: Mean and Second-Moment Bounds

We next study
$$
(f-\pi_qf)B_qf,
\qquad q>q_0,
$$
the remainder produced when the first failed truncation test occurs at level $q$.

Define its nonnegative envelope
$$
g_{q,f}=\Delta_qf\,B_qf.
$$

Since
$$
|f-\pi_qf|\le\Delta_qf,
$$
we have
$$
|(f-\pi_qf)B_qf|\le g_{q,f}.
$$

Our immediate task is to bound the size, mean, and second moment of $g_{q,f}$.

### Step 1: Use nesting of the partitions

The level-$q$ cell containing $f$ lies inside its level-$(q-1)$ cell. Its pointwise oscillation is therefore no larger:
$$
\Delta_qf\le\Delta_{q-1}f,
$$
with compatible choices of measurable majorants.

Consequently,
$$
g_{q,f}
=
\Delta_qf B_qf
\le
\Delta_{q-1}f B_qf.
$$

### Step 2: Use passage of the preceding test

On the event $B_qf=1$, all tests before level $q$ passed. In particular,
$$
\Delta_{q-1}f\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}.
$$

Thus
$$
\boxed{
0\le g_{q,f}\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}.
}
$$

Although the test at level $q$ failed, nesting and passage at level $q-1$ still provide a finite upper bound.

### Step 3: Recall the weak $L_2$ tail-mean inequality

For a nonnegative measurable random variable $Z$,
$$
\boxed{
tP[Z\mathbf1\{Z>t\}]
\le
2\|Z\|_{P,2,\infty}^2.
}
$$

To see this, write
$$
P[Z\mathbf1\{Z>t\}]
=
tP(Z>t)+\int_t^\infty P(Z>x)\,dx.
$$

If $A=\|Z\|_{P,2,\infty}$, then
$$
P(Z>x)\le\frac{A^2}{x^2}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
P[Z\mathbf1\{Z>t\}]
\le
\frac{A^2}{t}
+
\int_t^\infty\frac{A^2}{x^2}\,dx
=
\frac{2A^2}{t}.
$$

This is the inequality from textbook Problem 2.5.5.

### Step 4: Bound the mean of the failure envelope

The first-failure indicator implies
$$
\Delta_qf>\sqrt n\,a_q.
$$

Dropping its other restrictions can only enlarge the envelope:
$$
g_{q,f}
\le
\Delta_qf\,
\mathbf1\{\Delta_qf>\sqrt n\,a_q\}.
$$

Apply the tail-mean inequality with
$$
Z=\Delta_qf,
\qquad
t=\sqrt n\,a_q.
$$

Since the partition construction gives
$$
\|\Delta_qf\|_{P,2,\infty}\le2^{-q},
$$
we obtain
$$
\sqrt n\,a_q\,Pg_{q,f}
\le
2\cdot2^{-2q}.
$$

Hence
$$
\boxed{
Pg_{q,f}
\le
\frac{2\cdot2^{-2q}}{\sqrt n\,a_q}.
}
$$

Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{
\sqrt n\,Pg_{q,f}
\le
\frac{2\cdot2^{-2q}}{a_q}.
}
$$

The factor $n^{-1/2}$ in the mean will cancel the empirical process’s factor $\sqrt n$.

### Step 5: Bound the second moment

From
$$
0\le g_{q,f}\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1},
$$
we have
$$
g_{q,f}^2
\le
\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}\,g_{q,f}.
$$

Taking expectations and using the mean bound,
$$
Pg_{q,f}^2
\le
\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}
\frac{2\cdot2^{-2q}}{\sqrt n\,a_q}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
Pg_{q,f}^2
\le
2\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}\,2^{-2q}.
}
$$

In particular,
$$
\|g_{q,f}\|_{P,2}
\le
\sqrt2\,2^{-q}
\sqrt{\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}}.
$$

This supplies the second-moment bound needed for Bernstein’s maximal inequality.

### The three bounds we will use

Uniformly over $f\in\mathcal F$,
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\|g_{q,f}\|_\infty
&\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1},\\
Pg_{q,f}
&\le\frac{2\cdot2^{-2q}}{\sqrt n\,a_q},\\
Pg_{q,f}^2
&\le2\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}\,2^{-2q}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Each bound uses a different part of the construction:

- nesting and the preceding passed test give boundedness;
- weak $L_2$ control and the failed current test give a small mean;
- boundedness combined with the mean gives a second-moment bound.

Next we will combine these bounds with the finite-class Bernstein inequality.

## 3. Applying Bernstein to the Later Failure Terms

We now combine the preceding bounds to control
$$
\sum_{q>q_0}
\mathbb G_n\bigl((f-\pi_qf)B_qf\bigr)
$$
uniformly over $f\in\mathcal F$.

Write
$$
h_{q,f}=(f-\pi_qf)B_qf,
\qquad
g_{q,f}=\Delta_qf\,B_qf.
$$

Recall that
$$
|h_{q,f}|\le g_{q,f}.
$$

### Step 1: Replace the remainder by its finite-class envelope

For each $f$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}|
&\le
\sqrt n\bigl(P_n|h_{q,f}|+P|h_{q,f}|\bigr)\\
&\le
\sqrt n(P_ng_{q,f}+Pg_{q,f})\\
&=
\mathbb G_ng_{q,f}+2\sqrt n\,Pg_{q,f}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
\sup_f|\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}|
\le
\sup_f|\mathbb G_ng_{q,f}|
+
2\sqrt n\,\sup_fPg_{q,f}.
}
$$

We keep the supremum outside the population expectation in the second term. This allows us to use the uniform mean bound from the previous section.

### Step 2: Count the envelope functions

Define
$$
\mathcal A_q
=
\{g_{q,f}:f\in\mathcal F\}.
$$

Both $\Delta_qf$ and $B_qf$ are constant as function choices within each level-$q$ partition cell.

Therefore,
$$
|\mathcal A_q|\le N_q.
$$

The original remainder class may be infinite, but its dominating envelope class is finite.

### Step 3: Apply the finite-class Bernstein bound

Let
$$
L_q=\log(2N_q).
$$

For a finite class with uniform bounds
$$
\|g\|_\infty\le b,
\qquad
\|g\|_{P,2}\le\sigma,
$$
Bernstein’s maximal inequality gives
$$
E\sup_g|\mathbb G_ng|
\le
C\left[
\frac b{\sqrt n}L_q+\sigma\sqrt{L_q}
\right].
$$

Our bounds are
$$
b=\sqrt n\,a_{q-1},
$$
and
$$
\sigma
\le
\sqrt2\,2^{-q}
\sqrt{\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}}.
$$

Thus
$$
E\sup_f|\mathbb G_ng_{q,f}|
\le
C\left[
a_{q-1}L_q
+
2^{-q}\sqrt{\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}}\sqrt{L_q}
\right].
$$

The mean correction satisfies
$$
2\sqrt n\,\sup_fPg_{q,f}
\le
\frac{4\cdot2^{-2q}}{a_q}.
$$

Combining these,
$$
\boxed{
E^\star\sup_f|\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}|
\le
C\left[
a_{q-1}L_q
+
2^{-q}\sqrt{\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}}\sqrt{L_q}
+
\frac{2^{-2q}}{a_q}
\right].
}
$$

All dependence on $n$ has canceled.

### Step 4: Substitute the chosen thresholds

Recall
$$
a_q=\frac{2^{-q}}{\sqrt{L_{q+1}}}.
$$

The first term becomes
$$
a_{q-1}L_q
=
\frac{2^{-(q-1)}}{\sqrt{L_q}}L_q
=
2^{-(q-1)}\sqrt{L_q}.
$$

The third term becomes
$$
\frac{2^{-2q}}{a_q}
=
2^{-q}\sqrt{L_{q+1}}.
$$

For the middle term, nested partitions imply that $N_q$ is nondecreasing. Hence
$$
\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}
=
2\sqrt{\frac{L_{q+1}}{L_q}}
\ge1.
$$

Using $\sqrt r\le r$ for $r\ge1$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
2^{-q}\sqrt{\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}}\sqrt{L_q}
&\le
2^{-q}\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}\sqrt{L_q}\\
&=
2^{1-q}\sqrt{L_{q+1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
E^\star\sup_f|\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}|
\le
C\,2^{-q}
\left(\sqrt{L_q}+\sqrt{L_{q+1}}\right).
}
$$

The three contributions are controlled by the same entropy-series scale, allowing for a shift of one level.

### Step 5: Sum over the failure levels

Apply the triangle inequality first to finite partial sums:
$$
E^\star\sup_f
\left|
\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{Q}\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}
\right|
\le
\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{Q}
E^\star\sup_f|\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}|.
$$

The resulting summable bounds also control the infinite series. Thus
$$
E^\star\sup_f
\left|
\sum_{q>q_0}\mathbb G_nh_{q,f}
\right|
\le
C\sum_{q>q_0}
2^{-q}\left(\sqrt{L_q}+\sqrt{L_{q+1}}\right).
$$

The partition construction guarantees
$$
\sum_{q=1}^\infty2^{-q}\sqrt{L_q}<\infty.
$$

The shifted series is finite as well, because
$$
\sum_{q>q_0}2^{-q}\sqrt{L_{q+1}}
=
2\sum_{r>q_0+1}2^{-r}\sqrt{L_r}.
$$

Both tails tend to zero as $q_0\to\infty$.

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}
\sup_n
E^\star\sup_f
\left|
\sum_{q>q_0}
\mathbb G_n\bigl((f-\pi_qf)B_qf\bigr)
\right|
=
0.
}
$$

### Why the thresholds have this form

The professor emphasized that the thresholds were chosen to make the proof work:

- the boundedness contribution produces an entropy-series term;
- the second-moment contribution is bounded at the same scale;
- the mean correction produces a shifted entropy-series term.

Their sum is controlled by the tail of a convergent series.

The entropy assumptions and truncation thresholds were designed to fit together.

## 4. Controlling the Retained Chaining Increments

The third piece of the decomposition is
$$
\sum_{q>q_0}
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f.
$$

These are the increments retained while the truncation tests continue to pass.

Define
$$
u_{q,f}
=
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f.
$$

We will apply Bernstein’s maximal inequality directly to this finite collection of increments.

### Step 1: Use nesting to bound the increment

Both representatives
$$
\pi_qf
\quad\text{and}\quad
\pi_{q-1}f
$$
belong to the same level-$(q-1)$ cell.

Consequently,
$$
|\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f|
\le
\Delta_{q-1}f.
$$

Multiplying by the continuation indicator,
$$
|u_{q,f}|
\le
\Delta_{q-1}f\,A_{q-1}f.
$$

### Step 2: Obtain the uniform boundedness bound

On the event $A_{q-1}f=1$,
$$
\Delta_{q-1}f\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}.
$$

Thus
$$
\boxed{
\|u_{q,f}\|_\infty
\le
\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}.
}
$$

This is the boundedness input for Bernstein’s inequality.

### Step 3: Obtain the ordinary $L_2(P)$ bound

The level-$(q-1)$ partition cells have $L_2(P)$ diameter at most
$$
2^{-(q-1)}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\|\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f\|_{P,2}
\le2^{-(q-1)}.
$$

Multiplication by an indicator cannot increase the norm, so
$$
\boxed{
\|u_{q,f}\|_{P,2}
\le2^{-(q-1)}.
}
$$

In particular,
$$
\operatorname{Var}(u_{q,f}(X))
\le
Pu_{q,f}^2
\le
2^{-2(q-1)}.
$$

This bound uses the ordinary $L_2(P)$ covering part of the partition construction.

### Step 4: Count the possible increments

Within a level-$q$ cell:

- $\pi_qf$ is fixed;
- its parent representative $\pi_{q-1}f$ is fixed;
- the earlier tests defining $A_{q-1}f$ are fixed.

Hence the entire function $u_{q,f}$ is fixed within that cell.

Thus
$$
\boxed{
|\{u_{q,f}:f\in\mathcal F\}|\le N_q.
}
$$

Nesting avoids multiplying separate counts for the two representatives and the indicator.

### Step 5: Apply Bernstein’s maximal inequality

Write
$$
L_q=\log(2N_q).
$$

Using the bounds above,
$$
\begin{aligned}
E\sup_f|\mathbb G_nu_{q,f}|
&\le
C\left[
\frac{\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}}{\sqrt n}L_q
+
2^{-(q-1)}\sqrt{L_q}
\right]\\
&=
C\left[
a_{q-1}L_q
+
2^{-(q-1)}\sqrt{L_q}
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Since
$$
a_{q-1}
=
\frac{2^{-(q-1)}}{\sqrt{L_q}},
$$
the first term becomes
$$
a_{q-1}L_q
=
2^{-(q-1)}\sqrt{L_q}.
$$

The two Bernstein contributions therefore have the same order:
$$
\boxed{
E\sup_f|\mathbb G_nu_{q,f}|
\le
C\,2^{-q}\sqrt{L_q}.
}
$$

Again, the bound is independent of $n$.

### Step 6: Sum the increments

Apply the triangle inequality to finite partial sums, then use the summable bounds to pass to the infinite series:
$$
\boxed{
E^\star\sup_f
\left|
\sum_{q>q_0}\mathbb G_nu_{q,f}
\right|
\le
C\sum_{q>q_0}
2^{-q}\sqrt{\log(2N_q)}.
}
$$

The partition construction guarantees
$$
\sum_{q=1}^\infty
2^{-q}\sqrt{\log(2N_q)}
<\infty.
$$

Therefore its tail tends to zero:
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}
\sup_n
E^\star\sup_f
\left|
\sum_{q>q_0}
\mathbb G_n\bigl(
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f
\bigr)
\right|
=
0.
}
$$

### Why this piece is simpler than the failure remainders

The retained increments already form a finite class at each level, with direct boundedness and second-moment bounds.

We can apply Bernstein’s maximal inequality to them directly. The failure remainders required an additional envelope comparison and a mean correction.

## 5. Completing the Donsker Proof

We now combine the bounds for all three pieces of the stopped chaining decomposition.

Our target is
$$
Z_{n,q_0}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
|\mathbb G_n(f-\pi_{q_0}f)|.
$$

We want to show that, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star (Z_{n,q_0}>\varepsilon)
=
0.
$$

### Step 1: Collect the three bounds

Let $T_{1,n,q_0}$, $T_{2,n,q_0}$, and $T_{3,n,q_0}$ denote the suprema of the empirical-process contributions from:

1. failure at the starting level;
2. failure at a later level;
3. retained chaining increments.

The decomposition and triangle inequality give
$$
Z_{n,q_0}
\le
T_{1,n,q_0}
+
T_{2,n,q_0}
+
T_{3,n,q_0}.
$$

For every fixed $q_0$,
$$
E^\star T_{1,n,q_0}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.
$$

The other two bounds are independent of $n$:
$$
E^\star T_{2,n,q_0}
+
E^\star T_{3,n,q_0}
\le
C S_{q_0},
$$
where
$$
S_{q_0}
=
\sum_{q>q_0}
2^{-q}
\left[
\sqrt{\log(2N_q)}
+
\sqrt{\log(2N_{q+1})}
\right].
$$

Entropy summability gives
$$
S_{q_0}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }q_0\to\infty.
$$

### Step 2: Take the limits in the correct order

For fixed $q_0$,
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}E^\star Z_{n,q_0}
\le
C S_{q_0}.
$$

Now let $q_0\to\infty$:
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
E^\star Z_{n,q_0}
=
0.
}
$$

By Markov’s inequality,
$$
P^\star (Z_{n,q_0}>\varepsilon)
\le
\frac{E^\star Z_{n,q_0}}{\varepsilon}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star (Z_{n,q_0}>\varepsilon)
=
0.
}
$$

This is the uniform finite-approximation property needed for tightness.

### Step 3: Convert approximation control into within-cell control

If $f,g$ belong to the same level-$q_0$ cell, then
$$
\pi_{q_0}f=\pi_{q_0}g.
$$

Hence
$$
|\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng|
\le
|\mathbb G_n(f-\pi_{q_0}f)|
+
|\mathbb G_n(g-\pi_{q_0}g)|
\le
2Z_{n,q_0}.
$$

Consequently,
$$
\max_i
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{q_0,i}}
|\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng|
\le
2Z_{n,q_0}.
$$

Given any oscillation tolerance and probability tolerance, a sufficiently fine fixed partition satisfies the finite-partition tightness criterion.

Thus $\mathbb G_n$ is asymptotically tight in
$$
\ell^\infty(\mathcal F).
$$

### Step 4: Combine tightness with finite-dimensional convergence

For every finite collection $f_1,\ldots,f_k\in\mathcal F$, the multivariate central limit theorem gives
$$
(\mathbb G_nf_1,\ldots,\mathbb G_nf_k)
\Rightarrow
(\mathbb G_Pf_1,\ldots,\mathbb G_Pf_k),
$$
where the limit is centered Gaussian with covariance
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb G_Pf,\mathbb G_Pg)
=
P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
$$

Together with asymptotic tightness, this establishes
$$
\boxed{
\mathbb G_n\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G_P
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F).
}
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\text{ is }P\text{-Donsker}.}
$$

### What each assumption accomplished

| Assumption or construction | Role in the proof |
|---|---|
| Weak $L_2$ bracketing entropy | Controls pointwise oscillation envelopes and later failure tails |
| Ordinary $L_2(P)$ covering entropy | Controls second moments of retained increments |
| Vanishing envelope tail $P^\star (F>x)=o(x^{-2})$ | Makes the initial failure contribution vanish |
| Nested partitions | Connects successive representatives and truncation tests |
| Thresholds $a_q$ | Balance the Bernstein contributions |
| Entropy-series summability | Makes the contributions from fine levels vanish |

## 6. The Professor’s Closing Perspective: How the Two Proofs Fit Together

The professor ended Section 2.5 by comparing the two Donsker theorems and explaining how their proofs were designed.

### The common structure

Both proofs combine three ingredients:

1. A tail inequality for individual process increments.
2. A maximal inequality for finite collections of increments.
3. Chaining to approximate an infinite class at successively finer scales.

The entropy integral controls the accumulated cost of those approximations.

### How the ingredients differ

| Feature | Uniform entropy theorem | Bracketing-and-covering theorem |
|---|---|---|
| Initial reduction | Symmetrization to a Rademacher process | Work directly with the empirical process |
| Tail inequality | Conditional Hoeffding inequality | Bernstein inequality |
| Maximal bounds | Sub-Gaussian bounds, associated with $\psi_2$ | Bernstein bounds, associated with $\psi_1$ |
| Geometry | Empirical $L_2(P_n)$, controlled uniformly over $Q$ | Population $L_2(P)$ covers and weak $L_2(P)$ brackets |
| Handling large values | Square-integrable envelope and empirical-radius control | Truncation and tail bounds |
| Final small quantity | An entropy integral over a shrinking interval | The tail of a convergent entropy series |

The uniform entropy proof also used the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem for squared differences to connect empirical and population geometry.

### Why the thresholds seem mysterious at first

The professor emphasized that a finished proof presents carefully selected quantities:
$$
2^{-q},
\qquad
N_q,
\qquad
a_q=\frac{2^{-q}}{\sqrt{\log(2N_{q+1})}}.
$$

Their purpose becomes clearer when we work backward from the desired bounds.

Start with a generic threshold $a_q$. Bernstein’s inequality and the tail estimates produce terms of the form
$$
a_{q-1}\log(2N_q),
$$
$$
2^{-q}
\sqrt{\frac{a_{q-1}}{a_q}}
\sqrt{\log(2N_q)},
$$
and
$$
\frac{2^{-2q}}{a_q}.
$$

Choose $a_q$ so these terms are controlled by the entropy-series scale
$$
2^{-q}\sqrt{\log(2N_q)}
$$
or its shifted counterpart.

Then the finite entropy integral guarantees that the remaining tail tends to zero.

The threshold formula is therefore a consequence of balancing the estimates.

### Choosing which theorem to apply

The two entropy conditions are sufficient conditions for being Donsker. Neither condition is generally stronger than the other.

For a particular class, ask:

- Can I obtain covering bounds uniformly over finitely supported measures $Q$?
- Can I construct brackets under the population measure $P$?
- Which envelope condition can I verify?
- Are the theorem’s measurability requirements satisfied?

The professor’s practical advice was to choose the theorem whose assumptions are easier to check for the class at hand.
