
## Chapter 1.5 — Spaces of Bounded Functions

Let $T$ be an arbitrary index set.

The space

$$
\ell^\infty(T)
$$

is the collection of all uniformly bounded real-valued functions on $T$:

$$
\boxed{
\ell^\infty(T)
=
\left\{
z:T\to\mathbb R:
\|z\|_T<\infty
\right\},
}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
\|z\|_T
=
\sup_{t\in T}|z(t)|.
}
$$

The corresponding uniform metric is

$$
d(z_1,z_2)
=
\|z_1-z_2\|_T
=
\sup_{t\in T}|z_1(t)-z_2(t)|.
$$

Thus the "points" in $\ell^\infty(T)$ are entire bounded functions.

---

### Stochastic processes as random functions

A stochastic process is an indexed collection

$$
\{X(t):t\in T\}
$$

of random variables defined on the same probability space.

For each fixed $t$,

$$
X(t):\Omega\to\mathbb R
$$

is an ordinary random variable.

For each fixed $\omega$,

$$
t\mapsto X(t,\omega)
$$

is a **sample path**.

If the sample paths are uniformly bounded,

$$
\sup_{t\in T}|X(t,\omega)|<\infty,
$$

then

$$
X(\cdot,\omega)\in\ell^\infty(T),
$$

so the entire stochastic process may be viewed as a map

$$
\boxed{
X:\Omega\to\ell^\infty(T).
}
$$

---

### Why this matters

Previously, weak convergence was developed abstractly for random
elements taking values in a metric space $\mathbb D$.

Now we specialize to

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb D=\ell^\infty(T).
}
$$

Thus

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\quad\text{in }\ell^\infty(T)
$$

means convergence of **entire random functions**, rather than merely
convergence of one real-valued random variable.

A key question now becomes:

> How can we reduce weak convergence of an infinite-dimensional
> random function to conditions involving ordinary finite-dimensional
> random vectors?

The next several results answer exactly this question.


## Lemma 1.5.2 — Coordinatewise Asymptotic Measurability

Let

$$
X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\ell^\infty(T)
$$

be an asymptotically tight net.

Then

$$
\boxed{
X_\alpha \text{ is asymptotically measurable}
\iff
X_\alpha(t) \text{ is asymptotically measurable for every } t\in T.
}
$$

### What is a coordinate?

For a fixed $t\in T$, define the coordinate projection

$$
\pi_t(z)=z(t).
$$

Then

$$
X_\alpha(t)=\pi_t(X_\alpha).
$$

So the complicated object

$$
X_\alpha\in\ell^\infty(T)
$$

is an entire random function, while

$$
X_\alpha(t)\in\mathbb R
$$

is just one ordinary real-valued coordinate of that function.

---

### Easy direction

If the entire process $X_\alpha$ is asymptotically measurable, then
each coordinate $X_\alpha(t)$ is asymptotically measurable.

This follows because the projection

$$
\pi_t:\ell^\infty(T)\to\mathbb R
$$

is continuous under the uniform norm:

$$
|\pi_t(z_1)-\pi_t(z_2)|
=
|z_1(t)-z_2(t)|
\leq
\|z_1-z_2\|_T.
$$

Thus asymptotic measurability is preserved when we look at a
coordinate.

---

### The surprising direction

Suppose instead that every individual coordinate

$$
X_\alpha(t)
$$

is asymptotically measurable.

By itself, this does **not** obviously imply that the entire map

$$
X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\ell^\infty(T)
$$

is asymptotically measurable.

The reason is that $\ell^\infty(T)$ may be nonseparable, and its Borel
$\sigma$-field can contain much more information than that supplied by
individual coordinate maps.

The additional assumption that saves us is

$$
\boxed{\text{asymptotic tightness}.}
$$

Tightness says, roughly, that asymptotically almost all of the probability
mass of $X_\alpha$ can be confined arbitrarily close to a compact subset
of $\ell^\infty(T)$.

On these compact subsets, knowing sufficiently many coordinate-based
continuous functions is enough to recover the behavior of arbitrary
bounded continuous functions.

---

### Functions depending on finitely many coordinates

Consider functions of the form

$$
f(z)
=
g\bigl(z(t_1),\ldots,z(t_k)\bigr),
$$

where

$$
g\in C_b(\mathbb R^k),
\qquad
t_1,\ldots,t_k\in T.
$$

These functions inspect only finitely many coordinates of $z$.

Let $\mathcal F$ be the collection of all such functions.

The important properties are:

1. $\mathcal F$ is an algebra;
2. $\mathcal F$ contains the constant functions;
3. $\mathcal F$ separates points of $\ell^\infty(T)$.

To see the third point, if

$$
z_1\neq z_2,
$$

then there must be some $t\in T$ such that

$$
z_1(t)\neq z_2(t).
$$

Therefore a function depending on that coordinate can distinguish the
two functions.

---

### Why the lemma works

If every coordinate $X_\alpha(t)$ is asymptotically measurable, then
every finite vector

$$
\bigl(
X_\alpha(t_1),\ldots,X_\alpha(t_k)
\bigr)
$$

is asymptotically measurable.

Consequently,

$$
g\bigl(
X_\alpha(t_1),\ldots,X_\alpha(t_k)
\bigr)
$$

is asymptotically measurable for every finite-coordinate function
$f\in\mathcal F$.

Because

- $X_\alpha$ is asymptotically tight, and
- $\mathcal F$ is an algebra that separates points,

Lemma 1.3.13 applies and upgrades this limited coordinatewise
measurability to asymptotic measurability of the entire random function.

---

### Main intuition

The theorem is saying:

> Once tightness keeps the random functions from wandering through all
> of the enormous space $\ell^\infty(T)$, checking measurability one
> coordinate at a time is enough.

So there are two different jobs:

$$
\boxed{\text{coordinates provide measurability information}}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\text{tightness controls the infinite-dimensional space}.}
$$

Together they let us treat $X_\alpha$ as an asymptotically measurable
random element of $\ell^\infty(T)$.
