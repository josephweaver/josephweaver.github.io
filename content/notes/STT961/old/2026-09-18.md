
# September 18 — Maximal Inequalities and Covering Numbers



The lecture connected finite maxima to suprema over an infinite index set.

### The main progression

1. **Finite maxima:** the Orlicz maximal inequality gives

   $$
   \left\|\max_{1\le i\le m}|X_i|\right\|_\psi
   \le
   K\psi^{-1}(m)\max_i\|X_i\|_\psi.
   $$
2. **Geometry:** covering and packing numbers count how many representatives are needed at a given resolution.
3. **Process fluctuations:** an increment bound

   $$
   \|X_s-X_t\|_\psi\le C\,d(s,t)
   $$

   combines with packing numbers to control

   $$
   \sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|.
   $$

The resulting entropy integral accounts for complexity across successive distance scales.

## 1. Completing the maximal inequality

Let

$$
M=\max_{1\le i\le m}|X_i|,
\qquad
a=\max_{1\le i\le m}\|X_i\|_\psi.
$$

We want to prove that

$$
\|M\|_\psi
\le K\psi^{-1}(m)a,
$$

where $K$ depends only on $\psi$. Independence is not required.

### The temporary assumptions

We first work under the stronger growth condition

$$
\psi(x)\psi(y)\le \psi(c_0xy),
\qquad x,y\ge1,
$$

and assume that

$$
\psi(1)\le\frac12.
$$

These assumptions simplify the proof. The normalization needed for the general case comes afterward.

### Recall the bound from September 16

For $y\ge1$ and $C>0$, we obtained

$$
\max_{1\le i\le m}
\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Cy}\right)
\le
\frac{1}{\psi(y)}
\sum_{i=1}^m
\psi\left(\frac{c_0|X_i|}{C}\right)
+\psi(1).
$$

Because $\psi$ is nondecreasing,

$$
\psi\left(\frac{M}{Cy}\right)
=
\max_{1\le i\le m}
\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Cy}\right).
$$

Thus, taking expectations gives

$$
E\psi\left(\frac{M}{Cy}\right)
\le
\frac{1}{\psi(y)}
\sum_{i=1}^m
E\psi\left(\frac{c_0|X_i|}{C}\right)
+\psi(1).
$$

### Choose $C$ using the individual Orlicz norms

Assume $a<\infty$. Choose any $A>a$ and set

$$
C=c_0A.
$$

Then

$$
E\psi\left(\frac{c_0|X_i|}{C}\right)
=
E\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{A}\right)
\le1
$$

for every $i$. Consequently,

$$
E\psi\left(\frac{M}{c_0Ay}\right)
\le
\frac{m}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1).
$$

**Professor’s key step:** choose the remaining scale $y$ so that the right-hand side is at most $1$. This makes the denominator an admissible scale in the definition of the Orlicz norm.

### Choose $y$ to control the sum

Set

$$
y=\psi^{-1}(2m).
$$

Then $\psi(y)=2m$, so

$$
E\psi\left(
\frac{M}{c_0A\psi^{-1}(2m)}
\right)
\le
\frac12+\frac12=1.
$$

By the definition of the Orlicz norm,

$$
\|M\|_\psi
\le c_0A\psi^{-1}(2m).
$$

Letting $A\downarrow a$ yields

$$
\|M\|_\psi
\le c_0a\psi^{-1}(2m).
$$

### Replace $2m$ by $m$

Convexity and $\psi(0)=0$ imply

$$
\psi(2u)\ge2\psi(u).
$$

Taking $u=\psi^{-1}(m)$ gives

$$
\psi\left(2\psi^{-1}(m)\right)\ge2m,
$$

and therefore

$$
\psi^{-1}(2m)\le2\psi^{-1}(m).
$$

We conclude that

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\max_{1\le i\le m}|X_i|\right\|_\psi
\le
2c_0\psi^{-1}(m)
\max_{1\le i\le m}\|X_i\|_\psi.
}
$$

### Interpretation

The factor $\psi^{-1}(m)$ describes the cost of taking a maximum over $m$ random variables.

For example, if

$$
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,
$$

then

$$
\psi_p^{-1}(m)=\bigl[\log(1+m)\bigr]^{1/p}.
$$

Thus the maximum grows at a logarithmic rate for $\psi_1$, and at a square-root logarithmic rate for $\psi_2$.

## 2. Removing the Temporary Assumptions

The previous proof assumed

$$
\psi(1)\le\frac12
$$

and

$$
\psi(x)\psi(y)\le\psi(cxy),
\qquad x,y\ge1.
$$

The actual maximal inequality requires only

$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}
\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(cxy)}
<\infty
$$

for some $c>0$.

How do we pass from this asymptotic condition to the assumptions used in the proof?

### Rescale the Orlicz function

Introduce

$$
\phi(x)=U\psi(Tx),
$$

where $T>0$ and $0<U\le1$ are fixed constants.

The two constants have different purposes:

- $T$ moves the arguments into the region where the asymptotic growth bound holds.
- $U$ reduces the function's size so that the multiplicative bound has constant $1$ and $\phi(1)\le1/2$.

To see this, the limsup condition implies that there are constants $B>0$ and $R>0$ such that

$$
\psi(a)\psi(b)\le B\psi(cab),
\qquad a,b\ge R.
$$

Choose $T\ge R$. For $x,y\ge1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(x)\phi(y)
&=U^2\psi(Tx)\psi(Ty)\\
&\le U^2B\psi(cT^2xy)\\
&=UB\,\phi(cTxy).
\end{aligned}
$$

Choose $U$ small enough that

$$
UB\le1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
U\psi(T)\le\frac12.
$$

Then

$$
\phi(x)\phi(y)\le\phi(cTxy),
\qquad x,y\ge1,
$$

and

$$
\phi(1)\le\frac12.
$$

Thus $\phi$ satisfies both temporary assumptions.

### Rescaling changes the norm only by constants

Because $U\le1$,

$$
U\psi(Tx)\le\psi(Tx).
$$

Together with convexity, this gives

$$
UT\|X\|_\psi
\le
\|X\|_\phi
\le
T\|X\|_\psi.
$$

Therefore, the $\phi$-norm and the $\psi$-norm are comparable: each bounds the other up to fixed constants.

The inverse functions are also related:

$$
\phi^{-1}(m)
=
\frac1T\psi^{-1}\left(\frac mU\right).
$$

Convexity and $\psi(0)=0$ imply

$$
\psi^{-1}\left(\frac mU\right)
\le
\frac1U\psi^{-1}(m).
$$

So replacing $\psi$ by $\phi$ changes the maximum bound only through its constant.

### The general maximal inequality

Apply the previous proof to $\phi$, then translate back to $\psi$. We obtain

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\max_{1\le i\le m}|X_i|\right\|_\psi
\le
K\psi^{-1}(m)
\max_{1\le i\le m}\|X_i\|_\psi,
}
$$

where $K$ depends only on $\psi$.

**Main takeaway:** the temporary assumptions were conveniences for the proof. They are not additional restrictions in the final theorem.

### Why this does not directly handle an infinite supremum

As $m\to\infty$,

$$
\psi^{-1}(m)\to\infty.
$$

Consequently, simply applying the finite-maximum inequality to increasingly large collections generally gives a diverging bound.

For a stochastic process $\{X_t:t\in T\}$, we need to exploit the relationship between nearby indices.

This motivates covering numbers and chaining: approximate the index set at successive resolutions and control the small increments between those approximations.

## 3. Covering Numbers, Packing Numbers, and Entropy

To control a supremum over an infinite index set $T$, we first ask:

**How many points are needed to describe $T$ at resolution $\varepsilon$?**

Let $d$ be a metric or semimetric on $T$.

### Covering numbers: approximate every point

An $\varepsilon$-cover is a collection of balls of radius $\varepsilon$ whose union contains $T$.

The covering number

$$
N(\varepsilon,T,d)
$$

is the smallest number of such balls needed to cover $T$, with centers in $T$.

Equivalently, we seek points $t_1,\ldots,t_N$ such that every $t\in T$ satisfies

$$
d(t,t_j)\le\varepsilon
$$

for at least one $j$.

The centers form an **$\varepsilon$-net**.

**Interpretation:** every point in $T$ can be approximated by one of finitely many representatives, with error at most $\varepsilon$.

### Packing numbers: distinguish separated points

A set $\{t_1,\ldots,t_D\}\subseteq T$ is $\varepsilon$-separated if

$$
d(t_i,t_j)>\varepsilon,
\qquad i\ne j.
$$

The packing number

$$
D(\varepsilon,T,d)
$$

is the largest possible number of points in an $\varepsilon$-separated set, or infinity if arbitrarily large such sets exist.

**Interpretation:** packing counts how many points can remain distinguishable at resolution $\varepsilon$.

Here, the word “packing” refers to separated points. The definition does not require balls of radius $\varepsilon$ around those points to be disjoint.

### Covering and packing are closely related

With the conventions above,

$$
\boxed{
N(\varepsilon,T,d)
\le
D(\varepsilon,T,d)
\le
N(\varepsilon/2,T,d).
}
$$

#### Why is $N(\varepsilon,T,d)\le D(\varepsilon,T,d)$?

A maximal $\varepsilon$-separated set must also be an $\varepsilon$-net.

Otherwise, there would be a point farther than $\varepsilon$ from every selected point. We could add that point while preserving separation, contradicting maximality.

Thus the selected points provide a cover.

#### Why is $D(\varepsilon,T,d)\le N(\varepsilon/2,T,d)$?

Two points in the same ball of radius $\varepsilon/2$ have distance at most

$$
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
=\varepsilon.
$$

Therefore, each such ball contains at most one point of an $\varepsilon$-separated set.

### Metric entropy

The logarithms of these counts are called metric entropies:

$$
\log N(\varepsilon,T,d)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\log D(\varepsilon,T,d).
$$

The counts describe the number of representatives; their logarithms describe complexity on a logarithmic scale.

This connects to the maximal inequality. For example, a maximum over $m$ variables with bounded $\psi_2$-norm costs a factor of order

$$
\sqrt{\log(1+m)}.
$$

When $m$ is a covering or packing number, the geometry of $T$ determines that cost.

### Total boundedness

The space $(T,d)$ is **totally bounded** if

$$
N(\varepsilon,T,d)<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
$$

By the covering–packing inequalities, this is equivalent to

$$
D(\varepsilon,T,d)<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
$$

Total boundedness means that finite approximations exist at every positive resolution.

It does not mean that $T$ itself is finite or countable. For example, $[0,1]$ is uncountable but totally bounded.

As $\varepsilon$ decreases, covering and packing numbers cannot decrease. For an infinite metric space, they tend to infinity as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$; for a finite metric space, they eventually equal the number of points.

## 4. A Maximal Inequality for Stochastic Process Increments

Let $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ be a separable stochastic process, and let $d$ be a semimetric on $T$.

We now combine two kinds of information:

- **Probabilistic control:** increments are small when their indices are close.
- **Geometric control:** packing numbers describe how many indices must be considered at each resolution.

### The increment assumption

Suppose there is a constant $C$ such that

$$
\boxed{
\|X_s-X_t\|_\psi\le C\,d(s,t),
\qquad s,t\in T.
}
$$

This is a Lipschitz bound in the Orlicz norm.

It controls the size of each random increment. It does not assert a deterministic bound on every sample path.

Assume also that $\psi$ satisfies the growth condition from the finite-maximum lemma:

$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}
\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(cxy)}
<\infty
$$

for some $c>0$.

### The quantity we want to control

For $\delta>0$, define

$$
\omega_X(\delta)
=
\sup_{\substack{s,t\in T\\d(s,t)\le\delta}}
|X_s-X_t|.
$$

This is the largest fluctuation of the process between indices at distance at most $\delta$.

A bound on each individual increment does not automatically give a bound on this supremum: many pairs may be involved.

The packing numbers account for that complexity.

### Theorem 2.2.4

For every $\eta,\delta>0$,

$$
\boxed{
\|\omega_X(\delta)\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,T,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+
\delta\,
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,T,d)^2\bigr)
\right],
}
$$

where $K$ depends only on $\psi$ and the increment constant $C$.

### Understanding the two terms

#### 1. The integral: fluctuations across finer scales

The term

$$
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,T,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
$$

accumulates the cost of approximating the process at resolutions finer than $\eta$.

At resolution $\varepsilon$:

- increments have size controlled by the distance scale;
- packing numbers count the representatives needed;
- $\psi^{-1}$ converts that count into the cost of taking a maximum.

Chaining combines these costs across successive resolutions. The integral summarizes their accumulated contribution.

#### 2. The remaining term: pairs at the coarse scale

The term

$$
\delta\,
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\eta,T,d)^2\bigr)
$$

accounts for the pairs that remain at resolution $\eta$.

If there are approximately $D(\eta,T,d)$ representatives, there can be up to its square in ordered pairs.

This explains the squared packing number.

### Why are there two scales?

The parameters have different roles:

- $\delta$ specifies the distance between the original indices whose fluctuations we want to control.
- $\eta$ specifies the approximation scale used to obtain the bound.

We may choose $\eta$ to make the right-hand side useful. It need not equal $\delta$.

### Consequence: small local fluctuations

Suppose the integral is finite for some $\eta_0>0$:

$$
\int_0^{\eta_0}
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,T,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
<\infty.
$$

Then we can make the bound arbitrarily small:

1. Choose $\eta$ small enough to make the integral term small.
2. Hold $\eta$ fixed and choose $\delta$ small enough to make the second term small.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\|\omega_X(\delta)\|_\psi\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow0.
}
$$

This controls the largest local fluctuation in Orlicz norm, uniformly over the index set.

## 5. Controlling the Total Oscillation of a Process

The previous theorem controlled fluctuations between nearby indices:

$$
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|.
$$

Its global counterpart controls fluctuations over all pairs.

### The diameter of the index set

Define

$$
\operatorname{diam}(T,d)
=
\sup_{s,t\in T}d(s,t).
$$

The diameter is the largest distance between indices, possibly infinity.

### Global maximal inequality

Under the same assumptions as the previous theorem,

$$
\boxed{
\left\|
\sup_{s,t\in T}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_\psi
\le
K
\int_0^{\operatorname{diam}(T,d)}
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,T,d)\bigr)
\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

The constant $K$ depends only on $\psi$ and the constant in

$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_\psi\le C\,d(s,t).
$$

The supremum on the left is the **total oscillation** of the process.

The integral on the right accounts for the complexity of $T$ over all relevant distance scales.

### Oscillation versus absolute size

An increment bound controls how much the process varies across its index set. To control its absolute size, we also need its value at one reference point.

Choose $t_0\in T$. For every $t\in T$,

$$
|X_t|
\le
|X_{t_0}|+|X_t-X_{t_0}|.
$$

Taking suprema and using the triangle inequality for the Orlicz norm gives

$$
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|\right\|_\psi
\le
\|X_{t_0}\|_\psi
+
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t-X_{t_0}|\right\|_\psi.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|\right\|_\psi
\le
\|X_{t_0}\|_\psi
+
K
\int_0^{\operatorname{diam}(T,d)}
\psi^{-1}\bigl(D(\varepsilon,T,d)\bigr)
\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

This reference-point bound is a useful consequence of the lecture's global inequality.

### Why do we need the reference point?

Consider the constant process

$$
X_t=Z
\qquad\text{for every }t\in T.
$$

Every increment is zero:

$$
X_s-X_t=0.
$$

Thus its total oscillation is zero, regardless of how large the random variable $Z$ is.

However,

$$
\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|=|Z|.
$$

The reference value supplies the information about the process's overall level.

### Exponential Orlicz norms

For

$$
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,
\qquad p\ge1,
$$

the inverse is

$$
\psi_p^{-1}(v)=\bigl[\log(1+v)\bigr]^{1/p}.
$$

Hence the global bound becomes

$$
\left\|
\sup_{s,t\in T}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_{\psi_p}
\le
K
\int_0^{\operatorname{diam}(T,d)}
\bigl[\log(1+D(\varepsilon,T,d))\bigr]^{1/p}
\,d\varepsilon.
$$

In particular:

- For $\psi_1$, the integrand is $\log(1+D(\varepsilon,T,d))$.
- For $\psi_2$, the integrand is $\sqrt{\log(1+D(\varepsilon,T,d))}$.

This shows how metric entropy enters a bound on the fluctuations of a stochastic process.

## 6. Example: A Process Indexed by an Interval

Let

$$
T=[0,L],
\qquad d(s,t)=|s-t|,
$$

where $L>0$.

Suppose the process satisfies

$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_{\psi_p}\le C|s-t|,
\qquad
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,\quad p\ge1.
$$

We can now use the geometry of an interval to bound the process's fluctuations.

### Covering and packing an interval

A ball of radius $\varepsilon$ is an interval extending $\varepsilon$ in each direction, truncated at the endpoints of $T$.

For small $\varepsilon$, the covering and packing numbers have orders

$$
N(\varepsilon,T,d)\asymp\frac{L}{2\varepsilon},
\qquad
D(\varepsilon,T,d)\asymp\frac{L}{\varepsilon}.
$$

These describe their scale of growth rather than exact integer formulas.

For a rigorous upper bound, order any $\varepsilon$-separated points:

$$
t_1<\cdots<t_n.
$$

Since consecutive points are more than $\varepsilon$ apart,

$$
(n-1)\varepsilon<t_n-t_1\le L.
$$

Consequently,

$$
D(\varepsilon,T,d)\le1+\frac{L}{\varepsilon}.
$$

### Substitute into the maximal inequality

Recall that

$$
\psi_p^{-1}(v)=\bigl[\log(1+v)\bigr]^{1/p}.
$$

For $0<\eta\le L$, the packing bound gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&
\left\|
\sup_{|s-t|\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_{\psi_p}\\
&\quad\le
K\left[
\int_0^\eta
\left[\log\left(1+\frac{L}{\varepsilon}\right)\right]^{1/p}
\,d\varepsilon
+
\delta
\left[\log\left(1+\frac{L^2}{\eta^2}\right)\right]^{1/p}
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Fixed numerical factors from the packing estimate are absorbed into $K$.

This is the form used in the lecture's example.

### Evaluate the integral when $p=1$

For the $\psi_1$-norm, consider

$$
I(\eta)=
\int_0^\eta
\log\left(1+\frac{L}{\varepsilon}\right)
\,d\varepsilon.
$$

Integration gives

$$
\boxed{
I(\eta)
=
\eta\log\left(1+\frac{L}{\eta}\right)
+
L\log\left(1+\frac{\eta}{L}\right).
}
$$

Equivalently, the second term can be written as

$$
L\log(\eta+L)-L\log L.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&
\left\|
\sup_{|s-t|\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_{\psi_1}\\
&\quad\le K\left[
\eta\log\left(1+\frac{L}{\eta}\right)
+
L\log\left(1+\frac{\eta}{L}\right)
+
\delta\log\left(1+\frac{L^2}{\eta^2}\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
}
$$

### The singularity at zero is integrable

Although

$$
\log\left(1+\frac{L}{\varepsilon}\right)\to\infty
\qquad\text{as }\varepsilon\downarrow0,
$$

its integral is finite.

Indeed,

$$
\eta\log\left(1+\frac{L}{\eta}\right)\to0
$$

and

$$
L\log\left(1+\frac{\eta}{L}\right)\to0
$$

as $\eta\downarrow0$.

An integrand can diverge at an endpoint while still having a finite integral.

### A useful choice of approximation scale

As a consequence of the displayed bound, choose $\eta=\delta$ for $0<\delta\le L$.

Using $\log(1+x)\le x$, we obtain

$$
\boxed{
\left\|
\sup_{|s-t|\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_{\psi_1}
\le
K'\delta\left[1+\log\left(1+\frac L\delta\right)\right].
}
$$

The right-hand side tends to zero as $\delta\downarrow0$.

Thus the increment assumption, together with the interval's modest packing growth, controls the largest local fluctuation uniformly over the entire interval.

## 7.  the Professor’s Closing Comment

### Professor’s closing comment: shrinking scales

The professor emphasized that we will eventually choose a distance scale depending on $n$:

$$
\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

The goal will be to control fluctuations between increasingly close indices and obtain an asymptotic continuity property.

The interval example illustrates why logarithmic complexity is helpful:

$$
\delta\log\left(1+\frac L\delta\right)\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow0.
$$

Although the logarithm grows, the multiplying distance shrinks fast enough to make the bound vanish.

For a sequence of processes, we will also need suitable uniform control of the constants and complexity bounds. The formal asymptotic continuity argument comes later.

### What was established versus deferred?

The professor completed the finite-maximum proof and stated the process maximal inequality, then illustrated it on an interval.

The chaining proof of the process inequality was not given in this lecture.
