## 1. From Expectation Bounds to Probability Bounds

### Recall the previous symmetrization lemma

For independent, mean-zero stochastic processes $Z_i$,
independent Rademacher signs $\varepsilon_i$, and deterministic
functions $\mu_i$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
&\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)\\
&\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
2\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

provided $\Phi$ is nondecreasing and convex.

The useful choice $\Phi(t)=t$ compares expected suprema.

### Why this does not directly compare tail probabilities

To obtain a probability such as

$$
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x
\right),
$$

we might try

$$
\Phi(t)=\mathbf1\{t>x\}.
$$

This function is nondecreasing, but it is not convex.
Therefore, the Jensen step in the previous proof fails.

We need a separate symmetrization argument for probabilities.

### Statement: symmetrization for probabilities

Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ be independent stochastic processes
indexed by $\mathcal F$, and let the Rademacher signs be
independent of these processes.

For deterministic functions $\mu_i:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$,

$$
\boxed{
\beta_n(x)\,
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x
\right)
\leq
2P^\star\left(
4\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n
\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x
\right),
}
$$

for $x>0$, whenever

$$
0\leq\beta_n(x)
\leq
\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}
P\left(
\left|\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i(f)\right|<\frac{x}{2}
\right).
$$

The inequality is informative when $\beta_n(x)>0$.

Unlike the previous lemma, this statement does not require
the $Z_i$ to have mean zero. Centering will help us obtain
a useful lower bound for $\beta_n(x)$.

As before, outer probabilities are interpreted using the
textbook's product-space conventions.

### What does $\beta_n(x)$ measure?

For each fixed $f$, consider the probability that the sum
stays inside the interval

$$
\left(-\frac{x}{2},\frac{x}{2}\right).
$$

The quantity $\beta_n(x)$ is a common lower bound for these
probabilities over all $f\in\mathcal F$.

It does **not** require the entire process to be small
simultaneously:

$$
\inf_f P\left(\left|\sum_iZ_i(f)\right|<x/2\right)
$$

is different from

$$
P\left(\sup_f\left|\sum_iZ_i(f)\right|<x/2\right).
$$

The first concerns each fixed coordinate separately.
The second concerns all coordinates at once.

### Interpretation of the bound

When $\beta_n(x)>0$, we can write

$$
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x
\right)
\leq
\frac{2}{\beta_n(x)}
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
>\frac{x}{4}
\right).
$$

Thus a large deviation of the original process is controlled
by a large deviation of the randomized process, with a
smaller threshold and an additional probability factor.

### Check your understanding

Why can we not simply choose an indicator function in the
expectation symmetrization lemma?

Because its proof requires convexity, and the tail indicator
$\mathbf1\{t>x\}$ is not convex.

## 2. Bounding $\beta_n(x)$ Using Chebyshev's Inequality

The probability symmetrization lemma is useful when
$\beta_n(x)$ is bounded away from zero.

For i.i.d. mean-zero processes, Chebyshev's inequality
provides a convenient lower bound.

### Step 1: Fix one function

Suppose $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ are i.i.d. processes satisfying

$$
E[Z_i(f)]=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Z_i(f))<\infty
$$

for every $f\in\mathcal F$.

For a fixed $f$, define

$$
S_n(f)=\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i(f).
$$

Independence gives

$$
E[S_n(f)]=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(S_n(f))
=
n\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)).
$$

### Step 2: Apply Chebyshev

Chebyshev's inequality gives

$$
P\left(|S_n(f)|\geq\frac{x}{2}\right)
\leq
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(S_n(f))}{(x/2)^2}
=
\frac{4n}{x^2}\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)).
$$

Taking complements,

$$
P\left(|S_n(f)|<\frac{x}{2}\right)
\geq
1-\frac{4n}{x^2}\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)).
$$

### Step 3: Make the bound uniform over $f$

Let

$$
\sigma_{\mathcal F}^2
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)),
$$

and assume this quantity is finite.

Then

$$
\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}
P\left(|S_n(f)|<\frac{x}{2}\right)
\geq
1-\frac{4n\sigma_{\mathcal F}^2}{x^2}.
$$

Thus we may choose

$$
\boxed{
\beta_n(x)
=
\left(1-\frac{4n\sigma_{\mathcal F}^2}{x^2}\right)_+,
}
$$

where $t_+=\max(t,0)$.

The positive part ensures a nonnegative lower bound.
When the expression is negative, Chebyshev gives no
useful positive bound.

### When is this useful?

The bound is positive when

$$
x>2\sqrt n\,\sigma_{\mathcal F}.
$$

For example, if

$$
x\geq\sqrt{8n}\,\sigma_{\mathcal F},
$$

then $\beta_n(x)\geq1/2$, and symmetrization gives

$$
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x
\right)
\leq
4P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
>\frac{x}{4}
\right).
$$

A zero Chebyshev lower bound does not mean the actual
small-deviation probability is zero. It means this estimate
is too weak at that threshold.

### What happens under the $1/\sqrt n$ scaling?

Define

$$
T_n=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_iZ_i.
$$

To study the event $\|T_n\|_{\mathcal F}>t$, substitute
$x=t\sqrt n$. The lower bound becomes

$$
\beta_n(t\sqrt n)
\geq
\left(1-\frac{4\sigma_{\mathcal F}^2}{t^2}\right)_+.
$$

The factor $n$ cancels.

This is useful for classes of small increments. If the
variances of those increments become uniformly small,
then for a fixed $t>0$, this lower bound approaches $1$.

### Empirical-process example

For

$$
Z_i(f)=f(X_i)-Pf,
$$

we have

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f))
=
P(f-Pf)^2.
$$

For an increment $h=f-g$, define

$$
\rho_P(f,g)
=
\sqrt{P\bigl[(f-g)-P(f-g)\bigr]^2}.
$$

Restricting attention to pairs with $\rho_P(f,g)<\delta$
gives increment variances bounded by $\delta^2$.

Consequently, at threshold $t\sqrt n$, we can use

$$
\beta_n(t\sqrt n)
=
\left(1-\frac{4\delta^2}{t^2}\right)_+.
$$

This explains how probability symmetrization can help
control asymptotic equicontinuity.

### Check your understanding

Why does the variance of the sum grow like $n$, while
the variance of the scaled sum stays constant?

Because independence gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_iZ_i(f)\right)
=
n\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)),
$$

and dividing the sum by $\sqrt n$ divides its variance
by $n$.

## 3. Why Probability Symmetrization Works

This proof supplements the lemma stated in the handwritten
notes using the textbook argument.

Write

$$
S_Z(f)=\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i(f).
$$

Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ be an independent copy of the
original processes, and write

$$
S_Y(f)=\sum_{i=1}^nY_i(f).
$$

### Step 1: A large original sum and a small copy give a large difference

Fix a realization for which

$$
\|S_Z\|_{\mathcal F}>x.
$$

By the definition of supremum, there exists some $f\in\mathcal F$
such that

$$
|S_Z(f)|>x.
$$

For this fixed realization and function, if

$$
|S_Y(f)|<\frac{x}{2},
$$

then the reverse triangle inequality gives

$$
|S_Z(f)-S_Y(f)|
\geq
|S_Z(f)|-|S_Y(f)|
>
\frac{x}{2}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\|S_Z-S_Y\|_{\mathcal F}>\frac{x}{2}.
$$

### Step 2: Use the small-deviation probability

By the definition of $\beta_n(x)$,

$$
P_Y\left(|S_Y(f)|<\frac{x}{2}\right)
\geq
\beta_n(x)
$$

for every fixed $f$.

Thus, whenever the original process exceeds $x$, the
independent copy produces a difference exceeding $x/2$
with probability at least $\beta_n(x)$.

Integrating this bound gives

$$
\boxed{
\beta_n(x)\,
P^\star (\|S_Z\|_{\mathcal F}>x)
\leq
P_{Z,Y}^\star\left(
\|S_Z-S_Y\|_{\mathcal F}>\frac{x}{2}
\right).
}
$$

For potentially nonmeasurable suprema, the textbook uses
outer-probability inequalities on product spaces.
A measurable choice of the witnessing function $f$
is not required.

### Step 3: Insert independent random signs

The differences $Z_i-Y_i$ are independent and symmetric
as indexed processes. Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}>\frac{x}{2}
\right)\\
&\qquad=
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
>\frac{x}{2}
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

This step does not require the original processes to be
centered. Symmetry comes from exchanging independent copies.

### Step 4: Introduce the deterministic shifts

For any deterministic functions $\mu_i$,

$$
Z_i-Y_i
=
(Z_i-\mu_i)-(Y_i-\mu_i).
$$

Define

$$
A=
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F},
$$

and

$$
B=
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Y_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The triangle inequality gives

$$
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq A+B.
$$

### Step 5: Use a union bound

If $A+B>x/2$, then at least one of the following must hold:

$$
A>\frac{x}{4},
\qquad
B>\frac{x}{4}.
$$

Otherwise, their sum would be at most $x/2$.

Hence,

$$
P^\star (A+B>x/2)
\leq
P^\star (A>x/4)+P^\star (B>x/4).
$$

The two probabilities are equal because the original
sample and its copy have the same distribution. Therefore,

$$
P^\star (A+B>x/2)
\leq
2P^\star (A>x/4).
$$

No independence between $A$ and $B$ is needed for
the union bound; they share the same signs.

### Final conclusion

Combining the steps,

$$
\boxed{
\beta_n(x)\,
P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x
\right)
\leq
2P^\star\left(
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
>\frac{x}{4}
\right).
}
$$

### Where do the constants come from?

- The first halving, $x\to x/2$, allows the independent
  copy to be smaller than the original sum.
- The second halving, $x/2\to x/4$, separates two
  contributions using the triangle inequality.
- The factor $2$ comes from the union bound and the
  equal distributions of those contributions.

### Check your understanding

What replaces Jensen's inequality in this probability proof?

An event implication and a union bound:

$$
\{A+B>x/2\}
\subseteq
\{A>x/4\}\cup\{B>x/4\}.
$$

## 4. Asymptotic Equicontinuity: Controlling Small Increments

The notes now connect symmetrization to a central requirement
for empirical-process convergence: nearby functions should
produce nearby process values, uniformly over the class.

### Step 1: Recall the empirical process

Define

$$
\mathbb G_n f
=
\sqrt n\,(P_n-P)f
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-Pf].
$$

For two functions $f,g$,

$$
\mathbb G_n f-\mathbb G_n g
=
\mathbb G_n(f-g).
$$

Thus, controlling process increments is equivalent to
controlling the process on a class of differences.

### Step 2: Define the population variance semimetric

Assume the functions are square-integrable, and define

$$
\rho_P(f,g)
=
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_P(f-g)}
=
\left[
P\bigl((f-g)-P(f-g)\bigr)^2
\right]^{1/2}.
$$

This distance measures the standard deviation of the
centered difference between two functions.

It is a semimetric: distinct functions can have distance zero.
For example, if $f-g$ is constant $P$-almost surely, then

$$
\rho_P(f,g)=0.
$$

Correspondingly, $\mathbb G_n(f-g)=0$ almost surely.

This differs from the uncentered $L_2(P)$ distance, although

$$
\rho_P(f,g)\leq\|f-g\|_{P,2}.
$$

### Step 3: Collect all small differences

For $\delta>0$, define

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=
\{f-g:\ f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \rho_P(f,g)<\delta\}.
$$

Then

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}
=
\sup_{\substack{f,g\in\mathcal F\\
\rho_P(f,g)<\delta}}
|\mathbb G_n f-\mathbb G_n g|.
$$

This is the largest process fluctuation between functions
that are close in the population variance semimetric.

### Step 4: State asymptotic equicontinuity

The probability formulation is

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star\left(
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}>\eta
\right)
=
0
\qquad\text{for every }\eta>0.
}
$$

Interpretation: for any fixed tolerance $\eta$, sufficiently
close functions have process values differing by at most
$\eta$, with probability approaching one.

The supremum makes this a uniform statement over all
nearby pairs, rather than a statement about one fixed pair.

An equivalent sequential formulation is

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
\qquad
\text{for every deterministic }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

### Step 5: A sufficient expectation condition

The handwritten notes give the condition

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\longrightarrow0
\qquad
\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
}
$$

By Markov's inequality,

$$
P^\star\left(
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}>\eta
\right)
\leq
\frac{
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
}{\eta}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Thus convergence of expected suprema implies asymptotic
equicontinuity.

For arbitrary random variables, convergence in probability
does not imply convergence of expectations. The converse
here requires additional empirical-process arguments,
which the next result supplies.

### Why symmetrization helps

On the difference class, write

$$
Z_i(h)=h(X_i)-Ph,
\qquad h\in\mathcal F_\delta.
$$

The expectation symmetrization lemma gives

$$
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}
\leq
2E^\star
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_\delta}
\left|
\frac1{\sqrt n}
\sum_i\varepsilon_i[h(X_i)-Ph]
\right|.
$$

After conditioning on the observations, the randomized
process has fixed coefficients and sub-Gaussian increments.

This makes the expected supremum accessible to chaining
and maximal inequalities.

### Check your understanding

What does asymptotic equicontinuity prevent?

It prevents persistent process fluctuations of fixed size
between functions whose population distance becomes
arbitrarily small.





## 5. Tight Convergence and Expected Small Increments

The next result explains why, for normalized sums of
i.i.d. processes, we can work with expected suprema
instead of probabilities when proving equicontinuity.

### Setup

Let $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$ be i.i.d. stochastic processes,
linear in their function argument.

We state the centering and moment assumptions explicitly:

$$
E[Z_1(f)]=0,
\qquad
E[Z_1(f)^2]<\infty
\qquad
\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

Define

$$
T_n(f)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i(f).
$$

Linearity allows us to write

$$
Z_i(f-g)=Z_i(f)-Z_i(g).
$$

Define the variance semimetric

$$
\rho_Z(f,g)
=
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)-Z_1(g))}
$$

and the small-difference class

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=
\{f-g:\ f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \rho_Z(f,g)<\delta\}.
$$

### Three equivalent conditions

Under this setup, the following are equivalent.

**(i) Tight weak convergence**

$$
T_n\rightsquigarrow G
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
$$

where $G$ is a tight limit process.

The finite-dimensional central limit theorem identifies
this limit as centered Gaussian, with covariance

$$
E[G(f)G(g)]
=
E[Z_1(f)Z_1(g)].
$$

**(ii) Total boundedness and probability equicontinuity**

The semimetric space $(\mathcal F,\rho_Z)$ is totally
bounded, and

$$
\|T_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
\qquad
\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

**(iii) Total boundedness and expectation equicontinuity**

The space $(\mathcal F,\rho_Z)$ is totally bounded, and

$$
E^\star\|T_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\longrightarrow0
\qquad
\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

### What does total boundedness contribute?

For every $\delta>0$, finitely many $\rho_Z$-balls
cover the index class:

$$
N(\delta,\mathcal F,\rho_Z)<\infty.
$$

This permits approximation of the process by its values
at finitely many representative functions.

The ingredients then work together:

- The finite-dimensional CLT controls those representative values.
- Equicontinuity controls the approximation error.
- Total boundedness ensures finitely many representatives suffice
  at each fixed accuracy.

### Why is the expectation formulation valuable?

The implication

$$
\text{(iii)}\Longrightarrow\text{(ii)}
$$

follows directly from Markov's inequality.

The reverse implication is more substantial.
Probability convergence alone does not generally control
expectations: rare, very large values can contribute
significantly to the mean.

The textbook establishes additional tail control for
these i.i.d. process sums and uses it to obtain convergence
of the expected small-increment suprema.

We therefore lose no generality by using expectation
bounds within this theorem's setup.

### Application to empirical processes

Set

$$
Z_i(f)=f(X_i)-Pf.
$$

Then

$$
T_n=\mathbb G_n,
\qquad
\rho_Z(f,g)=\rho_P(f,g).
$$

Consequently, for a class of square-integrable measurable
functions, the following are equivalent:

1. $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker.
2. $(\mathcal F,\rho_P)$ is totally bounded and
   $\mathbb G_n$ is asymptotically equicontinuous
   in outer probability.
3. $(\mathcal F,\rho_P)$ is totally bounded and

   $$
   E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
   \longrightarrow0
   \qquad
   \text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
   $$

This makes symmetrization and bounds for expected suprema
a direct route to proving the Donsker property.

### An additional conclusion: convergence of moments

These equivalent conditions also imply

$$
E^\star\|T_n\|_{\mathcal F}^{\,r}
\longrightarrow
E\|G\|_{\mathcal F}^{\,r}
\qquad
\text{for every }0<r<2.
$$

The restriction $r<2$ matters: this result does not
automatically establish convergence of second moments.

### Check your understanding

Why is total boundedness alone insufficient?

A finite cover only identifies nearby functions.
Equicontinuity is needed to ensure that their process
values are also nearby.

## 6. Covering Numbers, Brackets, and Entropy

We now begin §2.4: **Glivenko–Cantelli Theorems**.

The first theorem uses finitely many brackets to reduce
a uniform convergence problem to ordinary laws of large numbers.

### Covering numbers: approximation by nearby functions

Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of functions in a normed
function space.

An $\varepsilon$-ball centered at $g$ is

$$
B_\varepsilon(g)
=
\{f:\|f-g\|<\varepsilon\}.
$$

The covering number

$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
$$

is the smallest number of such balls needed to cover
$\mathcal F$.

Equivalently, we seek finitely many centers
$g_1,\ldots,g_M$ such that

$$
\forall f\in\mathcal F,\quad
\exists j:\ \|f-g_j\|<\varepsilon.
$$

For the comparison below, we allow centers in the ambient
function space; they need not belong to $\mathcal F$.

The logarithm

$$
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
$$

is called the covering entropy, or entropy without bracketing.

### Brackets: approximation from above and below

For functions $l,u$ satisfying $l(x)\leq u(x)$, define

$$
[l,u]
=
\{f:\ l(x)\leq f(x)\leq u(x)
\text{ for every }x\in\mathcal X\}.
$$

The functions $l$ and $u$ are the lower and upper endpoints.

A bracket contains all functions lying pointwise between
its endpoints. The endpoints need not belong to $\mathcal F$.

An $\varepsilon$-bracket satisfies

$$
\|u-l\|<\varepsilon.
$$

Notice the two distinct requirements:

- Containment is defined by pointwise inequalities.
- Width is measured using the chosen norm.

### Example: bracket width in $L_1(P)$

Because $u-l\geq0$,

$$
\|u-l\|_{P,1}
=
P(u-l)
=
\int_{\mathcal X}[u(x)-l(x)]\,dP(x).
$$

Thus an $L_1(P)$ $\varepsilon$-bracket satisfies

$$
P(u-l)<\varepsilon.
$$

It need not have a small gap at every point.
A large gap can occur on a region with sufficiently
small probability, while its average width remains small.

For the forthcoming theorem, bracket endpoints are taken
in $L_1(P)$, so their ordinary strong laws apply.

### Bracketing numbers

The bracketing number

$$
N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
$$

is the smallest number of $\varepsilon$-brackets needed
to cover $\mathcal F$.

That means there are brackets
$[l_1,u_1],\ldots,[l_M,u_M]$ such that

$$
\forall f\in\mathcal F,\quad
\exists j:\ l_j\leq f\leq u_j,
$$

with

$$
\|u_j-l_j\|<\varepsilon
\qquad\text{for every }j.
$$

The logarithm

$$
\log N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
$$

is called bracketing entropy.

### Why brackets help with empirical averages

If $l\leq f\leq u$, then positivity of integration gives

$$
P_nl\leq P_nf\leq P_nu,
$$

and

$$
Pl\leq Pf\leq Pu.
$$

These inequalities let us control every function inside
a bracket through its two endpoints.

A finite collection of brackets therefore provides
finitely many endpoint averages to study.

### Check your understanding

What is the difference between a ball and a bracket?

A ball requires norm closeness to one center.
A bracket requires pointwise containment between two
endpoints, whose separation is small in the chosen norm.

## 7. From Brackets to Balls: The Midpoint Argument

Your notes show that a narrow bracket fits inside a
small norm ball centered at its midpoint.

### Assumption: the norm respects pointwise bounds

Suppose the norm satisfies

$$
|h(x)|\leq |k(x)|\ \text{for every }x
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\|h\|\leq\|k\|.
$$

The usual $L_p(P)$ norms, including $L_1(P)$,
$L_2(P)$, and the supremum norm, have this property.

### Step 1: Choose the midpoint

Let $[l,u]$ be a $2\varepsilon$-bracket:

$$
\|u-l\|<2\varepsilon.
$$

Define its midpoint

$$
m=\frac{l+u}{2}.
$$

If $f\in[l,u]$, then pointwise

$$
l\leq f\leq u.
$$

Subtracting $m$ gives

$$
-\frac{u-l}{2}
\leq
f-m
\leq
\frac{u-l}{2}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
|f-m|\leq\frac{u-l}{2}.
$$

### Step 2: Apply monotonicity of the norm

Using the pointwise bound and homogeneity,

$$
\|f-m\|
\leq
\frac12\|u-l\|
<
\varepsilon.
$$

Thus every function in the bracket belongs to the
$\varepsilon$-ball centered at $m$:

$$
\boxed{
[l,u]\subseteq
B_\varepsilon\left(\frac{l+u}{2}\right).
}
$$

### Step 3: Compare the covering numbers

Suppose $M$ brackets of width less than $2\varepsilon$
cover $\mathcal F$.

Replace each bracket by the $\varepsilon$-ball centered
at its midpoint. These $M$ balls also cover $\mathcal F$.

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
\leq
N_{[]}(2\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|).
}
$$

This uses our convention that covering-ball centers may
lie in the ambient function space.

If centers must belong to $\mathcal F$, choose a member
of each nonempty bracket instead. That yields the related bound

$$
N_{\mathrm{internal}}(2\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
\leq
N_{[]}(2\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|).
$$

### What does this imply?

If the bracketing number is finite at every positive
accuracy, then so is the covering number.

Thus finite bracketing numbers imply total boundedness
under the chosen norm.

The reverse implication does not follow from this argument:
norm closeness alone does not provide pointwise upper
and lower bounds.

### Envelope functions

The next definition in your notes is an envelope.

A nonnegative function $F:\mathcal X\to[0,\infty]$
is an envelope for $\mathcal F$ if

$$
\boxed{
|f(x)|\leq F(x)
\qquad
\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F
\text{ and every }x\in\mathcal X.
}
$$

The envelope bounds the magnitude of every function
in the class.

For example, for indicator functions,

$$
\mathcal F=\{\mathbf1_A:A\in\mathcal A\},
$$

we can use the constant envelope $F(x)=1$.

The notation distinguishes:

- $\mathcal F$: the class of functions.
- $F$: one function bounding the whole class.

### Check your understanding

Why use the midpoint rather than the lower endpoint?

The midpoint is at most half the bracket width from
every function in the bracket:

$$
\left|f-\frac{l+u}{2}\right|
\leq\frac{u-l}{2}.
$$

## 8. The Glivenko–Cantelli Theorem with Bracketing

### Recall the Glivenko–Cantelli property

A class $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Glivenko–Cantelli if

$$
\boxed{
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|P_nf-Pf|
\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}^{*}.
}
$$

The star accommodates possible nonmeasurability of the
supremum through measurable majorants.

This is a uniform law of large numbers: all functions
in the class are controlled simultaneously.

For comparison, the ordinary strong law gives

$$
P_nf\longrightarrow Pf
\qquad\text{almost surely}
$$

for each fixed integrable function $f$.

Pointwise convergence for each function does not, by itself,
imply convergence of the supremum over the class.

### Theorem 2.4.1: finite bracketing implies Glivenko–Cantelli

Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions.

If

$$
\boxed{
N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P))<\infty
\qquad
\text{for every }\varepsilon>0,
}
$$

where bracket endpoints belong to $L_1(P)$, then
$\mathcal F$ is $P$-Glivenko–Cantelli.

### What does the hypothesis require?

For every fixed accuracy $\varepsilon>0$, we can find
finitely many brackets

$$
[l_1,u_1],\ldots,[l_M,u_M]
$$

covering $\mathcal F$, with

$$
P(u_j-l_j)<\varepsilon
\qquad
\text{for every }j.
$$

The number $M$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and may
grow rapidly as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.

This theorem requires finiteness at each positive accuracy.
It imposes no entropy growth rate.

### Why the theorem is plausible

Every $f\in\mathcal F$ lies between two bracket endpoints:

$$
l_j\leq f\leq u_j.
$$

The empirical and population averages preserve these bounds:

$$
P_nl_j\leq P_nf\leq P_nu_j,
\qquad
Pl_j\leq Pf\leq Pu_j.
$$

Because there are finitely many integrable endpoints,
the strong law controls all their empirical averages
simultaneously.

The remaining error is bounded by the bracket width.
We first let $n\to\infty$ with the brackets fixed,
then make their widths arbitrarily small.

### Glivenko–Cantelli versus Donsker

The Glivenko–Cantelli property controls the unscaled error:

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The Donsker property describes the limiting process after
scaling:

$$
\sqrt n\,(P_n-P)\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G_P
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F).
$$

The bracketing theorem here establishes uniform consistency.
It does not by itself establish a convergence rate or
a Gaussian process limit.

### Check your understanding

Must one finite collection of brackets work for every
$\varepsilon>0$?

No. For each accuracy, we may choose a different finite
collection. During the strong-law argument, that accuracy
and its collection are held fixed.


## 9. Proof of the Glivenko–Cantelli Bracketing Theorem

The proof reduces the supremum over an entire function
class to a maximum over finitely many bracket endpoints.

### Step 1: Fix an accuracy and choose finite brackets

Fix $\varepsilon>0$.

By the hypothesis, choose finitely many brackets

$$
[l_1,u_1],\ldots,[l_M,u_M]
$$

covering $\mathcal F$, with integrable endpoints and

$$
P(u_j-l_j)<\varepsilon
\qquad
\text{for every }j.
$$

For any $f\in\mathcal F$, choose a bracket containing it:

$$
l_j\leq f\leq u_j.
$$

### Step 2: Bound the error from above

Since $P_nf\leq P_nu_j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(P_n-P)f
&=P_nf-Pf\\
&\leq P_nu_j-Pf\\
&=(P_n-P)u_j+P(u_j-f).
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $0\leq u_j-f\leq u_j-l_j$,

$$
P(u_j-f)<\varepsilon.
$$

Therefore,

$$
(P_n-P)f
\leq
(P_n-P)u_j+\varepsilon.
$$

Taking the supremum over the class gives

$$
\boxed{
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}(P_n-P)f
\leq
\max_{1\leq j\leq M}(P_n-P)u_j+\varepsilon.
}
$$

### Step 3: Bound the error from below

Since $P_nf\geq P_nl_j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(P_n-P)f
&\geq P_nl_j-Pf\\
&=(P_n-P)l_j+P(l_j-f).
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $0\leq f-l_j\leq u_j-l_j$,

$$
P(l_j-f)>-\varepsilon.
$$

Hence,

$$
(P_n-P)f
\geq
(P_n-P)l_j-\varepsilon,
$$

and therefore

$$
\boxed{
\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}(P_n-P)f
\geq
\min_{1\leq j\leq M}(P_n-P)l_j-\varepsilon.
}
$$

### Step 4: Combine the bounds

Define the endpoint error

$$
R_n(\varepsilon)
=
\max_{1\leq j\leq M}
\left\{
|(P_n-P)l_j|,
|(P_n-P)u_j|
\right\}.
$$

The upper and lower bounds imply

$$
|(P_n-P)f|
\leq
R_n(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon
\qquad
\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
R_n(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon.
}
$$

The right side is measurable because it is a finite
maximum of measurable quantities. It therefore also
bounds a measurable majorant of the supremum.

### Step 5: Apply the strong law to the endpoints

For each endpoint, integrability gives

$$
(P_n-P)l_j\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
(P_n-P)u_j\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

There are finitely many endpoints, so all these
convergences hold simultaneously on an event of
probability one. Consequently,

$$
R_n(\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

For this fixed accuracy,

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq\varepsilon
\qquad\text{almost surely}^{*}.
$$

### Step 6: Let the accuracy decrease along a countable sequence

Choose, for example,

$$
\varepsilon_k=\frac1k.
$$

For each $k$, choose its finite bracket collection and
the probability-one event where its endpoint strong laws hold.

The intersection of these countably many events still
has probability one. On this common event,

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq\frac1k
\qquad
\text{for every }k.
$$

Since the supremum is nonnegative, its limit is zero:

$$
\boxed{
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{almost surely}^{*}.
}
$$

This proves that $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Glivenko–Cantelli.

### Proof pattern to remember

**Finite brackets → endpoint strong laws → width error
→ countable sequence of shrinking widths.**

### Check your understanding

Why use a countable sequence $\varepsilon_k\downarrow0$?

Countably many probability-one events have a
probability-one intersection. An uncountable intersection
does not automatically have that property.
