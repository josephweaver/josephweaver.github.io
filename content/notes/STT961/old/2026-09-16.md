# September 16 — Maximal Inequalities

This lecture introduces Orlicz norms as tools for controlling
tails and maxima of random variables.

The choice of growth function determines the strength of control:

- $\psi(u)=u^p$ recovers the usual Lp norm.
- $\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1$ measures exponential tail behavior.

A finite exponential Orlicz norm gives a tail bound of the form

$$
P(|X|>x)\leq K e^{-Cx^p},
$$

and such a tail bound also implies a finite exponential
Orlicz norm.

The main maximal inequality is

$$
\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|\right\|_\psi
\leq
K\psi^{-1}(m)\max_i\|X_i\|_\psi.
$$

Thus the inverse growth function determines the cost of
taking a maximum:

$$
m^{1/p}
\quad\text{for Lp control},
\qquad
(\log(m+1))^{1/p}
\quad\text{for exponential Orlicz control}.
$$

These bounds require no independence.

The lecture begins the proof by splitting large and small
inputs, then replacing a maximum by a sum. The proof remains
unfinished at the end of class.

The eventual goal is to use bounds on finite maxima to
control empirical-process suprema and establish asymptotic
equicontinuity.

## 1. Orlicz Norms: Measuring Size Through a Growth Function

Let $\psi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ be a nonzero,
nondecreasing, convex function with $\psi(0)=0$.

For a real-valued random variable $X$, define its Orlicz norm by

$$
\|X\|_\psi
=
\inf\left\{
c>0:
E\left[\psi\left(\frac{|X|}{c}\right)\right]\leq1
\right\}.
$$

If no such $c$ exists, the infimum is defined to be infinity.

### Reading the definition

For each candidate scale $c$:

1. Divide the magnitude $|X|$ by $c$.
2. Apply the growth function $\psi$.
3. Take the expectation.
4. Check whether the result is at most one.

The norm is the infimum of the scales that pass this test.

Increasing $c$ decreases $|X|/c$, so the expected penalty
cannot increase.

The definition uses an infimum; we should not assume without
justification that a smallest admissible positive scale exists.

### What does psi control?

The function $\psi$ determines how strongly large values
of $|X|$ contribute to the expected penalty.

A polynomial choice measures moments.
An exponential choice penalizes large values much more strongly.

Thus the choice of $\psi$ lets us measure different kinds
of tail behavior.

### Recovering the familiar Lp norm

Take

$$
\psi(u)=u^p,\qquad p\geq1.
$$

The defining condition becomes

$$
E\left[\left(\frac{|X|}{c}\right)^p\right]\leq1,
$$

or

$$
\frac{E|X|^p}{c^p}\leq1.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
c\geq(E|X|^p)^{1/p}.
$$

Taking the infimum gives

$$
\boxed{\|X\|_\psi=(E|X|^p)^{1/p}=\|X\|_p.}
$$

So Orlicz norms include the usual Lp norms as special cases.

The restriction $p\geq1$ ensures convexity of $u^p$.
For $0<p<1$, this construction does not give an ordinary norm.

### Example: a constant random variable

If $X=a$ almost surely and $\psi(u)=u^p$, then

$$
\|X\|_\psi
=
(E|a|^p)^{1/p}
=
|a|.
$$

If $X=0$ almost surely, every $c>0$ passes the defining test,
so the infimum is zero.

### The norm properties

On the space of random variables with finite Orlicz norm,
identifying variables that agree almost surely, the properties are:

$$
\|X\|_\psi=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
X=0\text{ almost surely},
$$

$$
\|aX\|_\psi=|a|\|X\|_\psi,
$$

and

$$
\|X+Y\|_\psi\leq\|X\|_\psi+\|Y\|_\psi.
$$

The professor identifies verifying these properties as homework.
Convexity is especially useful for proving the triangle inequality.

### Why introduce this now?

Our goal is to control maxima and suprema of random variables.

Orlicz norms provide a framework in which the growth of $\psi$
determines how costly it is to take a maximum over many variables.

The next example uses exponential growth functions, which will
give much sharper control of maxima than a fixed moment bound.

## 2. Exponential Orlicz Norms

The next example uses the growth functions

$$
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,
\qquad p\geq1.
$$

These functions are nondecreasing, convex, and satisfy
$\psi_p(0)=0$.

They penalize large values exponentially, rather than polynomially.

### Definition

Substituting $\psi_p$ into the Orlicz norm gives

$$
\|X\|_{\psi_p}
=
\inf\left\{
c>0:
E\left[e^{(|X|/c)^p}-1\right]\leq1
\right\}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{
\|X\|_{\psi_p}
=
\inf\left\{
c>0:
E\left[e^{|X|^p/c^p}\right]\leq2
\right\}.
}
$$

The threshold becomes two because we move the subtracted
one to the other side.

A finite norm means that the exponential expectation is
controlled at some positive scale $c$.

### Two important choices

For $p=1$,

$$
\psi_1(u)=e^u-1.
$$

A finite $\psi_1$ norm describes sub-exponential tail behavior.

For $p=2$,

$$
\psi_2(u)=e^{u^2}-1.
$$

A finite $\psi_2$ norm describes sub-Gaussian tail behavior.

“Sub-Gaussian” does not mean that $X$ is normally distributed.
It means that its tails admit a bound of Gaussian type,
with suitable constants.

We will derive these tail bounds in the next section.

### Comparison with the matching Lp norm

For every $u\geq0$,

$$
u^p\leq e^{u^p}-1.
$$

Therefore, whenever $c$ passes the exponential Orlicz test,

$$
E\left[\left(\frac{|X|}{c}\right)^p\right]
\leq
E\left[e^{(|X|/c)^p}-1\right]
\leq1.
$$

This implies $\|X\|_p\leq c$. Taking the infimum gives

$$
\boxed{\|X\|_p\leq\|X\|_{\psi_p}.}
$$

Thus a finite exponential Orlicz norm implies a finite
matching pth moment.

### Example: a constant random variable

If $X=a$ almost surely, the defining condition is

$$
e^{(|a|/c)^p}\leq2.
$$

Taking logarithms gives

$$
\left(\frac{|a|}{c}\right)^p\leq\log2.
$$

Hence,

$$
\boxed{
\|a\|_{\psi_p}
=
\frac{|a|}{(\log2)^{1/p}}.
}
$$

This also shows that an Orlicz norm's numerical normalization
depends on the chosen growth function.

### Stronger control, rather than universal numerical ordering

A finite exponential Orlicz norm implies finiteness of every
fixed positive polynomial moment.

However, we should not claim that one exponential Orlicz norm
is numerically larger than every Lr norm without constants.

The useful comparison is that, for each fixed $r>0$,

$$
\|X\|_r\leq C_{p,r}\|X\|_{\psi_p},
$$

where the constant can depend on $p$ and $r$.

### Why the professor favors these norms

Maximal inequalities must account for the number of variables
over which we take a maximum.

Exponential Orlicz control will lead to a cost involving

$$
(\log(m+1))^{1/p}
$$

for a maximum over $m$ variables.

For $p=2$, this becomes $\sqrt{\log(m+1)}$, which grows much
more slowly than a power of $m$.

We will establish that comparison when we reach the
finite-maximum inequality.

## 3. From Orlicz Norms to Tail Bounds

An Orlicz norm controls the probability that a random variable
has a large magnitude.

The main tool is Markov's inequality.

### Start with an admissible scale

Suppose $c>0$ satisfies

$$
E\left[\psi\left(\frac{|X|}{c}\right)\right]\leq1.
$$

For $x>0$ with $\psi(x/c)>0$, monotonicity of $\psi$ gives

$$
\{|X|>x\}
\subseteq
\left\{
\psi\left(\frac{|X|}{c}\right)
\geq\psi\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)
\right\}.
$$

Applying Markov's inequality,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(|X|>x)
&\leq
\frac{
E[\psi(|X|/c)]
}{
\psi(x/c)
}\\
&\leq
\frac1{\psi(x/c)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{P(|X|>x)\leq\frac1{\psi(x/c)}.}
$$

If the right-hand side exceeds one, we can replace it by one.

### Using the norm as the scale

Let $a=\|X\|_\psi$ with $0<a<\infty$.

Every $c>a$ is admissible. For the continuous growth functions
used here, letting $c\downarrow a$ gives

$$
P(|X|>x)
\leq
\frac1{\psi(x/a)}.
$$

Using an admissible $c$ first avoids assuming without
justification that the infimum is attained.

If the norm is zero, then $X=0$ almost surely, so its positive
tail probabilities are zero.

### Polynomial choice: a moment bound

For $\psi(u)=u^p$,

$$
P(|X|>x)
\leq
\frac{c^p}{x^p}.
$$

Taking $c=\|X\|_p$ when this norm is positive and finite gives

$$
P(|X|>x)
\leq
\frac{E|X|^p}{x^p}.
$$

This is the familiar moment form of Markov's inequality.

### Exponential choice

For

$$
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,
$$

the bound becomes

$$
P(|X|>x)
\leq
\frac1{e^{(x/a)^p}-1},
\qquad a=\|X\|_{\psi_p}>0.
$$

For large $x$, this behaves like

$$
e^{-(x/a)^p}.
$$

There is also a convenient bound valid for every $x>0$.
Since the defining condition gives

$$
E[e^{(|X|/a)^p}]\leq2,
$$

Markov's inequality applied directly to the exponential yields

$$
\boxed{
P(|X|>x)\leq2e^{-(x/a)^p}.
}
$$

### The important special cases

A finite $\psi_1$ norm gives

$$
P(|X|>x)\leq2e^{-x/a}.
$$

A finite $\psi_2$ norm gives

$$
P(|X|>x)\leq2e^{-x^2/a^2}.
$$

The second bound has Gaussian-type decay. The variable itself
need not have a Gaussian distribution.

### Corrections to the handwritten tail calculation

The tail event must be

$$
|X|>x,
$$

rather than $|X|<x$.

Also, the normalization gives an expected penalty at most one:

$$
E[\psi(|X|/a)]\leq1.
$$

It need not equal one. The resulting tail bound is an inequality.

## 4. From Exponential Tail Bounds to Orlicz Norms

The previous section showed that a finite exponential Orlicz
norm gives an exponential tail bound.

The next lemma establishes the reverse direction.

### Lemma: a tail bound controls the norm

Suppose, for constants $K,C>0$ and $p\geq1$,

$$
P(|X|>x)\leq K e^{-Cx^p}
\qquad\text{for every }x\geq0.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
\|X\|_{\psi_p}
\leq
\left(\frac{1+K}{C}\right)^{1/p},
\qquad
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1.
}
$$

Thus exponential tail decay guarantees a finite exponential
Orlicz norm.

### What must we prove?

We need a scale $c>0$ such that

$$
E[e^{|X|^p/c^p}-1]\leq1.
$$

Introduce

$$
D=\frac1{c^p}.
$$

Our task becomes choosing $D>0$ so that

$$
E[e^{D|X|^p}-1]\leq1.
$$

We use uppercase $C$ for the given tail constant and
lowercase $c$ for the candidate norm scale.

### Step 1: express the exponential as an integral

For every $z\geq0$,

$$
e^{Dz}-1=\int_0^z D e^{Ds}\,ds.
$$

Taking $z=|X|^p$,

$$
e^{D|X|^p}-1
=
\int_0^\infty
D e^{Ds}\mathbf 1_{\{s<|X|^p\}}\,ds.
$$

The indicator turns the random upper limit into a fixed
integration range.

### Step 2: exchange expectation and integration

The integrand is nonnegative, so Tonelli's theorem gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[e^{D|X|^p}-1]
&=
\int_0^\infty
D e^{Ds}P(|X|^p>s)\,ds\\
&=
\int_0^\infty
D e^{Ds}P(|X|>s^{1/p})\,ds.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the central trick: write an expectation in terms
of tail probabilities.

Tonelli applies even before we know the expectation is finite.

### Step 3: insert the assumed tail bound

Since $(s^{1/p})^p=s$,

$$
P(|X|>s^{1/p})\leq K e^{-Cs}.
$$

Therefore, for $0<D<C$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[e^{D|X|^p}-1]
&\leq
KD\int_0^\infty e^{-(C-D)s}\,ds\\
&=
\frac{KD}{C-D}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The restriction $D<C$ makes the integral finite.

### Step 4: make the bound at most one

We require

$$
\frac{KD}{C-D}\leq1.
$$

Solving,

$$
KD\leq C-D
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
D(K+1)\leq C.
$$

Choose

$$
D=\frac{C}{K+1}.
$$

Because $K>0$, this choice satisfies $D<C$.

Recalling $D=1/c^p$, the corresponding scale is

$$
c=\left(\frac{K+1}{C}\right)^{1/p}.
$$

This scale passes the defining Orlicz test, proving the bound.

### The two-way connection

We now have both directions:

- A finite $\psi_p$ norm gives a tail bound of the form
  $K e^{-Cx^p}$.
- A tail bound of that form gives a finite $\psi_p$ norm.

These are equivalent descriptions of tail behavior, with
constants allowed to change.

## 5. A First Maximal Inequality: The Lp Bound

We now move from controlling one random variable to controlling
the largest magnitude among several.

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_m$ be arbitrary real-valued random variables,
and let $p\geq1$.

No independence assumption is needed.

### The inequality

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|\right\|_p
\leq
\left(\sum_{i=1}^m\|X_i\|_p^p\right)^{1/p}
\leq
m^{1/p}\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|X_i\|_p.
}
$$

The left side is the norm of the random maximum.

The right side uses the largest individual norm, multiplied
by a factor accounting for the number of variables.

### Proof: replace the maximum by a sum

Pointwise,

$$
\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|\right)^p
=
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|^p
\leq
\sum_{i=1}^m|X_i|^p.
$$

Taking expectations,

$$
E\left[\left(\max_i|X_i|\right)^p\right]
\leq
\sum_{i=1}^m E|X_i|^p.
$$

Taking pth roots gives

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_p
\leq
\left(\sum_{i=1}^m\|X_i\|_p^p\right)^{1/p}.
$$

Finally,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^m\|X_i\|_p^p
\leq
m\left(\max_i\|X_i\|_p\right)^p,
$$

which proves the second bound.

The professor emphasizes the useful trick:
**replace a maximum of nonnegative quantities by their sum.**

### Norm of the maximum versus maximum of the norms

These are different quantities:

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\max_i\|X_i\|_p.
$$

The first forms the maximum separately for each outcome,
then computes its norm.

The second computes each variable's norm first, then takes
the largest resulting number.

Since $|X_i|\leq\max_j|X_j|$,

$$
\max_i\|X_i\|_p
\leq
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_p.
$$

Together,

$$
\max_i\|X_i\|_p
\leq
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_p
\leq
m^{1/p}\max_i\|X_i\|_p.
$$

### Example: identical variables

If $X_1=\cdots=X_m=Z$, then

$$
\max_i|X_i|=|Z|.
$$

The maximum's norm is simply $\|Z\|_p$.

The general upper bound remains valid, but its factor
$m^{1/p}$ is unnecessary in this particular example.

Thus the inequality is a worst-case bound, rather than
an assertion that every maximum grows at that rate.

### Why seek a sharper inequality?

If all individual norms are at most $a$, the bound gives

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_p\leq a\,m^{1/p}.
$$

For a fixed $p$, this cost grows as a power of $m$.

The next Orlicz maximal inequality replaces this factor
with a quantity involving $\psi^{-1}(m)$.

For exponential growth functions, that will produce
a logarithmic rather than polynomial cost.

## 6. The Orlicz Maximal Inequality

The next lemma generalizes the Lp bound for a finite maximum.

Its central message is that the inverse growth function
$\psi^{-1}$ determines the cost of taking a maximum.

### Assumptions on psi

Let $\psi$ be nonzero, nondecreasing, and convex, with
$\psi(0)=0$.

Assume also that, for some constant $c_0>0$,

$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}
\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(c_0xy)}
<\infty.
$$

This growth condition allows products of penalties to be
controlled by a penalty at a scaled product of inputs.

The polynomial and exponential examples used in this lecture
satisfy the condition.

### The maximal inequality

For arbitrary random variables $X_1,\ldots,X_m$,

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|\right\|_\psi
\leq
K\psi^{-1}(m)
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\|X_i\|_\psi,
}
$$

where $K$ depends only on $\psi$.

In particular, $K$ does not depend on $m$ or on the individual
distributions of the $X_i$.

No independence assumption is required.

### Recovering the polynomial factor

For $\psi(u)=u^p$,

$$
\psi^{-1}(m)=m^{1/p}.
$$

Thus the general inequality has the same form as the Lp bound:

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_p
\leq
K m^{1/p}\max_i\|X_i\|_p.
$$

For this polynomial case, the previous direct proof gives $K=1$.

### Exponential growth gives a logarithmic factor

For

$$
\psi_p(u)=e^{u^p}-1,
$$

solve $\psi_p(u)=m$:

$$
e^{u^p}-1=m
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u^p=\log(m+1).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\psi_p^{-1}(m)
=
(\log(m+1))^{1/p}.
$$

The maximal inequality becomes

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_{\psi_p}
\leq
K(\log(m+1))^{1/p}
\max_i\|X_i\|_{\psi_p}.
}
$$

### Two important cases

For sub-exponential control,

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}
\leq
K\log(m+1)\max_i\|X_i\|_{\psi_1}.
$$

For sub-Gaussian control,

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_{\psi_2}
\leq
K\sqrt{\log(m+1)}\max_i\|X_i\|_{\psi_2}.
$$

Compare the costs for the quadratic choices:

- An L2 bound gives a factor $\sqrt m$.
- A $\psi_2$ bound gives a factor $\sqrt{\log(m+1)}$.

The sharper conclusion requires the stronger exponential
tail control supplied by the $\psi_2$ norms.

### Why rapid growth helps

A rapidly growing $\psi$ has a slowly growing inverse.

This means strong control of each variable's tails can
offset the many opportunities for a large value when we
take a maximum.

The inequality bounds the possible growth of the maximum.
It does not assert that every collection attains that growth.

### Connection to empirical processes

Finite approximations of a function class produce finite
collections of process increments.

This lemma bounds their maximum using:

1. The largest individual increment norm.
2. The number of increments being considered.

Later, covering numbers will measure the sizes of these
finite approximations.

## 7. Proof Strategy: Split Large and Small Inputs

The lecture begins proving the Orlicz maximal inequality by
first assuming the stronger growth condition

$$
\psi(x)\psi(y)\leq\psi(c_0xy)
\qquad\text{for all }x,y\geq1.
$$

The general lemma only assumes a corresponding bound
asymptotically. This stronger assumption simplifies the
first part of the proof.

### Step 1: derive a rescaling inequality

For $x\geq y\geq1$, both $x/y$ and $y$ are at least one.

Apply the growth condition to these two inputs:

$$
\psi(x/y)\psi(y)
\leq
\psi\bigl(c_0(x/y)y\bigr)
=
\psi(c_0x).
$$

Whenever $\psi(y)>0$, divide to obtain

$$
\boxed{
\psi(x/y)\leq\frac{\psi(c_0x)}{\psi(y)}.
}
$$

This tells us how dividing an input by $y$ reduces its penalty.

### Step 2: identify the quantity to bound

Let

$$
M=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|.
$$

To bound $\|M\|_\psi$, we want a scale $Cy$ such that

$$
E\left[\psi\left(\frac{M}{Cy}\right)\right]\leq1.
$$

Since $\psi$ is nondecreasing,

$$
\psi\left(\frac{M}{Cy}\right)
=
\max_i\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Cy}\right).
$$

Fix $y\geq1$ with $\psi(y)>0$.

### Step 3: split according to input size

For each $i$, consider two cases.

**Large input:** if $|X_i|/(Cy)\geq1$, then
$x=|X_i|/C\geq y$. The rescaling inequality gives

$$
\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Cy}\right)
\leq
\frac{\psi(c_0|X_i|/C)}{\psi(y)}.
$$

**Small input:** if $|X_i|/(Cy)<1$, monotonicity gives

$$
\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Cy}\right)\leq\psi(1).
$$

Combining the cases,

$$
\psi\left(\frac{|X_i|}{Cy}\right)
\leq
\frac{\psi(c_0|X_i|/C)}{\psi(y)}
+
\psi(1).
$$

The added terms are nonnegative, so this bound holds in
either case.

### Step 4: replace the maximum by a sum

Taking the maximum over $i$ and then bounding it by a sum,

$$
\boxed{
\psi\left(\frac{M}{Cy}\right)
\leq
\frac1{\psi(y)}
\sum_{i=1}^m\psi\left(\frac{c_0|X_i|}{C}\right)
+
\psi(1).
}
$$

This is the reduction reached at the end of your handwritten notes.

### Step 5: take expectations

Write

$$
a=\max_i\|X_i\|_\psi,
$$

and suppose $0<a<\infty$.

Choose $C>c_0a$. Then $C/c_0$ is an admissible scale
for every $X_i$, giving

$$
E\left[\psi\left(\frac{c_0|X_i|}{C}\right)\right]\leq1.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
E\left[\psi\left(\frac{M}{Cy}\right)\right]
\leq
\frac{m}{\psi(y)}+\psi(1).
}
$$

The problem has now become choosing $y$ so that the
right-hand side is at most one.

This explains the appearance of $\psi^{-1}(m)$:
to make $m/\psi(y)$ small, we choose $\psi(y)$ proportional
to $m$.

The additive term $\psi(1)$ requires an additional normalization
in the completed textbook proof.

### The reusable proof pattern

The proof combines three ideas:

1. Use the growth condition where inputs are large enough.
2. Bound the remaining small inputs by a constant.
3. Replace a maximum by a sum before taking expectations.

Independence is unnecessary because these steps use
pointwise inequalities and linearity of expectation.
