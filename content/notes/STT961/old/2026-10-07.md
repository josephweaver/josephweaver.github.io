## 1. Completing the Proof: Total Boundedness in $L_2(P)$

The October 5 lecture established asymptotic equicontinuity. The remaining step in the Donsker theorem without bracketing is to prove that $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded under
$$
d_P(f,g)=\|f-g\|_{P,2}.
$$

Total boundedness means that, for every radius $r>0$, finitely many balls of radius $r$ cover $\mathcal F$.

### Step 1: Recall the uniform law for squared differences

We already proved that
$$
\mathcal H=\{(f-g)^2:f,g\in\mathcal F\}
$$
is $P$-Glivenko–Cantelli.

Thus
$$
D_n
:=
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F}
\left|(P_n-P)(f-g)^2\right|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Equivalently,
$$
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\|f-g\|_{P_n,2}^2
-
\|f-g\|_{P,2}^2
\right|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

This controls the discrepancy between empirical and population squared distances uniformly over all pairs of functions.

### Step 2: Obtain a finite empirical cover

The uniform entropy assumption implies
$$
N(u,\mathcal F,L_2(Q))<\infty
$$
for every $u>0$ and every admissible finitely supported probability measure $Q$.

In particular, on a sample realization with finite empirical envelope norm, $\mathcal F$ has a finite $L_2(P_n)$-cover at every positive radius.

We can take the covering centers in $\mathcal F$, allowing a universal enlargement of radius if necessary.

Fix $\varepsilon>0$, and choose centers
$$
g_1,\ldots,g_m\in\mathcal F
$$
such that every $f\in\mathcal F$ satisfies
$$
\|f-g_j\|_{P_n,2}\le\varepsilon
$$
for some $j$.

### Step 3: Transfer the empirical cover to a population cover

Choose a sample realization for which
$$
D_n<\varepsilon^2.
$$

Such realizations exist for sufficiently large $n$, because $D_n\to0$ in outer probability.

For $f\in\mathcal F$, choose its empirical covering center $g_j$. Then
$$
P(f-g_j)^2
=
P_n(f-g_j)^2
+
(P-P_n)(f-g_j)^2.
$$

Therefore,
$$
P(f-g_j)^2
\le
\varepsilon^2+D_n
<
2\varepsilon^2.
$$

Taking square roots,
$$
\boxed{
\|f-g_j\|_{P,2}<\sqrt2\,\varepsilon.
}
$$

The same finite collection of centers therefore covers $\mathcal F$ in $L_2(P)$, with radius $\sqrt2\,\varepsilon$.

### Step 4: Conclude total boundedness

Given any desired population radius $r>0$, run the argument with
$$
\varepsilon=\frac r{\sqrt2}.
$$

We obtain a finite population cover of radius $r$. Hence
$$
\boxed{
N(r,\mathcal F,L_2(P))<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }r>0.
}
$$

Thus $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded in the $L_2(P)$ semimetric.

### Why is a sample-dependent cover enough?

Total boundedness is an existence statement.

We may first construct centers using a particular sample realization. Once that realization is fixed, the selected centers are simply a finite collection of functions in $\mathcal F$.

The uniform distance comparison proves that this collection covers the entire class under the deterministic population semimetric.

### Completion of the theorem

The proof now has all three ingredients:

1. Finite-dimensional convergence from the multivariate central limit theorem.
2. Asymptotic equicontinuity from symmetrization and chaining.
3. Total boundedness in $L_2(P)$ from the uniform law for squared differences.

Under the theorem’s stated assumptions,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\text{ is }P\text{-Donsker}.}
$$

## 2. Example: The Classical Empirical Distribution Function

Consider the class of half-line indicators
$$
\mathcal F
=
\{f_t:t\in\mathbb R\},
\qquad
f_t(x)=\mathbf1\{x\le t\}.
$$

For this class,
$$
P_nf_t=F_n(t),
\qquad
Pf_t=F_P(t),
$$
where $F_n$ is the empirical CDF and $F_P$ is the population CDF.

Thus
$$
\mathbb G_nf_t
=
\sqrt n\bigl(F_n(t)-F_P(t)\bigr).
$$

We will verify the uniform entropy condition and conclude that this class is $P$-Donsker for every probability distribution $P$ on $\mathbb R$.

### Step 1: Compute the distance between two indicators

For $s<t$,
$$
f_t-f_s=\mathbf1\{s<x\le t\}.
$$

Because an indicator equals its square,
$$
(f_t-f_s)^2=|f_t-f_s|.
$$

Therefore, for any probability measure $Q$,
$$
\boxed{
\|f_t-f_s\|_{Q,2}^2
=
Q((s,t])
=
\|f_t-f_s\|_{Q,1}.
}
$$

An $L_2(Q)$ distance of size $\varepsilon$ corresponds to probability mass of size $\varepsilon^2$.

### Step 2: Bound the number of separated functions

Suppose
$$
t_1<\cdots<t_m
$$
index an $\varepsilon$-separated collection in $L_2(Q)$.

For consecutive functions,
$$
\|f_{t_{j+1}}-f_{t_j}\|_{Q,2}>\varepsilon,
$$
so
$$
Q((t_j,t_{j+1}])>\varepsilon^2.
$$

These intervals are disjoint. Their total probability cannot exceed one:
$$
(m-1)\varepsilon^2
<
\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}Q((t_j,t_{j+1}])
\le1.
$$

Consequently,
$$
m\le1+\frac1{\varepsilon^2}.
$$

A maximal separated collection provides a cover at the same radius, up to the usual boundary conventions. Hence, for a universal constant $K\ge1$,
$$
\boxed{
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(Q))
\le
\frac K{\varepsilon^2},
\qquad 0<\varepsilon\le1.
}
$$

Crucially, $K$ does not depend on $Q$. The argument also allows atoms in $Q$.

### Step 3: Identify the envelope

The constant function
$$
F(x)=1
$$
is an envelope for $\mathcal F$.

For every probability measure $Q$,
$$
\|F\|_{Q,2}=1.
$$

Thus the normalized covering radius in the theorem is simply $\varepsilon$:
$$
\varepsilon\|F\|_{Q,2}=\varepsilon.
$$

Also,
$$
PF^2=1<\infty.
$$

### Step 4: Verify the uniform entropy integral

The covering-number bound gives
$$
\sup_Q
\sqrt{
\log N(\varepsilon\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q))
}
\le
\sqrt{\log K+2\log(1/\varepsilon)}
$$
for $0<\varepsilon\le1$.

For $\varepsilon>1$, one ball covers the class, so the entropy contribution is zero.

It remains to check
$$
\int_0^1
\sqrt{\log K+2\log(1/\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon
<\infty.
$$

Use
$$
u=\log(1/\varepsilon),
\qquad
\varepsilon=e^{-u},
\qquad
d\varepsilon=-e^{-u}\,du.
$$

Then
$$
\int_0^1
\sqrt{\log K+2\log(1/\varepsilon)}\,d\varepsilon
=
\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{\log K+2u}\,e^{-u}\,du.
$$

This is finite: the exponential factor dominates the square-root growth. More explicitly,
$$
\sqrt{\log K+2u}
\le
\sqrt{\log K}+\sqrt{2u},
$$
so the integral is at most
$$
\sqrt{\log K}
+
\sqrt2\,\Gamma(3/2)
<\infty.
$$

Therefore the uniform entropy condition holds.

### Step 5: Check measurability

For every real $t$, choose rational numbers $r_k\downarrow t$. Then
$$
f_{r_k}(x)\longrightarrow f_t(x)
\qquad\text{for every }x.
$$

The rationally indexed indicators form a countable approximating subclass. This also supplies the required measurability for the difference and squared-difference classes.

For local differences, the strict condition
$$
\|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta
$$
is preserved eventually under these approximations, by dominated convergence.

### Conclusion

The Donsker theorem without bracketing applies:
$$
\boxed{
\sqrt n(F_n-F_P)
\rightsquigarrow
\mathbb G_P
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathbb R).
}
$$

The limit is a centered Gaussian process with covariance
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb G_P(s),\mathbb G_P(t))
=
F_P(\min\{s,t\})-F_P(s)F_P(t).
$$

It can be represented as
$$
\mathbb G_P(t)=B(F_P(t)),
$$
where $B$ is a standard Brownian bridge.

No continuity assumption on $F_P$ is needed.

## 3. The Weak $L_2$ Size of a Function

Before stating the next Donsker theorem, the professor introduced
$$
\boxed{
\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}
=
\sup_{x>0}
x\sqrt{P(|f|>x)}.
}
$$

Equivalently,
$$
\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}^2
=
\sup_{x>0}x^2P(|f|>x).
$$

This is called the weak $L_2$ quasi-norm.

### Step 1: Interpret the definition

If
$$
\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}=A<\infty,
$$
then, for every $x>0$,
$$
x^2P(|f|>x)\le A^2.
$$

Thus
$$
\boxed{
P(|f|>x)\le\frac{A^2}{x^2}.
}
$$

The quantity measures the size of $f$ through its tail probabilities.

Unlike the ordinary $L_2$ norm, it does not directly integrate $f^2$.

### Step 2: Compare it with the ordinary $L_2$ norm

For every $x>0$,
$$
x^2\mathbf1\{|f|>x\}\le f^2.
$$

Taking expectations,
$$
x^2P(|f|>x)\le Pf^2.
$$

Taking the supremum and square roots gives
$$
\boxed{
\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}\le\|f\|_{P,2}.
}
$$

Therefore, every square-integrable function has finite weak $L_2$ size.

The converse need not hold.

### Step 3: See why finite weak $L_2$ size is weaker

Suppose a nonnegative random variable $Z$ has tail
$$
P(Z>x)=x^{-2},
\qquad x\ge1,
$$
and $P(Z>x)=1$ for $0<x<1$.

Then
$$
\sup_{x>0}x^2P(Z>x)=1,
$$
so
$$
\|Z\|_{2,\infty}=1.
$$

However, the tail-integral formula gives
$$
EZ^2
=
\int_0^\infty 2xP(Z>x)\,dx.
$$

Its contribution from $x\ge1$ is
$$
\int_1^\infty 2x\cdot x^{-2}\,dx
=
2\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x}
=
\infty.
$$

Thus
$$
\boxed{
\|Z\|_{2,\infty}<\infty
\quad\text{but}\quad
EZ^2=\infty.
}
$$

A bound of order $x^{-2}$ on the tail lies at the boundary of second-moment integrability.

### Step 4: Why call it a quasi-norm?

It has absolute homogeneity:
$$
\|af\|_{P,2,\infty}
=
|a|\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}.
$$

It also vanishes precisely when $f=0$, $P$-almost surely.

But the ordinary triangle inequality can fail. Instead, we have a triangle inequality with a universal constant.

Indeed,
$$
\{|f+g|>x\}
\subseteq
\{|f|>x/2\}\cup\{|g|>x/2\}.
$$

Consequently,
$$
x^2P(|f+g|>x)
\le
4\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}^2
+
4\|g\|_{P,2,\infty}^2.
$$

Hence
$$
\boxed{
\|f+g\|_{P,2,\infty}
\le
2\bigl(\|f\|_{P,2,\infty}
+
\|g\|_{P,2,\infty}\bigr).
}
$$

The professor noted that an equivalent genuine norm exists. For the proof, universal multiplicative constants are sufficient.

### Step 5: Distinguish bounded weak size from a vanishing tail condition

The next theorem assumes that the envelope satisfies
$$
\boxed{
x^2P^\star (F>x)\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty.
}
$$

Equivalently,
$$
P^\star (F>x)=o(x^{-2}).
$$

This is stronger than merely requiring
$$
\sup_{x>0}x^2P^\star (F>x)<\infty.
$$

The example above has $x^2P(Z>x)=1$ for all $x\ge1$, so it fails the vanishing-tail condition.

On the other hand, a finite second moment implies the vanishing-tail condition. For a measurable envelope,
$$
x^2P(F>x)
\le
P\!\left[F^2\mathbf1\{F>x\}\right]
\longrightarrow0.
$$

### Why introduce this quantity?

The next theorem uses brackets whose widths are small in weak $L_2$, together with covers that are small in ordinary $L_2(P)$.

These control two different features:

- ordinary $L_2(P)$ distances control the variance of increments;
- weak $L_2$ bracket widths control the probability of large pointwise errors.

Both enter the upcoming chaining construction.

## 4. A Donsker Theorem Using Bracketing and Covering

The professor next stated a theorem that combines two entropy conditions:

- bracketing in weak $L_2(P)$;
- covering in ordinary $L_2(P)$.

Both conditions concern the population measure $P$.

### Theorem

Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions with envelope $F$. Suppose
$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{
\log N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_{2,\infty}(P))
}\,d\varepsilon
<\infty
}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))
}\,d\varepsilon
<\infty.
}
$$

Assume also that
$$
\boxed{
x^2P^\star (F>x)\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }x\to\infty.
}
$$

Then
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\text{ is }P\text{-Donsker}.}
$$

This is the hybrid bracketing-and-covering theorem stated in the lecture.

### Step 1: Interpret the bracketing condition

A bracket $[l,u]$ contains all functions satisfying
$$
l(x)\le f(x)\le u(x)
\qquad\text{for every }x.
$$

Its weak $L_2(P)$ width is
$$
\|u-l\|_{P,2,\infty}.
$$

Thus
$$
N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_{2,\infty}(P))
$$
is the minimum number of brackets of weak $L_2(P)$ width at most $\varepsilon$ needed to contain the class.

If $f,g$ belong to the same bracket, then
$$
|f-g|\le u-l.
$$

The bracket therefore controls all within-bracket differences by a single pointwise envelope.

### Step 2: Interpret the covering condition

The quantity
$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))
$$
counts balls needed to approximate every function in ordinary $L_2(P)$.

If $f$ and $g$ lie in the same ball of radius $\varepsilon$, then
$$
\|f-g\|_{P,2}\le2\varepsilon.
$$

This controls the variance of the empirical-process increment:
$$
\operatorname{Var}(f(X)-g(X))
\le
P(f-g)^2.
$$

Unlike a bracket, an $L_2(P)$ ball does not provide a common pointwise bound on all its members.

### Step 3: Why require both?

The two conditions control different aspects of the class.

| Condition | What it controls |
|---|---|
| Weak $L_2(P)$ brackets | Tails of a common pointwise bound on within-cell differences |
| Ordinary $L_2(P)$ covers | Second moments and variances of individual differences |
| Envelope tail condition | Extreme values across the whole class |

The proof intersects brackets and balls to obtain partition cells with both types of control.

### Step 4: Compare with the theorem without bracketing

The previous theorem required
$$
\int_0^\infty
\sup_Q
\sqrt{
\log N(\varepsilon\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q))
}\,d\varepsilon
<\infty
$$
and a square-integrable envelope, together with its measurability assumptions.

The new theorem uses entropy under the single measure $P$, but requires two different entropy integrals.

| Feature | Uniform entropy theorem | Hybrid theorem |
|---|---|---|
| Measures appearing in entropy | All admissible finitely supported $Q$ | Population measure $P$ |
| Approximation tools | Covers | Covers and brackets |
| Envelope assumption | $P^\star F^2<\infty$ | $x^2P^\star (F>x)\to0$ |
| Main proof tools | Symmetrization and sub-Gaussian chaining | Bernstein bounds and a truncated chaining construction |

There is no automatic ordering from these statements alone. Which assumptions are easier to verify depends on the class.

The professor emphasized one practical tradeoff: removing the supremum over $Q$ can simplify the entropy condition, but the resulting quantities depend on the potentially unknown population distribution $P$.

### Step 5: Why ordinary $L_2$ bracketing is a useful special case

Suppose instead that
$$
\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{\log N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(P))}
\,d\varepsilon
<\infty.
$$

Because
$$
\|u-l\|_{P,2,\infty}
\le
\|u-l\|_{P,2},
$$
an ordinary $L_2(P)$ bracket is also a weak $L_2(P)$ bracket of no greater width.

Furthermore, choosing a representative from each nonempty bracket gives an $L_2(P)$ cover: for any two functions in that bracket,
$$
|f-g|\le u-l.
$$

Thus ordinary $L_2(P)$ bracketing entropy controls both entropy terms in the hybrid theorem. A square-integrable envelope also supplies the required vanishing-tail condition.

## 5. Proof Strategy: Balancing the Two Terms in Bernstein’s Bound

The proof uses Bernstein’s inequality to control finite collections of empirical-process increments. Chaining then extends those bounds to the possibly infinite class $\mathcal F$.

### Step 1: Recall the two scales in Bernstein’s inequality

For a bounded measurable function $h$, Bernstein’s inequality gives
$$
P\bigl(|\mathbb G_nh|>x\bigr)
\le
2\exp\left\{
-\frac{x^2}
{2\left(Ph^2+\dfrac{2\|h\|_\infty x}{3\sqrt n}\right)}
\right\}.
$$

Here we used
$$
\operatorname{Var}(h(X))\le Ph^2,
\qquad
\|h-Ph\|_\infty\le2\|h\|_\infty.
$$

The exact universal constants are secondary. The important features are:

- a variance term $Ph^2$;
- a boundedness term proportional to $\|h\|_\infty x/\sqrt n$.

### Step 2: Apply the finite-class maximal bound

Suppose $\mathcal A$ is a finite collection with $m$ functions satisfying
$$
\sup_{h\in\mathcal A}\|h\|_{P,2}\le\sigma,
\qquad
\sup_{h\in\mathcal A}\|h\|_\infty\le b.
$$

The Bernstein maximal inequality gives
$$
\boxed{
E\max_{h\in\mathcal A}|\mathbb G_nh|
\le
C\left[
\sigma\sqrt{\log(2m)}
+
\frac{b}{\sqrt n}\log(2m)
\right].
}
$$

The first term reflects variance. The second reflects the size of individual observations.

### Step 3: Balance the two terms

We want the boundedness term to be no larger than the variance term:
$$
\frac{b}{\sqrt n}\log(2m)
\lesssim
\sigma\sqrt{\log(2m)}.
$$

Solving for $b$,
$$
\boxed{
b
\lesssim
\frac{\sqrt n\,\sigma}{\sqrt{\log(2m)}}.
}
$$

Under this balance,
$$
E\max_{h\in\mathcal A}|\mathbb G_nh|
\lesssim
\sigma\sqrt{\log(2m)}.
$$

This resembles a sub-Gaussian maximal bound, despite starting from Bernstein’s inequality.

### Step 4: Why truncation is needed

The theorem does not assume that all functions are uniformly bounded by a finite constant.

Instead, the proof splits each approximation error into:

- a portion where its pointwise size stays below a selected threshold;
- a remainder where that threshold is exceeded.

Bernstein’s inequality controls the bounded portions. The weak $L_2$ bracket bounds control the probabilities and contributions of the remainders.

The thresholds are chosen to achieve the balance above at each approximation scale.

### Step 5: Why chaining is needed

The finite-class bound cannot be applied directly when $\mathcal F$ is infinite.

At level $q$, the proof constructs a finite approximation with error scale
$$
\sigma_q\asymp2^{-q}
$$
and a finite number of possible increments.

If that number is controlled by $N_{q+1}$, the balancing calculation suggests a threshold of order
$$
\sqrt n\,a_q,
\qquad
a_q
\asymp
\frac{2^{-q}}{\sqrt{\log(2N_{q+1})}}.
$$

The corresponding maximal bound has size
$$
2^{-q}\sqrt{\log(2N_{q+1})}.
$$

The entropy assumptions allow these contributions to be summed over increasingly fine levels.

### The professor’s main point

The threshold formula comes from balancing the two terms in Bernstein’s maximal inequality.

The proof combines:

1. finite approximations of the class;
2. variance control at each scale;
3. truncation at thresholds matched to the number of increments;
4. tail control for the discarded portions;
5. summation over the chain.

The next step is to construct nested finite partitions that provide the required variance and tail bounds simultaneously.

## 6. Constructing Nested Finite Partitions

The chaining proof begins by partitioning the function class at successively finer scales.

Each partition must control both:

- ordinary $L_2(P)$ distances between its members;
- the weak $L_2(P)$ size of a common pointwise bound on their differences.

### Step 1: State the desired partition properties

For each integer $q\ge1$, construct a finite partition
$$
\mathcal F
=
\bigcup_{i=1}^{N_q}\mathcal F_{q,i},
$$
with disjoint, nonempty cells.

The partitions are nested: every cell at level $q+1$ lies inside a cell at level $q$.

We require
$$
\boxed{
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{q,i}}
\|f-g\|_{P,2}
\le2^{-q}
}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{
\left\|
\left(
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{q,i}}|f-g|
\right)^\star
\right\|_{P,2,\infty}
\le2^{-q}.
}
$$

The star denotes a measurable majorant of the possibly nonmeasurable pointwise supremum.

The numbers of cells must also satisfy
$$
\boxed{
\sum_{q=1}^\infty
2^{-q}\sqrt{\log(2N_q)}
<\infty.
}
$$

The harmless factor $2$ avoids a zero logarithm when $N_q=1$.

### Step 2: Combine balls and brackets

At each level, cover $\mathcal F$ using:

1. $L_2(P)$ balls of sufficiently small radius;
2. weak $L_2(P)$ brackets of sufficiently small width.

Intersect the two collections.

If $f,g$ lie in the same ball with center $a$ and radius $r$, then
$$
\|f-g\|_{P,2}
\le
\|f-a\|_{P,2}+\|g-a\|_{P,2}
\le2r.
$$

If they also lie in the same bracket $[l,u]$, then
$$
|f-g|\le u-l.
$$

Thus the ball supplies the ordinary $L_2$ diameter bound, and the bracket supplies a common pointwise bound with small weak $L_2$ size.

Choose radii and widths with the required constant factors to obtain the bounds $2^{-q}$.

### Step 3: Make the cells disjoint and nested

The intersections may overlap. Assign each function to one of the intersections containing it, using a fixed ordering, and discard empty cells.

To obtain nesting, refine the preceding partition at each new level. In other words, intersect the new cells with the existing cells.

Refinement cannot increase either diameter bound because it only removes functions from cells.

### Step 4: Connect cell counts to the entropy integrals

Before enforcing nesting, the number of intersections is at most the product of the covering and bracketing numbers.

Therefore its logarithm is bounded by the sum of the corresponding log entropy terms.

Nesting accumulates these counts across earlier levels, but the geometric weights $2^{-q}$ keep that accumulation summable.

The two entropy integrals consequently yield
$$
\sum_{q=1}^\infty
2^{-q}\sqrt{\log(2N_q)}
<\infty.
$$

The professor outlined this construction rather than proving every counting detail.

### Step 5: Choose one representative per cell

For each cell, choose
$$
f_{q,i}\in\mathcal F_{q,i}.
$$

Define
$$
\boxed{
\pi_qf=f_{q,i}
\qquad\text{when }f\in\mathcal F_{q,i}.
}
$$

As $f$ varies over $\mathcal F$, $\pi_qf$ takes at most $N_q$ distinct function values.

Also,
$$
\|f-\pi_qf\|_{P,2}\le2^{-q}.
$$

Thus $\pi_qf$ is a finite approximation to $f$ at level $q$.

### Step 6: Define the cell’s pointwise oscillation envelope

For $f\in\mathcal F_{q,i}$, define
$$
\boxed{
\Delta_qf
=
\left(
\sup_{g,h\in\mathcal F_{q,i}}|g-h|
\right)^\star .
}
$$

This is a function of the observation $x$:
$$
(\Delta_qf)(x)
\ge
\sup_{g,h\in\mathcal F_{q,i}}|g(x)-h(x)|.
$$

In particular,
$$
|f-\pi_qf|\le\Delta_qf,
\qquad
\|\Delta_qf\|_{P,2,\infty}\le2^{-q}.
$$

**Important:** $\Delta_qf$ is a measurable envelope function, not a single numerical diameter. Its weak $L_2$ size is a number.

As $f$ varies, $\Delta_qf$ takes at most $N_q$ distinct function values, one for each cell.

### Why nesting matters for the chain

The representatives $\pi_qf$ and $\pi_{q-1}f$ belong to the same level-$(q-1)$ cell.

Therefore,
$$
\|\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f\|_{P,2}
\le2^{-(q-1)}
$$
and
$$
|\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f|
\le\Delta_{q-1}f.
$$

These are exactly the variance and pointwise bounds needed to control successive chaining increments.

## 7. The Target: Approximation by a Finite Partition

Before developing the chain further, the professor returned to the goal: prove asymptotic tightness of
$$
\mathbb G_n
\quad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F).
$$

The nested partitions provide a way to apply the finite-partition tightness criterion from Chapter 1.

### Step 1: Recall the tightness criterion

A sequence of processes $X_n$ indexed by $T$ is asymptotically tight if:

1. each coordinate $X_n(t)$ is asymptotically tight;
2. for every $\varepsilon>0$ and $\eta>0$, there is a finite partition
   $$
   T=\bigcup_{i=1}^kT_i
   $$
   such that
   $$
   \limsup_{n\to\infty}
   P^\star\left(
   \max_{1\le i\le k}
   \sup_{s,t\in T_i}|X_n(s)-X_n(t)|
   >\varepsilon
   \right)
   <\eta.
   $$

The second condition says that a finite partition can make the process’s within-cell oscillations small with high probability.

### Step 2: Apply it to the empirical process

Here,
$$
T=\mathcal F,
\qquad
X_n(f)=\mathbb G_nf.
$$

For each fixed $f$, the covering assumption places the class in $L_2(P)$, so the ordinary central limit theorem gives
$$
\mathbb G_nf
\Rightarrow
N(0,Pf^2-(Pf)^2).
$$

Thus the coordinate tightness requirement is satisfied.

We must control the oscillations within the cells $\mathcal F_{q_0,i}$.

### Step 3: Compare each function with its representative

Define
$$
Z_{n,q_0}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|\mathbb G_n(f-\pi_{q_0}f)\right|.
$$

If $f,g$ lie in the same level-$q_0$ cell, they have the same representative:
$$
\pi_{q_0}f=\pi_{q_0}g.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng
=
\mathbb G_n(f-\pi_{q_0}f)
-
\mathbb G_n(g-\pi_{q_0}g).
$$

By the triangle inequality,
$$
|\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng|
\le2Z_{n,q_0}.
$$

Taking the supremum over all pairs within each cell,
$$
\boxed{
\max_{1\le i\le N_{q_0}}
\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{q_0,i}}
|\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng|
\le2Z_{n,q_0}.
}
$$

### Step 4: State the precise approximation goal

It is enough to prove that, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\boxed{
\lim_{q_0\to\infty}
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star (Z_{n,q_0}>\varepsilon)
=
0.
}
$$

The order matters:

1. fix a finite approximation level $q_0$;
2. let the sample size grow;
3. then make the approximation increasingly fine.

For any desired probability tolerance $\eta$, we can then choose a sufficiently large, fixed $q_0$. Its finite partition satisfies the tightness criterion.

### Step 5: Why small $L_2(P)$ error is not enough by itself

We already know
$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\|f-\pi_{q_0}f\|_{P,2}
\le2^{-q_0}.
$$

This bounds the variance of each individual approximation error.

However, our target involves the supremum over the entire class:
$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
|\mathbb G_n(f-\pi_{q_0}f)|.
$$

Controlling each function separately does not control that supremum. Chaining and entropy account for how many different errors the process can exhibit at each scale.

### Interpretation

The process
$$
f\longmapsto\mathbb G_n(\pi_{q_0}f)
$$
takes only finitely many values, one per partition cell.

The desired bound says that this finite approximation captures the full empirical process uniformly, with an error that becomes small as the partition is refined.

## 8. Truncation Indicators: Where the Chain Continues or Stops

The next construction determines which chaining increments are small enough for Bernstein’s inequality.

The decision is made separately at each observation point $x$.

### Step 1: Define the thresholds

Fix a starting level $q_0$. Set
$$
\boxed{
a_q
=
\frac{2^{-q}}{\sqrt{\log(2N_{q+1})}}.
}
$$

The lecture writes $\log N_{q+1}$; using $\log(2N_{q+1})$ avoids a zero denominator and changes only universal constants.

The pointwise truncation threshold at level $q$ is
$$
\sqrt n\,a_q.
$$

This is the threshold suggested by balancing the two terms in Bernstein’s maximal inequality.

### Step 2: Define the continuation indicator

For $q\ge q_0$, define
$$
\boxed{
A_qf(x)
=
\mathbf1\left\{
\Delta_kf(x)\le\sqrt n\,a_k
\text{ for every }k=q_0,\ldots,q
\right\}.
}
$$

Thus $A_qf(x)=1$ precisely when every test through level $q$ has passed.

The events are nested:
$$
A_qf(x)\le A_{q-1}f(x).
$$

Once a test fails, the continuation indicators remain zero at all later levels.

### Step 3: Define the first-failure indicator

For $q>q_0$, define
$$
\boxed{
B_qf(x)
=
\mathbf1\left\{
\begin{array}{l}
\Delta_kf(x)\le\sqrt n\,a_k
\quad\text{for }k=q_0,\ldots,q-1,\\
\Delta_qf(x)>\sqrt n\,a_q
\end{array}
\right\}.
}
$$

At the starting level, define
$$
\boxed{
B_{q_0}f(x)
=
\mathbf1\{\Delta_{q_0}f(x)>\sqrt n\,a_{q_0}\}.
}
$$

Therefore $B_qf(x)=1$ means that level $q$ is the first failed test.

For $q>q_0$,
$$
B_qf=A_{q-1}f-A_qf.
$$

### Step 4: Understand the two possible outcomes

Fix $f$ and $x$. There are two possibilities.

**A first failure occurs at some level $r$.**

Then
$$
B_rf(x)=1,
$$
all other $B_qf(x)$ are zero, and
$$
A_qf(x)=
\begin{cases}
1,&q<r,\\
0,&q\ge r.
\end{cases}
$$

**No failure ever occurs.**

Then
$$
B_qf(x)=0
\quad\text{for every }q,
$$
and
$$
A_qf(x)=1
\quad\text{for every }q\ge q_0.
$$

The stopping level depends on both $f$ and $x$. It need not be the same across sample observations.

### Step 5: Explain how this bounds a chaining increment

Nesting of the partitions gives
$$
|\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f|
\le\Delta_{q-1}f.
$$

On the continuation event $A_{q-1}f=1$,
$$
\Delta_{q-1}f\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}.
$$

Hence
$$
\boxed{
|(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f|
\le\sqrt n\,a_{q-1}.
}
$$

This makes the retained increment a bounded function, suitable for Bernstein’s inequality.

Notice the indexing: the increment from level $q-1$ to level $q$ is retained when the test at level $q-1$ has passed.

### Step 6: Keep the indexing and observation spaces separate

For fixed $q$, functions in the same partition cell share the same $\Delta_qf$. Nesting also makes their earlier tests agree.

Thus $A_qf$ and $B_qf$ are constant as choices of functions across a level-$q$ cell.

They are still indicator functions of $x$, and their values may differ at different observation points.

## 9. The Stopped Chaining Decomposition

The lecture ends by decomposing the approximation error
$$
f-\pi_{q_0}f
$$
into retained increments and remainders at the first failed truncation test.

### Step 1: Recall ordinary telescoping

For a finite level $Q>q_0$,
$$
f-\pi_{q_0}f
=
(f-\pi_Qf)
+
\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{Q}
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f).
$$

Without truncation, some increments may have large pointwise values. The indicators $A_qf$ and $B_qf$ modify the chain so that its retained increments are bounded.

### Step 2: Write the finite stopped identity

For every $Q\ge q_0$,
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
f-\pi_{q_0}f
={}&
(f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f\\
&+\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{Q}(f-\pi_qf)B_qf\\
&+\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{Q}
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f\\
&+(f-\pi_Qf)A_Qf.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is a pointwise identity: evaluate every function and indicator at the same observation point $x$.

### Step 3: Check an immediate failure

If the first test fails at level $q_0$, then
$$
B_{q_0}f(x)=1.
$$

Every continuation indicator and later first-failure indicator is zero. The right side reduces to
$$
f(x)-\pi_{q_0}f(x).
$$

### Step 4: Check a later failure

Suppose the first failure occurs at level $r>q_0$, with $r\le Q$.

The retained increments are exactly those with
$$
q=q_0+1,\ldots,r.
$$

They telescope:
$$
\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{r}
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)
=
\pi_rf-\pi_{q_0}f.
$$

The first-failure remainder is
$$
(f-\pi_rf)B_rf=f-\pi_rf.
$$

Adding them gives
$$
(f-\pi_rf)+(\pi_rf-\pi_{q_0}f)
=
f-\pi_{q_0}f.
$$

The increment arriving at level $r$ is included because the level-$(r-1)$ test passed. The failure at level $r$ stops subsequent increments.

### Step 5: Check continued passage through level $Q$

If every test through level $Q$ passes, then all first-failure terms through that level vanish.

The retained increments telescope to
$$
\pi_Qf-\pi_{q_0}f.
$$

Adding the final remainder,
$$
(f-\pi_Qf)+(\pi_Qf-\pi_{q_0}f)
=
f-\pi_{q_0}f.
$$

Thus the finite identity holds in every case.

### Step 6: Pass to the infinite decomposition

If a first failure occurs, the final remainder
$$
(f-\pi_Qf)A_Qf
$$
is zero for all sufficiently large $Q$.

If no failure occurs, then
$$
|f-\pi_Qf|
\le\Delta_Qf
\le\sqrt n\,a_Q
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Therefore the final remainder vanishes pointwise, giving
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
f-\pi_{q_0}f
={}&
(f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f\\
&+\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{\infty}(f-\pi_qf)B_qf\\
&+\sum_{q=q_0+1}^{\infty}
(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

### Step 7: Identify the three pieces

| Piece | Meaning | Intended control |
|---|---|---|
| $(f-\pi_{q_0}f)B_{q_0}f$ | Failure at the starting level | Tail bounds |
| $\sum_{q>q_0}(f-\pi_qf)B_qf$ | Remainder at a later first failure | Weak $L_2$ bounds |
| $\sum_{q>q_0}(\pi_qf-\pi_{q-1}f)A_{q-1}f$ | Retained bounded increments | Bernstein maximal bounds and entropy summability |

The professor planned to bound the empirical-process contribution of each piece separately.

For rigorous expectation arguments, we begin with the finite identity. Passing an empirical-process operator through an infinite sum requires the additional convergence bounds developed in the continuation of the proof.
