# September 23 — Chaining and the Entropy Integral

## 1. What the chaining theorem controls

Let $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ be a separable stochastic process indexed by a
semimetric space $(T,d)$.

Suppose its increments satisfy
$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_\psi \le C\,d(s,t),
\qquad s,t\in T.
$$

This assumption connects the geometry of the index set to the random
behavior of the process: nearby indices have small increments in
Orlicz norm.

It does **not** assert the pathwise inequality
$$
|X_s-X_t|\le C\,d(s,t).
$$
The Orlicz norm controls the distribution of the increment, including
its tails.

### Theorem 2.2.4

Assume $\psi$ is convex, nondecreasing, nonzero, satisfies $\psi(0)=0$,
and, for some $c>0$,
$$
\limsup_{x,y\to\infty}
\frac{\psi(x)\psi(y)}{\psi(cxy)}
<\infty.
$$

Then, for every $\eta,\delta>0$,
$$
\left\|
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right],
$$
where $K$ depends only on $\psi$ and $C$.

Here $D(\varepsilon,d)$ is the packing number: the maximum number of
points in $T$ whose pairwise distances are strictly greater than
$\varepsilon$.

### What is on the left?

Define the random continuity modulus
$$
\omega_X(\delta)
=
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|.
$$

For a particular realization of the process, this measures the
largest change between any two indices at distance at most $\delta$.

The theorem bounds the Orlicz norm of this random quantity. Thus it
controls all nearby pairs simultaneously, rather than just one
fixed pair.

### What are the two terms on the right?

The entropy integral
$$
I(\eta)
=
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
$$
accounts for the accumulated cost of approximating the index set
at increasingly fine scales.

Smaller scales give shorter increments, but usually require more
representative points. The integral balances these two effects.

The second term,
$$
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr),
$$
controls the remaining comparison between chains at the coarse
scale $\eta$. The square arises because we compare pairs of
coarse representatives.

The parameters have different roles:
- $\eta$ sets the starting scale for the chaining construction.
- $\delta$ specifies how close the pairs on the left must be.

### Why this helps establish continuity

Suppose the entropy integral is finite for some $\eta_0>0$.

First choose $\eta$ small enough that $I(\eta)$ is small.
Then, holding that $\eta$ fixed, choose $\delta$ small enough that
the second term is small.

Consequently,
$$
\|\omega_X(\delta)\|_\psi\longrightarrow 0
\qquad\text{as }\delta\downarrow0.
$$

This gives probabilistic control of small-scale oscillations.
The professor emphasized that such control will be useful when
studying asymptotic equicontinuity.

### Check your understanding

Why do we choose $\eta$ first and then $\delta$?

Because decreasing $\eta$ reduces the integral, but can increase
$D(\eta,d)$. Once $\eta$ is fixed, its packing number is fixed,
and decreasing $\delta$ controls the second term.

## 2. Constructing the nested approximations and chains

Fix $\eta>0$. We construct finite, nested subsets
$$
T_0\subseteq T_1\subseteq T_2\subseteq\cdots\subseteq T,
$$
where each $T_j$ is a maximal $\eta 2^{-j}$-separated set.

Thus, for distinct $s,t\in T_j$,
$$
d(s,t)>\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

As $j$ increases, the required separation decreases:
$$
\eta,\quad \frac{\eta}{2},\quad \frac{\eta}{4},\quad\ldots.
$$
This allows us to add more points and obtain finer approximations
of $T$.

### “Maximal” means no further point can be added

A separated set is **maximal** if adding any new point from $T$
would violate the separation requirement.

This does not mean it has the largest possible cardinality.
Different maximal separated sets may have different sizes.

Nevertheless, the definition of the packing number gives
$$
|T_j|\le D(\eta 2^{-j},d).
$$

When the packing number is finite, we can construct $T_j$ by adding
points until no more can be added. To construct $T_{j+1}$, start
with $T_j$ and extend it at the finer separation scale.

### Why maximality gives coverage

For every $t\in T$, there exists $u\in T_j$ such that
$$
d(t,u)\le\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

Otherwise,
$$
d(t,u)>\eta 2^{-j}
\qquad\text{for every }u\in T_j,
$$
so we could add $t$ to $T_j$ while preserving separation.
That contradicts maximality.

Therefore, $T_j$ serves two purposes:
- Its distinct points are separated by more than $\eta 2^{-j}$.
- Its closed balls of radius $\eta 2^{-j}$ cover $T$.

The professor described these nested approximations as an
increasingly fine “sieve” over the index set.

### Linking consecutive levels

For each $t_{j+1}\in T_{j+1}$, choose one point $t_j\in T_j$
satisfying
$$
d(t_{j+1},t_j)\le\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

Such a point exists because $T_j$ covers $T$ at that scale.

There may be several eligible points. We choose one and keep
that choice fixed. Thus, the parent is unique by our assignment,
rather than necessarily unique geometrically.

Repeating this assignment gives a chain
$$
t_{k+1},t_k,\ldots,t_1,t_0,
$$
connecting a fine-scale point to a coarse representative in $T_0$.

### Why these links are useful

The increment assumption implies that every link satisfies
$$
\|X_{t_{j+1}}-X_{t_j}\|_\psi
\le C\,d(t_{j+1},t_j)
\le C\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

Also, because each point of $T_{j+1}$ has exactly one assigned parent,
the number of assigned links at level $j$ is at most
$$
|T_{j+1}|
\le D(\eta 2^{-(j+1)},d).
$$

We now have the two ingredients needed for the maximal inequality:
1. A bound on the size of each random increment.
2. A bound on how many increments we must maximize over.

### Check your understanding

Why does separation alone fail to guarantee coverage?

Because a separated set could contain only a few points and leave
large regions uncovered. Maximality guarantees that any uncovered
point could not be added, which forces every point to be close
to an existing representative.

## 3. Telescoping along two chains

Take two points $s_{k+1},t_{k+1}\in T_{k+1}$ and follow their
assigned chains back to $T_0$:
$$
s_{k+1},s_k,\ldots,s_0,
\qquad
t_{k+1},t_k,\ldots,t_0.
$$

### Step 1: Write each increment as a telescoping sum

Along the first chain,
$$
X_{s_{k+1}}-X_{s_0}
=
\sum_{j=0}^{k}(X_{s_{j+1}}-X_{s_j}).
$$

All intermediate terms cancel. Similarly,
$$
X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_0}
=
\sum_{j=0}^{k}(X_{t_{j+1}}-X_{t_j}).
$$

Subtracting gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
&(X_{s_{k+1}}-X_{s_0})
-
(X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_0})\\
&\qquad=
\sum_{j=0}^{k}
\left[
(X_{s_{j+1}}-X_{s_j})
-
(X_{t_{j+1}}-X_{t_j})
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 2: Bound each level by its largest link

Let $L_j$ be the collection of assigned links from $T_{j+1}$ to $T_j$,
and define
$$
M_j=\max_{(u,v)\in L_j}|X_u-X_v|.
$$

Both chains use a link from $L_j$ at level $j$. Hence,
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left|
(X_{s_{k+1}}-X_{s_0})
-
(X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_0})
\right|\\
&\qquad\le
\sum_{j=0}^{k}
\left(
|X_{s_{j+1}}-X_{s_j}|
+
|X_{t_{j+1}}-X_{t_j}|
\right)\\
&\qquad\le 2\sum_{j=0}^{k}M_j.
\end{aligned}
$$

The factor $2$ appears because we are bounding two chains.

### Step 3: Maximize over the starting points

Write $s_0(s)$ for the coarse endpoint of the chain starting at $s$,
and similarly $t_0(t)$.

Define
$$
A_k=
\max_{s,t\in T_{k+1}}
\left|
(X_s-X_{s_0(s)})
-
(X_t-X_{t_0(t)})
\right|.
$$

The preceding bound holds for every pair, so
$$
A_k\le 2\sum_{j=0}^{k}M_j.
$$

Using monotonicity and the triangle inequality for the Orlicz norm,
$$
\|A_k\|_\psi
\le 2\sum_{j=0}^{k}\|M_j\|_\psi.
$$

### Step 4: Apply the finite maximal inequality

Lemma 2.2.2 states that, for some constant $K_\psi$,
$$
\left\|\max_{1\le i\le m}|Y_i|\right\|_\psi
\le
K_\psi\,\psi^{-1}(m)\,
\max_{1\le i\le m}\|Y_i\|_\psi.
$$

At level $j$, we know
$$
|L_j|\le D(\eta 2^{-(j+1)},d)
$$
and
$$
\max_{(u,v)\in L_j}\|X_u-X_v\|_\psi
\le C\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\|M_j\|_\psi
\le
K_\psi C\eta 2^{-j}
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta 2^{-(j+1)},d)\bigr).
$$

Combining the bounds,
$$
\boxed{
\|A_k\|_\psi
\le
2K_\psi C
\sum_{j=0}^{k}
\eta 2^{-j}
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta 2^{-(j+1)},d)\bigr).
}
$$

No independence between links is required by this maximal inequality.

### What have we controlled so far?

We have controlled the changes along the chains, after subtracting
their coarse endpoints.

We have not yet bounded $|X_s-X_t|$ completely, because
$$
X_s-X_t
=
\left[(X_s-X_{s_0})-(X_t-X_{t_0})\right]
+
(X_{s_0}-X_{t_0}).
$$

The coarse-endpoint difference remains to be handled.

### Check your understanding

Why does the packing number use scale $\eta 2^{-(j+1)}$,
while the increment bound uses scale $\eta 2^{-j}$?

We count links using the number of points in the finer set $T_{j+1}$.
But each link reaches back to $T_j$, whose covering radius is
$\eta 2^{-j}$.


## 4. From the chaining sum to the entropy integral

Our previous bound has the form
$$
\|A_k\|_\psi
\le
B\sum_{j=0}^{k}
\eta 2^{-j}
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta 2^{-(j+1)},d)\bigr),
$$
where $B=2K_\psi C$.

Define
$$
f(\varepsilon)
=
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr).
$$

Then
$$
\|A_k\|_\psi
\le B\sum_{j=0}^{k}\eta 2^{-j}f(\eta 2^{-(j+1)}).
$$

### Step 1: Observe the direction of monotonicity

As $\varepsilon$ increases, fewer separated points can fit in $T$.
Thus $D(\varepsilon,d)$ is nonincreasing.

Because $\psi^{-1}$ is nondecreasing, $f$ is nonincreasing:
$$
\varepsilon_1\le\varepsilon_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f(\varepsilon_1)\ge f(\varepsilon_2).
$$

The integrand therefore becomes larger as we approach zero.

### Step 2: Bound each summand by an integral

Consider the interval
$$
I_j=
[\eta 2^{-(j+2)},\,\eta 2^{-(j+1)}].
$$

For every $\varepsilon\in I_j$,
$$
f(\varepsilon)\ge f(\eta 2^{-(j+1)}).
$$

The interval has length $\eta 2^{-(j+2)}$, so
$$
\int_{I_j}f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon
\ge
\eta 2^{-(j+2)}f(\eta 2^{-(j+1)}).
$$

Multiplying by $4$ gives
$$
\eta 2^{-j}f(\eta 2^{-(j+1)})
\le
4\int_{I_j}f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

### Step 3: Sum over the disjoint intervals

The intervals $I_0,\ldots,I_k$ have disjoint interiors and together
cover
$$
[\eta 2^{-(k+2)},\,\eta/2].
$$

Consequently,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{j=0}^{k}
\eta 2^{-j}f(\eta 2^{-(j+1)})
&\le
4\int_{\eta 2^{-(k+2)}}^{\eta/2}
f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon\\
&\le
4\int_0^\eta f(\varepsilon)\,d\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
\|A_k\|_\psi
\le
4B\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

This bound is uniform in $k$: adding finer levels does not increase
the upper bound beyond the same integral.

### Why the integral matters

Each scale contributes two competing quantities:
- The link size decreases like $\eta 2^{-j}$.
- The number of links can increase as the scale becomes finer.

The entropy integral measures whether the accumulated cost across
all these scales remains finite.

For sub-Gaussian increments, we use
$$
\psi_2(x)=e^{x^2}-1,
\qquad
\psi_2^{-1}(m)=\sqrt{\log(1+m)}.
$$

The integral then becomes
$$
\int_0^\eta
\sqrt{\log(1+D(\varepsilon,d))}\,d\varepsilon,
$$
the familiar form of a Dudley entropy integral.

If this integral diverges, this particular bound provides no finite
control. Divergence alone does not prove that the process is unbounded.

### Check your understanding

Why did we integrate over an interval to the left of
$\eta 2^{-(j+1)}$?

Because $f$ is nonincreasing. On that interval, its values are at
least as large as the value appearing in our summand, giving the
upper bound we need.


## 5. Controlling the coarse-endpoint differences

Let
$$
I(\eta)=
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

We have already proved
$$
\|A_k\|_\psi\le K_1 I(\eta),
$$
where
$$
A_k=
\max_{s,t\in T_{k+1}}
\left|
(X_s-X_{s_0})-(X_t-X_{t_0})
\right|.
$$

Here $s_0,t_0$ are the assigned endpoints of the chains.

Our goal is to bound
$$
W_k(\delta)=
\max_{\substack{s,t\in T_{k+1}\\d(s,t)\le\delta}}
|X_s-X_t|.
$$

### Step 1: Separate the chain increments from the endpoints

For every eligible pair,
$$
X_s-X_t
=
\left[(X_s-X_{s_0})-(X_t-X_{t_0})\right]
+
(X_{s_0}-X_{t_0}).
$$

Thus,
$$
W_k(\delta)\le A_k+E_k,
$$
where $E_k$ is the maximum of $|X_a-X_b|$ over those endpoint
pairs $(a,b)\in T_0\times T_0$ arising from an eligible close pair.

The difficulty is that these coarse endpoints need not themselves
be within distance $\delta$.

### Step 2: Choose one representative close pair per endpoint pair

For each relevant endpoint pair $(a,b)$, choose one pair
$$
s^{a,b},t^{a,b}\in T_{k+1}
$$
such that
$$
d(s^{a,b},t^{a,b})\le\delta,
$$
and whose chains end at $a,b$, respectively.

Such a pair exists by the definition of a relevant endpoint pair.

There are at most
$$
|T_0|^2\le D(\eta,d)^2
$$
chosen pairs.

Define
$$
R_k=
\max_{(a,b)}
|X_{s^{a,b}}-X_{t^{a,b}}|.
$$

### Step 3: Bound the endpoints using the selected pairs

For each relevant $(a,b)$,
$$
X_a-X_b
=
(X_a-X_{s^{a,b}})
-
(X_b-X_{t^{a,b}})
+
(X_{s^{a,b}}-X_{t^{a,b}}).
$$

The first two terms together are controlled by $A_k$. Therefore,
$$
E_k\le A_k+R_k.
$$

Combining this with Step 1,
$$
W_k(\delta)\le 2A_k+R_k.
$$

Although the argument returns to fine-scale increments, it is not
circular: $R_k$ involves only one selected pair per endpoint pair,
rather than all close pairs in $T_{k+1}$.

### Step 4: Apply the finite maximal inequality

Each selected pair satisfies
$$
\|X_{s^{a,b}}-X_{t^{a,b}}\|_\psi
\le C\,d(s^{a,b},t^{a,b})
\le C\delta.
$$

There are at most $D(\eta,d)^2$ such increments, so Lemma 2.2.2 gives
$$
\|R_k\|_\psi
\le
K_\psi C\delta\,
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr).
$$

Consequently,
$$
\|W_k(\delta)\|_\psi
\le
2K_1 I(\eta)
+
K_\psi C\delta\,
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr).
$$

Absorbing the constants into one constant $K$,
$$
\boxed{
\|W_k(\delta)\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
I(\eta)
+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
}
$$

This is the theorem's bound on the finite approximation $T_{k+1}$,
with a constant independent of $k$.

### Check your understanding

Why select only one close pair for each coarse-endpoint pair?

Every pair with the same endpoints has the same leftover term
$X_a-X_b$. One representative is enough to control that term,
and limits the number of comparisons to $|T_0|^2$.


## 6. Passing from finite approximations to the full process

For every $k$, we have established
$$
\left\|
\max_{\substack{s,t\in T_{k+1}\\d(s,t)\le\delta}}
|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
I(\eta)+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right],
$$
where
$$
I(\eta)=
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

The constant $K$ does not depend on $k$.

### Step 1: The approximations become arbitrarily fine

Since $T_j$ is maximal at separation scale $\eta 2^{-j}$,
for every $t\in T$ we can choose $\pi_j(t)\in T_j$ with
$$
d(t,\pi_j(t))\le\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
d(t,\pi_j(t))\longrightarrow0.
$$

The union $\bigcup_jT_j$ is dense in the semimetric sense.
It need not contain every point of $T$.

### Step 2: Process values converge at each fixed index

The increment assumption gives
$$
\|X_t-X_{\pi_j(t)}\|_\psi
\le C\eta 2^{-j}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Hence,
$$
X_{\pi_j(t)}\longrightarrow X_t
\qquad\text{in probability}.
$$

This uses the assumed Orlicz increment bound. We do not assume
sample-path continuity to justify the approximation.

### Step 3: Approximate a finite collection of close pairs

If $d(s,t)\le\delta$, then the triangle inequality gives
$$
d(\pi_j(s),\pi_j(t))
\le\delta+2\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

Thus we apply the finite bound with the slightly enlarged radius
$$
\delta_j=\delta+2\eta 2^{-j}.
$$

For any fixed finite collection of close pairs, their approximated
increments converge in probability to the original increments.

Taking an almost surely convergent subsequence and applying Fatou's
lemma to the defining Orlicz expectations transfers the bound to
the maximum over this finite collection.

Because $\delta_j\to\delta$, the resulting bound is
$$
K\left[
I(\eta)+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
$$

### Step 4: Use separability to reach the full supremum

For this theorem, separability allows the relevant supremum to be
determined almost surely using a suitable countable subset of $T$.

Enumerate its eligible pairs and take maxima over the first $m$ pairs.
These finite maxima increase to the countable supremum.

Monotone convergence in the Orlicz expectations preserves the bound.
Separability then identifies this with the full supremum, almost surely.

We obtain
$$
\boxed{
\left\|
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
}
$$

This completes the chaining proof of Theorem 2.2.4.

### Distinguish the two ingredients

The dense approximations concern the geometry of $(T,d)$.

Process separability concerns whether a countable collection of
indices captures the random supremum. Geometric density alone
does not automatically guarantee that property.

### Check your understanding

Why enlarge $\delta$ when approximating a close pair?

Each endpoint moves slightly. Even if $d(s,t)\le\delta$, the
approximating points may be farther apart than $\delta$.
The extra allowance vanishes as the approximation becomes finer.

## 7. The diameter corollary and a bound on the whole process

Let
$$
r=\operatorname{diam}(T)
=\sup_{s,t\in T}d(s,t),
$$
and assume $0<r<\infty$.

### Corollary 2.2.5: Control all increments

Under the assumptions of Theorem 2.2.4,
$$
\boxed{
\left\|\sup_{s,t\in T}|X_s-X_t|\right\|_\psi
\le
K\int_0^r
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

This controls the largest difference between process values
anywhere in the index set.

### Why the coarse-endpoint term disappears

In the chaining construction, choose $\eta=r$.

The set $T_0$ must have pairwise distances strictly greater than $r$.
But no two points in $T$ have distance greater than its diameter.

Therefore, a maximal such set contains exactly one point:
$$
T_0=\{t_0\}.
$$

Every chain ends at this same point. Consequently,
$$
X_{s_0}-X_{t_0}=X_{t_0}-X_{t_0}=0.
$$

The chain increments alone now control $X_s-X_t$, leaving only
the entropy integral.

### From increments to the magnitude of the process

Fix any reference index $t_0\in T$. For every $t\in T$,
$$
|X_t|
\le |X_{t_0}|+|X_t-X_{t_0}|.
$$

Taking the supremum and then the Orlicz norm gives
$$
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|\right\|_\psi
\le
\|X_{t_0}\|_\psi
+
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t-X_{t_0}|\right\|_\psi.
$$

Applying the increment bound,
$$
\boxed{
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|\right\|_\psi
\le
\|X_{t_0}\|_\psi
+
K\int_0^r
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

Thus, controlling the whole process requires:
- Control of its value at one reference index.
- Control of its fluctuations through the entropy integral.

### Why the reference value is necessary

Suppose
$$
X_t=Z
\qquad\text{for every }t\in T,
$$
where $Z$ is a random variable.

Then every increment is zero:
$$
X_s-X_t=0.
$$

Nevertheless,
$$
\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|=|Z|,
$$
which can have heavy tails.

Increment bounds control changes in the process. They do not
control a random level shared by every index.

If $X_{t_0}=0$ almost surely, the reference term vanishes.

### What finiteness tells us

If
$$
\|X_{t_0}\|_\psi<\infty
\quad\text{and}\quad
\int_0^r
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon<\infty,
$$
then
$$
\left\|\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|\right\|_\psi<\infty.
$$

In particular, the process is bounded over $T$ almost surely.
This is a random bound; it does not say every realization lies
below one fixed deterministic ceiling.

### Check your understanding

Can a process have small increments but large absolute values?

Yes. Adding the same random variable $Z$ to every process value
leaves all increments unchanged, while potentially increasing
the magnitude of the entire process.



## 8. Interpreting chaining and connecting it to equicontinuity

### The professor's main interpretation

Chaining does not turn an infinite-dimensional problem into a
finite-dimensional one.

Instead, it measures the complexity of the index set through
packing numbers at successively finer scales.

At each scale, we balance:
- How small the process increments are.
- How many increments we must control simultaneously.

The entropy integral collects these costs:
$$
I(\eta)
=
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon.
$$

The relevant complexity is therefore more specific than simply
saying that $T$ is infinite or infinite-dimensional.

### Why finite packing numbers alone are insufficient

Total boundedness means
$$
D(\varepsilon,d)<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
$$

This ensures that each individual approximation is finite.

However, chaining uses infinitely many scales. We also need the
packing numbers to grow slowly enough that the entropy integral
converges.

For example, in the sub-Gaussian case,
$$
\psi_2^{-1}(m)=\sqrt{\log(1+m)}.
$$

If, for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$,
$$
D(\varepsilon,d)\le A\varepsilon^{-v},
$$
then the integrand grows at most on the order of
$$
\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)},
$$
which is integrable near zero.

Thus, even though the number of representatives grows without
bound as the scale shrinks, the total chaining cost can be finite.

### From Orlicz control to probability control

Define
$$
\omega_X(\delta)
=
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|.
$$

The theorem gives
$$
\|\omega_X(\delta)\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
I(\eta)+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
$$

If $\|Y\|_\psi<a$, the Orlicz definition and Markov's inequality give
$$
P(|Y|>u)
\le
\frac{1}{\psi(u/a)},
\qquad u>0,
$$
whenever the denominator is positive.

Consequently, making the Orlicz norm small makes the probability
of a fixed-size fluctuation small.

If $I(\eta_0)<\infty$ for some $\eta_0>0$, first choose $\eta$ small,
and then choose $\delta$ small. This yields
$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}
P\bigl(\omega_X(\delta)>u\bigr)=0
\qquad\text{for every }u>0.
$$

### Connection to asymptotic equicontinuity

For a sequence of processes $X_n$, the corresponding target is
$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star\left(
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_n(s)-X_n(t)|>u
\right)=0,
\qquad u>0.
$$

The outer probability $P^\star $ accommodates possible measurability
issues with the supremum.

Chaining can establish this if the increment and entropy bounds
are sufficiently uniform in $n$.

Applying the theorem separately to each $X_n$ is not enough:
the resulting constants or entropy integrals could deteriorate
as $n$ increases.

### What the theorem assumes about the index set

The packing numbers describe the entire index set under the
chosen semimetric. They are not automatically inferred from a
finite observed sample.

To use the theorem, we must establish appropriate bounds on
those packing numbers and on the process increments.

### Check your understanding

What is the difference between these two claims?

1. Every scale requires only finitely many representatives.
2. The accumulated chaining cost over all scales is finite.

The first is total boundedness. The second requires an additional
growth-rate condition, expressed here by the entropy integral.


## 9. Lecture summary — The chaining method

Chaining controls a stochastic process by expressing its increments
as sums of shorter increments at successively finer scales.

The proof proceeds as follows:

1. Construct nested maximal separated sets
   $T_0\subseteq T_1\subseteq\cdots$, using scales $\eta 2^{-j}$.
2. Assign each fine-scale point a nearby parent in the previous set.
3. Telescope process increments along the resulting chains.
4. Apply the finite maximal inequality at each level.
5. Bound the sum over levels by an entropy integral.
6. Control the coarse-endpoint differences using at most
   $D(\eta,d)^2$ representative pairs.
7. Extend the bound to the full process using increment control
   and separability.

The resulting inequality is
$$
\left\|
\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\right\|_\psi
\le
K\left[
\int_0^\eta
\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\varepsilon,d)\bigr)\,d\varepsilon
+
\delta\,\psi^{-1}\!\bigl(D(\eta,d)^2\bigr)
\right].
$$

The central idea is a balance between **increment size** and
**the number of increments**.

A finite entropy integral allows this balance to remain manageable
across infinitely many scales. Choosing $\eta$ first and then
$\delta$ makes small-scale oscillations small in Orlicz norm
and therefore in probability.

To control the absolute magnitude of the process, also control
its value at one reference index.
