# September 25 — Sub-Gaussian Bounds and Bernstein’s Inequality

## 1. From Gaussian tails to Rademacher sums

### The Gaussian benchmark

Recall that if $Z\sim N(0,1)$, then
$$
P(|Z|>x)\le 2e^{-x^2/2},
\qquad x>0.
$$

The important feature is the quadratic exponent: large deviations
are controlled by a bound of the form $e^{-cx^2}$.

For the Orlicz function
$$
\psi_2(u)=e^{u^2}-1,
$$
the standard normal has norm
$$
\|Z\|_{\psi_2}=\sqrt{\frac83}.
$$

Indeed, for $c>\sqrt2$,
$$
E\exp(Z^2/c^2)
=
(1-2/c^2)^{-1/2}.
$$

The condition
$$
E\psi_2(|Z|/c)\le1
$$
is therefore equivalent to
$$
(1-2/c^2)^{-1/2}\le2,
$$
which holds exactly when $c^2\ge8/3$.

The next question is whether non-Gaussian random variables can
satisfy a similar tail bound.

### Rademacher random variables

A Rademacher random variable $\varepsilon$ takes the values
$+1$ and $-1$ with equal probability:
$$
P(\varepsilon=1)=P(\varepsilon=-1)=\frac12.
$$

Consequently,
$$
E\varepsilon=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)=1.
$$

Let $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$ be independent Rademacher
variables, and let $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ be deterministic real numbers.

Consider the randomly signed sum
$$
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\varepsilon_i.
$$

Its mean and variance are
$$
ES=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2=\|a\|_2^2.
$$

### Hoeffding’s inequality — Lemma 2.2.7

For $x>0$ and $\|a\|_2>0$,
$$
\boxed{
P\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\varepsilon_i\right|>x\right)
\le
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\|a\|_2^2}\right).
}
$$

If every $a_i=0$, the sum is identically zero.

Although $S$ generally has a discrete distribution, its tails
satisfy the same upper bound as a centered Gaussian with variance
$\|a\|_2^2$.

Thus, Gaussian-type tail control does not require a Gaussian
distribution.

### Consequence for the Orlicz norm

The earlier tail-to-Orlicz lemma states that
$$
P(|Y|>x)\le A e^{-Bx^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\|Y\|_{\psi_2}\le\sqrt{\frac{1+A}{B}}.
$$

Here,
$$
A=2,
\qquad
B=\frac{1}{2\|a\|_2^2}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\varepsilon_i\right\|_{\psi_2}
\le\sqrt6\,\|a\|_2.
}
$$

This is an upper bound, rather than an exact norm calculation.

### Example: the average of random signs

Take $a_i=1/n$. Then
$$
S=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
\|a\|_2^2=\frac1n.
$$

Hoeffding’s inequality becomes
$$
P(|S|>x)\le2e^{-nx^2/2}.
$$

The typical fluctuation scale is therefore $1/\sqrt n$.

### Check your understanding

Does a Gaussian-shaped tail bound imply that the random variable
is normally distributed?

No. A Rademacher sum is discrete, but Hoeffding’s inequality still
gives it Gaussian-type tail control.

## 2. Proving Hoeffding’s inequality — Chernoff’s method

Let
$$
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2>0.
$$

We first bound the upper tail $P(S>x)$ for $x>0$.

### Step 1: Bound the exponential moment of one random sign

For a Rademacher variable $\varepsilon$ and any real $u$,
$$
Ee^{u\varepsilon}
=
\frac{e^u+e^{-u}}2
=
\cosh(u).
$$

Using the power series,
$$
\cosh(u)
=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2k}}{(2k)!}
\le
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{2k}}{2^k k!}
=
e^{u^2/2}.
$$

Here $(2k)!\ge 2^k k!$, so the inequality holds term by term.

Taking $u=\lambda a_i$ gives
$$
Ee^{\lambda a_i\varepsilon_i}
\le e^{\lambda^2a_i^2/2}.
$$

### Step 2: Use independence to control the sum

Because the $\varepsilon_i$ are independent,
$$
\begin{aligned}
Ee^{\lambda S}
&=
E\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda a_i\varepsilon_i}\\
&=
\prod_{i=1}^{n}Ee^{\lambda a_i\varepsilon_i}\\
&\le
\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{\lambda^2a_i^2/2}\\
&=
e^{\lambda^2v/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Independence is what lets us factor the expectation.

### Step 3: Apply Markov’s inequality to the exponential

For every $\lambda>0$, the exponential is increasing, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
P(S>x)
&=P(e^{\lambda S}>e^{\lambda x})\\
&\le e^{-\lambda x}Ee^{\lambda S}\\
&\le
\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^2v}{2}-\lambda x\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

We now have an entire family of valid bounds, indexed by $\lambda$.

### Step 4: Choose the best parameter

Since the exponential is increasing, minimize its exponent:
$$
g(\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^2v}{2}-\lambda x.
$$

Differentiate:
$$
g'(\lambda)=v\lambda-x,
\qquad
g''(\lambda)=v>0.
$$

Thus the unique minimum occurs at
$$
\lambda_\star =\frac{x}{v}>0.
$$

Substitution gives
$$
g(\lambda_\star )
=
\frac{x^2}{2v}-\frac{x^2}{v}
=
-\frac{x^2}{2v}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
P(S>x)\le e^{-x^2/(2v)}.
$$

### Step 5: Bound the lower tail and combine

Apply the same argument to
$$
-S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-a_i)\varepsilon_i.
$$

Its coefficient vector has the same squared norm $v$, giving
$$
P(S<-x)\le e^{-x^2/(2v)}.
$$

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
P(|S|>x)
\le
2e^{-x^2/(2v)}
=
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\|a\|_2^2}\right).
}
$$

### The professor's methodological point

Rather than trying to guess one sharp probability bound:

1. Introduce a positive tuning parameter $\lambda$.
2. Obtain a bound valid for every $\lambda>0$.
3. Optimize over $\lambda$.

Exponentiation is especially useful for sums because it turns
a sum into a product. Independence then makes the exponential
moment manageable.

### Check your understanding

Why must $\lambda>0$ when bounding $P(S>x)$ this way?

Because multiplication by a positive $\lambda$ preserves the
inequality:
$$
S>x
\iff
e^{\lambda S}>e^{\lambda x}.
$$
For negative $\lambda$, the inequality reverses.

## 3. Sub-Gaussian processes

### Definition

A stochastic process $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ is sub-Gaussian with respect
to a semimetric $d$ if
$$
\boxed{
P(|X_s-X_t|>x)
\le
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2d(s,t)^2}\right)
}
$$
for every $s,t\in T$ and $x>0$.

When $d(s,t)=0$, the condition is interpreted as requiring
$X_s=X_t$ almost surely.

The semimetric determines the scale of the increment:
smaller $d(s,t)$ means stronger concentration of $X_s-X_t$
around zero.

This definition concerns increments. It does not by itself
control the random level of the entire process.

### Example 1: A centered Gaussian process

Let $\{G_t:t\in T\}$ be a centered Gaussian process. Define
$$
d(s,t)
=
\sqrt{E(G_s-G_t)^2}
=
\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(G_s-G_t)}.
$$

Because each increment is Gaussian,
$$
G_s-G_t\sim N(0,d(s,t)^2).
$$

The Gaussian tail bound gives
$$
P(|G_s-G_t|>x)
\le
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2d(s,t)^2}\right).
$$

Thus a centered Gaussian process is sub-Gaussian for its
standard-deviation semimetric.

The centering qualification matters: for a Gaussian process
with varying means, standard deviation alone does not control
the uncentered increments.

### What does this semimetric measure?

Writing the covariance function as
$$
K(s,t)=\operatorname{Cov}(G_s,G_t),
$$
we obtain
$$
d(s,t)^2
=
K(s,s)+K(t,t)-2K(s,t).
$$

So closeness is determined by how similarly the process behaves
at the two indices. It need not equal physical distance.

For example, standard Brownian motion satisfies
$$
E(B_s-B_t)^2=|s-t|,
$$
so its natural semimetric is
$$
d(s,t)=\sqrt{|s-t|}.
$$

### Example 2: A Rademacher process

Fix independent Rademacher variables
$\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$.

For each coefficient vector $a\in T\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, define
$$
X_a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\varepsilon_i.
$$

The same random signs are used for every index $a$.

For two coefficient vectors,
$$
X_a-X_b
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_i-b_i)\varepsilon_i.
$$

Hoeffding's inequality therefore gives
$$
P(|X_a-X_b|>x)
\le
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\|a-b\|_2^2}\right).
$$

Hence the Rademacher process is sub-Gaussian for
$$
d(a,b)=\|a-b\|_2.
$$

Here the index is a coefficient vector, rather than time.

### Connection to the chaining theorem

The tail-to-Orlicz lemma yields
$$
\boxed{
\|X_s-X_t\|_{\psi_2}
\le\sqrt6\,d(s,t),
\qquad
\psi_2(u)=e^{u^2}-1.
}
$$

This is exactly the increment condition required by the general
chaining theorem, with $\psi=\psi_2$ and $C=\sqrt6$.

Therefore, sub-Gaussian increment tails let us apply chaining
using packing numbers computed in the corresponding semimetric.

### Check your understanding

Why must the Rademacher process use the same random signs
at every index?

Because then subtracting two process values gives
$$
X_a-X_b=\sum_i(a_i-b_i)\varepsilon_i,
$$
whose tail scale is $\|a-b\|_2$.

Using separate independent signs at different indices would
generally destroy this increment structure.

## 4. The sub-Gaussian entropy bound — Corollary 2.2.8

Let $\{X_t:t\in T\}$ be a separable sub-Gaussian process with
respect to $d$.

Then, for every $\delta>0$,
$$
\boxed{
E\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\le
K\int_0^\delta
\sqrt{\log D(\varepsilon,d)}\,d\varepsilon,
}
$$
where $K$ is a universal constant.

“Universal” means that the constant does not depend on the
particular process or index set under the stated normalization.

### Step 1: Use the sub-Gaussian Orlicz function

Recall
$$
\psi_2(u)=e^{u^2}-1,
\qquad
\psi_2^{-1}(m)=\sqrt{\log(1+m)}.
$$

Sub-Gaussian increments satisfy
$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_{\psi_2}\le\sqrt6\,d(s,t).
$$

Apply Theorem 2.2.4 with $\eta=\delta$. Writing
$$
W_\delta=\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|,
$$
we get
$$
\|W_\delta\|_{\psi_2}
\le K_0\left[
\int_0^\delta
\sqrt{\log(1+D(\varepsilon,d))}\,d\varepsilon
+
\delta\sqrt{\log(1+D(\delta,d)^2)}
\right].
$$

### Step 2: Absorb the second term into the integral

For $m\ge1$,
$$
1+m^2\le(1+m)^2,
$$
so
$$
\sqrt{\log(1+m^2)}
\le\sqrt2\,\sqrt{\log(1+m)}.
$$

Also, $D(\varepsilon,d)\ge D(\delta,d)$ when
$0<\varepsilon\le\delta$. Therefore,
$$
\delta\sqrt{\log(1+D(\delta,d))}
\le
\int_0^\delta
\sqrt{\log(1+D(\varepsilon,d))}\,d\varepsilon.
$$

The second term is thus bounded by a constant times the first.
Increasing the overall constant gives
$$
\|W_\delta\|_{\psi_2}
\le
K_1\int_0^\delta
\sqrt{\log(1+D(\varepsilon,d))}\,d\varepsilon.
$$

### Step 3: Remove the extra 1 inside the logarithm

Let $r=\operatorname{diam}(T)$.

For $\varepsilon<r$, some two points have distance greater than
$\varepsilon$, so
$$
D(\varepsilon,d)\ge2.
$$

For $m\ge2$,
$$
\log(1+m)\le2\log m.
$$

Thus, when $\delta\le r$, the extra 1 can be removed at the cost
of another constant factor.

If $\delta>r$, use the global increment corollary with upper limit
$r$. For $\varepsilon\ge r$, $D(\varepsilon,d)=1$, so
$\log D(\varepsilon,d)=0$.

This gives the same stated integral bound for every $\delta>0$.

### Step 4: Pass from Orlicz norm to expectation

Because $\psi_2(u)\ge u^2$,
$$
EW_\delta
\le\|W_\delta\|_2
\le\|W_\delta\|_{\psi_2}.
$$

Combining these inequalities proves the corollary.

### Bounding the whole process

For any reference index $t_0$,
$$
|X_t|\le|X_{t_0}|+|X_t-X_{t_0}|.
$$

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{
E\sup_{t\in T}|X_t|
\le
E|X_{t_0}|
+
K\int_0^\infty
\sqrt{\log D(\varepsilon,d)}\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

For a finite-diameter index set, the integrand vanishes beyond
the diameter.

### Interpretation

The packing entropy is
$$
H(\varepsilon)=\log D(\varepsilon,d).
$$

For sub-Gaussian increments, the cost of controlling many links
grows like the square root of their logarithmic count. Chaining
accumulates that cost across scales.

### Check your understanding

Where did the theorem's second term go?

It was bounded by a constant times the entropy integral and
absorbed into that integral by increasing $K$.

## 5. Bernstein’s inequality — Two deviation regimes

Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ be independent random variables satisfying
$$
EY_i=0,
\qquad
|Y_i|\le M \quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

Define
$$
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i,
$$
and choose
$$
v\ge\operatorname{Var}(S)
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(Y_i).
$$

The variables need not be identically distributed.

### Bernstein’s inequality — Lemma 2.2.9

For $x>0$,
$$
\boxed{
P(|S|>x)
\le
2\exp\left(
-\frac{x^2}{2(v+Mx/3)}
\right).
}
$$

The denominator contains two quantities:
- $v$, describing the variance scale of the sum.
- $Mx/3$, incorporating the bound on individual summands.

### Regime 1: Quadratic, Gaussian-type behavior

When
$$
\frac{Mx}{3}\ll v,
$$
the variance term dominates, and the upper bound behaves like
$$
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2v}\right).
$$

The exponent is approximately quadratic in $x$.

This is a statement about the concentration bound. It does not
require $S$ to have a normal distribution.

### Regime 2: Linear, exponential-type behavior

When
$$
\frac{Mx}{3}\gg v,
$$
the range term dominates, and the upper bound behaves like
$$
2\exp\left(-\frac{3x}{2M}\right).
$$

The exponent is approximately linear in $x$.

The transition occurs around
$$
x\approx\frac{3v}{M}.
$$

These regimes describe the bound, not necessarily the exact tails.
In particular, $S$ is bounded by $nM$, so its actual tail probability
is zero beyond that point.

### A useful bound displaying both regimes

Since
$$
v+\frac{Mx}{3}
\le
2\max\left\{v,\frac{Mx}{3}\right\},
$$
Bernstein’s inequality implies
$$
P(|S|>x)
\le
2\exp\left[
-\min\left\{
\frac{x^2}{4v},
\frac{3x}{4M}
\right\}
\right].
$$

This makes the quadratic and linear scales explicit.

### Application to a sample average

Suppose the $Y_i$ are identically distributed with variance
$\sigma^2$. For
$$
\overline Y_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i,
$$
take $v=n\sigma^2$ and $x=nt$. Then
$$
\boxed{
P(|\overline Y_n|>t)
\le
2\exp\left(
-\frac{nt^2}{2(\sigma^2+Mt/3)}
\right).
}
$$

For an average of observations $Z_i$, apply this to
$Y_i=Z_i-EZ_i$ and bound the centered variables.

### Why Bernstein is useful

Bounded variables also admit Hoeffding-type concentration bounds.
Bernstein’s distinctive advantage is that it uses their actual
variance.

If the variance is much smaller than the squared range bound,
this can give substantially stronger control of moderate deviations.

The professor introduced Bernstein as the next tool for handling
bounds with both Gaussian-type and exponential-type behavior.

### Check your understanding

Which term controls moderate deviations, and which controls
sufficiently large deviations?

The variance term $v$ controls the quadratic regime.
The term $Mx/3$ controls the linear regime.

## 6. An exponential-moment bound for a bounded variable

Let $Y$ satisfy
$$
EY=0,
\qquad
|Y|\le M \quad\text{almost surely},
\qquad
EY^2=\sigma^2,
$$
where $M>0$.

For every $\lambda>0$, we claim
$$
\boxed{
Ee^{\lambda Y}
\le
\exp\left[
\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}
\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M\right)
\right].
}
$$

This plays the same role in the Bernstein proof as
$$
Ee^{u\varepsilon}\le e^{u^2/2}
$$
played in the Hoeffding proof.

### Step 1: Expand the exponential

Because $Y$ is bounded, the exponential series converges uniformly
for $Y\in[-M,M]$, allowing expectation to pass through the sum:
$$
Ee^{\lambda Y}
=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\lambda^kEY^k}{k!}.
$$

The first terms are
$$
Ee^{\lambda Y}
=
1+\lambda EY+\frac{\lambda^2EY^2}{2}
+
\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^kEY^k}{k!}.
$$

Since $EY=0$,
$$
Ee^{\lambda Y}
=
1+
\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^kEY^k}{k!}.
$$

### Step 2: Control all higher moments by the second moment

For every integer $k\ge2$,
$$
|Y|^k
=
Y^2|Y|^{k-2}
\le
Y^2M^{k-2}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
EY^k
\le E|Y|^k
\le M^{k-2}EY^2
=
M^{k-2}\sigma^2.
$$

The first inequality is useful for odd powers, whose expectations
may be negative.

Since $\lambda>0$, all series coefficients are nonnegative.
Substituting these upper bounds gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
Ee^{\lambda Y}
&\le
1+\sigma^2
\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}
\frac{\lambda^kM^{k-2}}{k!}\\
&=
1+\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}
\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}
\frac{(\lambda M)^k}{k!}\\
&=
1+\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}
\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 3: Put the bound into exponential form

Using $1+u\le e^u$,
$$
Ee^{\lambda Y}
\le
\exp\left[
\frac{\sigma^2}{M^2}
\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M\right)
\right].
$$

This proves the lemma.

### Why this form is useful

For independent variables, exponential moments multiply.
An upper bound written as an exponential lets us combine those
products by adding their exponents.

For small $\lambda M$,
$$
e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M
=
\frac{\lambda^2M^2}{2}
+
\frac{\lambda^3M^3}{6}
+\cdots.
$$

Thus the exponent begins with
$$
\frac{\sigma^2\lambda^2}{2},
$$
the same quadratic term as a centered Gaussian exponential moment.

The remaining terms account for higher-order contributions using
the magnitude bound $M$.

### Check your understanding

Why does the assumption $EY=0$ matter?

It removes the linear term $\lambda EY$, leaving an exponential
moment bound whose leading contribution is determined by the
variance.

## 7. Optimizing the exponential bound — Bennett’s inequality

Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ be independent, with
$$
EY_i=0,
\qquad
|Y_i|\le M.
$$

Write
$$
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_i,
\qquad
v\ge\sum_{i=1}^{n}EY_i^2.
$$

Assume $M>0$ and $v>0$. If the actual variance is zero, all the
centered summands are zero almost surely.

### Step 1: Combine the exponential-moment bounds

By independence and the preceding lemma,
$$
\begin{aligned}
Ee^{\lambda S}
&=\prod_{i=1}^{n}Ee^{\lambda Y_i}\\
&\le
\exp\left[
\frac{\sum_iEY_i^2}{M^2}
\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M\right)
\right]\\
&\le
\exp\left[
\frac{v}{M^2}
\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M\right)
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 2: Apply exponential Markov

For every $\lambda>0$,
$$
P(S>x)
\le e^{-\lambda x}Ee^{\lambda S}
\le e^{g(\lambda)},
$$
where
$$
g(\lambda)
=
-\lambda x
+
\frac{v}{M^2}
\left(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M\right).
$$

As in the Hoeffding proof, we minimize the exponent.

### Step 3: Find the minimizing parameter

Differentiate:
$$
g'(\lambda)
=
-x+\frac{v}{M}\left(e^{\lambda M}-1\right).
$$

Setting $g'(\lambda)=0$ gives
$$
e^{\lambda M}=1+\frac{Mx}{v}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
\lambda_\star =
\frac1M\log\left(1+\frac{Mx}{v}\right).
}
$$

Also,
$$
g''(\lambda)=ve^{\lambda M}>0,
$$
so this is a minimum.

### Step 4: Evaluate the optimized exponent

Introduce
$$
u=\frac{Mx}{v}.
$$

Then
$$
\lambda_\star M=\log(1+u),
\qquad
e^{\lambda_\star M}=1+u.
$$

Substitution yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
g(\lambda_\star )
&=
-\frac{x}{M}\log(1+u)
+
\frac{v}{M^2}\left[u-\log(1+u)\right]\\
&=
-\frac{v}{M^2}
\left[(1+u)\log(1+u)-u\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Define the Bennett function
$$
h(u)=(1+u)\log(1+u)-u,
\qquad u\ge0.
$$

The upper-tail bound becomes
$$
P(S>x)
\le
\exp\left[
-\frac{v}{M^2}h\left(\frac{Mx}{v}\right)
\right].
$$

### Step 5: Include the lower tail

The variables $-Y_i$ satisfy the same assumptions. Applying the
same argument and combining the two tails gives
$$
\boxed{
P(|S|>x)
\le
2\exp\left[
-\frac{v}{M^2}h\left(\frac{Mx}{v}\right)
\right].
}
$$

This is Bennett’s inequality.

### How this connects to Gaussian behavior

Near zero,
$$
h(u)=\frac{u^2}{2}-\frac{u^3}{6}+O(u^4).
$$

Thus, for small $Mx/v$, the leading term in the exponent is
$$
-\frac{v}{M^2}\frac12\left(\frac{Mx}{v}\right)^2
=
-\frac{x^2}{2v}.
$$

The optimized bound therefore recovers Gaussian-type behavior
for moderate deviations.

### Check your understanding

Why is $\lambda_\star $ different from the Hoeffding optimizer $x/v$?

The exponential-moment bound now has an $e^{\lambda M}$ term,
rather than only a quadratic term in $\lambda$. Its derivative
therefore leads to a logarithmic optimizer.


## 8. From Bennett’s bound to Bernstein’s inequality

Recall Bennett’s inequality:
$$
P(|S|>x)
\le
2\exp\left[
-\frac{v}{M^2}h\left(\frac{Mx}{v}\right)
\right],
$$
where
$$
h(u)=(1+u)\log(1+u)-u.
$$

We will prove that, for every $u\ge0$,
$$
\boxed{
h(u)\ge\frac{u^2}{2(1+u/3)}.
}
$$

### Step 1: Define the difference

Let
$$
F(u)
=
h(u)-\frac{u^2}{2(1+u/3)}
=
h(u)-\frac{3u^2}{2(u+3)}.
$$

We want to show $F(u)\ge0$.

At zero,
$$
F(0)=0.
$$

Since
$$
h'(u)=\log(1+u),
$$
and the derivative of the rational term is zero at $u=0$,
we also have
$$
F'(0)=0.
$$

### Step 2: Show the second derivative is nonnegative

For the Bennett function,
$$
h''(u)=\frac1{1+u}.
$$

To differentiate the rational term, write
$$
\frac{3u^2}{2(u+3)}
=
\frac32\left(u-3+\frac9{u+3}\right).
$$

Its second derivative is
$$
\frac{27}{(u+3)^3}.
$$

Therefore,
$$
\begin{aligned}
F''(u)
&=
\frac1{1+u}-\frac{27}{(u+3)^3}\\
&=
\frac{(u+3)^3-27(1+u)}
{(1+u)(u+3)^3}\\
&=
\frac{u^2(u+9)}
{(1+u)(u+3)^3}\\
&\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $F'$ is nondecreasing. Since $F'(0)=0$,
$$
F'(u)\ge0
\qquad\text{for }u\ge0.
$$

It follows that $F$ is nondecreasing. Since $F(0)=0$,
$$
F(u)\ge0.
$$

This proves the desired lower bound.

### Step 3: Substitute into Bennett’s inequality

Take
$$
u=\frac{Mx}{v}.
$$

Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{v}{M^2}h\left(\frac{Mx}{v}\right)
&\ge
\frac{v}{M^2}
\frac{(Mx/v)^2}{2(1+Mx/(3v))}\\
&=
\frac{x^2}{2(v+Mx/3)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Negating reverses the inequality, so
$$
\exp\left[
-\frac{v}{M^2}h\left(\frac{Mx}{v}\right)
\right]
\le
\exp\left[
-\frac{x^2}{2(v+Mx/3)}
\right].
$$

Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
P(|S|>x)
\le
2\exp\left[
-\frac{x^2}{2(v+Mx/3)}
\right].
}
$$

This is Bernstein’s inequality.

### What the simplification accomplishes

Bennett’s bound is at least as sharp as this Bernstein bound.

Bernstein replaces the logarithmic function $h$ with a rational
expression that makes the variance and range contributions easy
to see and use.

The professor emphasized the derivative argument as a useful
general technique: establish an inequality by checking the
difference and its derivative at zero, then controlling the sign
of its second derivative.

### Check your understanding

Why do we need a lower bound on $h$, rather than an upper bound?

Because $h$ appears with a negative sign in the exponent.
A lower bound on $h$ produces an upper bound on $e^{-ch}$.

## 9. Lecture summary — Concentration and exponential moments

### The common proof strategy

Both concentration proofs use Chernoff’s method:

1. Bound the exponential moment of one summand.
2. Use independence to multiply those bounds.
3. Apply Markov’s inequality:
   $$
   P(S>x)\le e^{-\lambda x}Ee^{\lambda S}.
   $$
4. Optimize over $\lambda>0$.
5. Repeat for $-S$ and combine the two tails.

The exponential-moment bound determines the resulting
concentration inequality.

### Hoeffding: Randomly signed sums

For independent Rademacher variables $\varepsilon_i$,
$$
S=\sum_i a_i\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
v=\sum_i a_i^2.
$$

The basic exponential-moment bound is
$$
Ee^{\lambda S}\le e^{\lambda^2v/2}.
$$

Optimizing at $\lambda_\star =x/v$ gives
$$
P(|S|>x)\le2e^{-x^2/(2v)}.
$$

This is Gaussian-type tail control, even though $S$ is discrete.

### Sub-Gaussian processes and chaining

A sub-Gaussian process satisfies
$$
P(|X_s-X_t|>x)
\le
2e^{-x^2/(2d(s,t)^2)}.
$$

This implies
$$
\|X_s-X_t\|_{\psi_2}\le\sqrt6\,d(s,t).
$$

The chaining theorem then gives
$$
E\sup_{d(s,t)\le\delta}|X_s-X_t|
\le
K\int_0^\delta
\sqrt{\log D(\varepsilon,d)}\,d\varepsilon.
$$

Thus, increment concentration and the geometry of the index set
combine to control the process uniformly.

### Bernstein: Bounded centered sums

For independent variables with $EY_i=0$ and $|Y_i|\le M$,
take
$$
v\ge\sum_i EY_i^2.
$$

The exponential-moment bound is
$$
Ee^{\lambda S}
\le
\exp\left[
\frac{v}{M^2}
(e^{\lambda M}-1-\lambda M)
\right].
$$

Optimizing produces Bennett’s inequality. Using
$$
(1+u)\log(1+u)-u
\ge
\frac{u^2}{2(1+u/3)}
$$
then gives Bernstein’s inequality:
$$
P(|S|>x)
\le
2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2(v+Mx/3)}\right).
$$

The variance term controls the quadratic regime; the range term
controls the linear regime of this upper bound.

### Techniques to remember

- Introduce a tuning parameter and optimize a family of bounds.
- Exponentiation turns sums into products.
- Independence factors exponential moments.
- Boundedness controls higher moments through the second moment.
- A derivative argument can simplify an optimized bound.

### Final check

Which parts of these proofs require independence?

The factorization
$$
Ee^{\lambda\sum_iY_i}
=
\prod_i Ee^{\lambda Y_i},
$$
and the identity
$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_iY_i\right)
=
\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(Y_i).
$$

Markov’s inequality itself does not require independence.
