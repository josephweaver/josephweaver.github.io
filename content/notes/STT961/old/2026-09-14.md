# September 14 — Empirical Processes

The September 14 lecture extends the familiar law of large numbers and central limit theorem from one function to an entire function class.
The empirical measure averages functions over the sample,

$$
P_nf=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i),
$$

while the empirical process describes their scaled estimation errors,

$$
\mathbb G_nf=\sqrt n(P_nf-Pf).
$$

A Glivenko–Cantelli class has uniformly vanishing estimation errors. A Donsker class has scaled errors that converge jointly, as an entire process, to a Gaussian $P$-Brownian bridge. Donsker implies Glivenko–Cantelli.
The central challenge is moving from finite collections of functions to the whole class. Total boundedness provides finite approximations; asymptotic equicontinuity controls the gaps between them. The coming lectures develop tools for verifying those conditions.

## 1. The Empirical Measure and Its Action on Functions

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be random elements taking values in a
measurable space $(\mathcal X,\mathcal A)$.

The observations need not be numbers. They can be vectors,
functions, images, or other objects.

### The empirical measure

For a measurable set $C\in\mathcal A$, define

$$
P_n(C)
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf 1_{\{X_i\in C\}}.
$$

Thus $P_n(C)$ is the proportion of observations belonging to $C$.

Equivalently,

$$
P_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{X_i},
$$

where $\delta_x$ is the Dirac probability measure at $x$:

$$
\delta_x(C)=
\begin{cases}
1,&x\in C,\\
0,&x\notin C.
\end{cases}
$$

Here $\delta_x$ is a measure, rather than an ordinary
real-valued “delta function.”

Each observation contributes probability mass $1/n$.
Repeated observations contribute their masses at the same location.

### Example

For observations

$$
X_1=2,\qquad X_2=2,\qquad X_3=5,\qquad X_4=9,
$$

the empirical measure is

$$
P_4=\frac12\delta_2+\frac14\delta_5+\frac14\delta_9.
$$

For $C=(-\infty,5]$,

$$
P_4(C)=\frac34.
$$

Three of the four observations belong to $C$.

### Why is it a random measure?

Before observing the sample, the locations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$
are random. Therefore, for each fixed measurable set $C$,
the value $P_n(C)$ is random.

After observing the sample, we obtain one realized probability
measure.

The definition itself does not require independence or identical
distributions. Those assumptions enter the convergence results.

### A measure acts on functions by integration

Let $\mathcal F$ be a collection of measurable functions

$$
f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R.
$$

The empirical measure induces the map

$$
f\longmapsto P_nf,
$$

where

$$
P_nf
:=
\int f\,dP_n
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i).
$$

Thus integrating against the empirical measure simply averages
the function's values over the sample.

For any measure $Q$, the notation

$$
Qf:=\int f\,dQ
$$

is shorthand for integration, whenever the integral is defined.
It does not mean ordinary multiplication of $Q$ and $f$.

### Population versus sample

If the observations have common distribution $P$, then

$$
Pf=\int f\,dP=E[f(X_i)]
$$

is the population expectation, while

$$
P_nf=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i)
$$

is its sample counterpart.

Two familiar choices illustrate the notation:

- If $f(x)=x$, then $P_nf$ is the sample mean.
- If $f(x)=\mathbf 1_C(x)$, then $P_nf=P_n(C)$ is a sample proportion.

### The change in viewpoint

For a fixed sample, we can regard

$$
f\mapsto P_nf
$$

as a function whose input is another function.

Later, the index set of our stochastic process will be
$\mathcal F$. Instead of indexing coordinates by time $t$,
we will index them by test functions $f$.

### Professor's comment: a foundation for later differentiation

Viewing a measure through the map $f\mapsto Pf$ is useful beyond
notation. Later, we will study statistical functionals that take
a measure as input and ask about their continuity and differentiability.

This viewpoint prepares us to study how a statistic changes
when its underlying distribution changes.

## 2. The Empirical Process: Centering and Scaling

Assume $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are i.i.d. with common distribution $P$.

Recall that

$$
P_nf=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i),
\qquad
Pf=E[f(X_i)].
$$

Their difference is the estimation error for the expectation of $f$.

### Definition of the empirical process

For each $f\in\mathcal F$ with finite $Pf$, define

$$
\mathbb G_nf
=
\sqrt n(P_n-P)f
=
\sqrt n(P_nf-Pf).
$$

Expanding the sample average gives

$$
\mathbb G_nf
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}
\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(f(X_i)-Pf\bigr).
$$

The map

$$
\mathbb G_n:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R,
\qquad
f\mapsto\mathbb G_nf,
$$

is the empirical process indexed by $\mathcal F$.

### What does centering do?

Subtracting $Pf$ makes each summand have mean zero:

$$
E[f(X_i)-Pf]=0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
E[\mathbb G_nf]=0.
$$

The process describes fluctuations around the population expectation.

### Why multiply by $\sqrt n$?

If $f(X_i)$ has finite variance $\sigma_f^2$, independence gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(P_nf)=\frac{\sigma_f^2}{n}.
$$

Thus the standard deviation of the sample average is
$\sigma_f/\sqrt n$.

Multiplying the estimation error by $\sqrt n$ keeps its variance
at a constant scale:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb G_nf)=\sigma_f^2.
$$

Without scaling, the error tends to zero. With scaling, we can
study its nontrivial limiting distribution when $\sigma_f^2>0$.

### For one fixed function: the law of large numbers

If

$$
P|f|=E|f(X_1)|<\infty,
$$

the strong law of large numbers gives

$$
P_nf\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}Pf.
$$

The absolute-integrability condition makes “$Pf$ exists”
precise for this result.

### For one fixed function: the central limit theorem

If

$$
Pf^2=E[f(X_1)^2]<\infty,
$$

then

$$
\mathbb G_nf
\rightsquigarrow
N\left(0,P[(f-Pf)^2]\right).
$$

The limiting variance is

$$
P[(f-Pf)^2]
=
Pf^2-(Pf)^2
=
\operatorname{Var}(f(X_1)).
$$

If this variance is zero, the limiting distribution is
concentrated at zero.

### Example: a sample proportion

Take $f=\mathbf 1_C$ and write $p=P(C)$.

Then

$$
P_nf=P_n(C),
\qquad
Pf=p,
$$

so

$$
\mathbb G_nf
=
\sqrt n\bigl(P_n(C)-p\bigr).
$$

Since an indicator has variance $p(1-p)$,

$$
\sqrt n\bigl(P_n(C)-p\bigr)
\rightsquigarrow N(0,p(1-p)).
$$

This is the familiar central limit theorem for a sample proportion.

### The next question

These results concern one fixed $f$ at a time.

Empirical process theory asks when we can control all
$f\in\mathcal F$ together, uniformly over the class.

That requires conditions on the class itself, beyond moment
conditions for each individual function.

## 3. Glivenko–Cantelli Classes: A Uniform Law of Large Numbers

For each fixed integrable function $f$, the law of large numbers gives

$$
P_nf\to Pf.
$$

We now ask whether the largest estimation error over an entire
class $\mathcal F$ also tends to zero.

### Measuring error over a function class

For a signed measure $Q$, define

$$
\|Q\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|Qf|
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\left|\int f\,dQ\right|,
$$

whenever the integrals are defined.

In particular,

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|P_nf-Pf|.
$$

This is the worst absolute discrepancy between sample and
population expectations over $\mathcal F$.

The notation acts like a supremum norm on the map $f\mapsto Qf$.
It need not be a genuine norm on measures: the chosen class
might fail to distinguish two different measures.

### Definition

A class $\mathcal F$ is **$P$-Glivenko–Cantelli in probability** if

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

It is **$P$-Glivenko–Cantelli outer almost surely** if

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}^\star }0.
$$

The lecture distinguishes these as probability-GC and
outer-almost-sure-GC classes.

The prefix $P$ records the distribution generating the
i.i.d. observations.

Outer convergence handles the possibility that the supremum
over $\mathcal F$ is not measurable.

### Pointwise versus uniform convergence

Pointwise convergence says:

$$
\text{For every fixed }f\in\mathcal F,\qquad
|P_nf-Pf|\to 0.
$$

Uniform convergence says:

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|P_nf-Pf|\to 0.
$$

Uniform convergence requires all functions to have small errors
simultaneously. The function with the largest error can change
with the sample and with $n$.

Even if every individual function satisfies a law of large
numbers, uniform convergence over an infinite class does not
automatically follow.

### Why finite classes are easier

Suppose

$$
\mathcal F=\{f_1,\ldots,f_m\}
$$

and each $f_j$ is integrable.

The strong law holds simultaneously for these finitely many
functions on one event of probability one. Therefore,

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\max_{1\leq j\leq m}|P_nf_j-Pf_j|
\to 0
$$

on that event.

For an infinite class, controlling the supremum requires
additional structure.

### Why uniformity matters for data-dependent choices

Suppose the data are used to select a function
$\widehat f_n\in\mathcal F$.

Whenever this selection is well defined,

$$
|P_n\widehat f_n-P\widehat f_n|
\leq
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|P_nf-Pf|.
$$

Thus a uniform law of large numbers controls the estimation
error even for a function chosen after looking at the data.

A fixed-function law of large numbers does not directly give
this guarantee because $\widehat f_n$ changes with the sample.

For a class of loss functions, this is a key step toward
consistency of empirical risk minimization.

### Example: distribution functions

For real-valued observations, take

$$
\mathcal F=
\{f_t:f_t(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x\leq t\}},\ t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

Then

$$
P_nf_t=F_n(t),
\qquad
Pf_t=F(t),
$$

where $F_n$ and $F$ are the empirical and population CDFs.

Consequently,

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{t\in\mathbb R}|F_n(t)-F(t)|.
$$

The classical Glivenko–Cantelli theorem says this supremum
converges to zero almost surely.

## 4. Donsker Classes: A Functional Central Limit Theorem

A Glivenko–Cantelli class gives a uniform law of large numbers:

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|P_nf-Pf|\to 0.
$$

A Donsker class describes the limiting distribution of these
errors after multiplying by $\sqrt n$.

Recall the empirical process

$$
\mathbb G_nf=\sqrt n(P_nf-Pf).
$$

### Viewing the empirical process as a random function

For each realized sample, the map

$$
f\mapsto\mathbb G_nf
$$

is a real-valued function on $\mathcal F$.

To regard it as an element of $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$,
this function must be bounded:

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|\mathbb G_nf|<\infty.
$$

The textbook uses the sufficient condition

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|f(x)-Pf|<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathcal X.
$$

Indeed,

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|\mathbb G_nf|
\leq
\frac1{\sqrt n}
\sum_{i=1}^n
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|f(X_i)-Pf|,
$$

which is finite under this condition.

Boundedness of each realized path does not automatically
give Borel measurability of the map into $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

### Definition of a Donsker class

The class $\mathcal F$ is called **$P$-Donsker** if

$$
\mathbb G_n\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
$$

where $\mathbb G$ is a tight, Borel measurable random element
of $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

In this i.i.d. setting, the limit is a centered Gaussian process
with covariance

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb Gf,\mathbb Gg)
=
P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
$$

We will derive this covariance in the next section.

### What does “functional CLT” mean?

For one fixed $f$, the ordinary CLT describes one real number:

$$
\mathbb G_nf
\rightsquigarrow
N\left(0,P[(f-Pf)^2]\right).
$$

The functional CLT describes the entire random function

$$
f\mapsto\mathbb G_nf.
$$

It captures the joint fluctuations across the class and controls
them in the supremum-norm topology.

Separate scalar CLTs, or even convergence of every finite
collection of coordinates, do not by themselves establish
this functional convergence.

### Connection to Chapter 1

Previously, we studied random functions indexed by $t\in T$:

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow X
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(T).
$$

Now the index set is a class of functions:

$$
T=\mathcal F.
$$

The Chapter 1 framework still applies. We need finite-dimensional
convergence together with control of the process over the
entire index set.

### Using a Donsker limit

If $\Phi:\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)\to\mathbb R$ is continuous,
then the continuous mapping theorem gives

$$
\Phi(\mathbb G_n)\rightsquigarrow\Phi(\mathbb G).
$$

For example, the supremum norm is continuous, so

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F}
\rightsquigarrow
\|\mathbb G\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\sqrt n\,
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|P_nf-Pf|
\rightsquigarrow
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|\mathbb Gf|.
$$

This turns convergence of the empirical process into a limiting
distribution for its largest absolute estimation error.

## 5. The Gaussian Limit and Its Covariance

Suppose $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are i.i.d. with distribution $P$.

For square-integrable functions $f_1,\ldots,f_k\in\mathcal F$,
consider the finite vector

$$
(\mathbb G_nf_1,\ldots,\mathbb G_nf_k).
$$

### Apply the multivariate CLT

Define the centered random vectors

$$
V_i=
\begin{pmatrix}
f_1(X_i)-Pf_1\\
\vdots\\
f_k(X_i)-Pf_k
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

These vectors are i.i.d., have mean zero, and have finite
second moments. Furthermore,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\mathbb G_nf_1\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb G_nf_k
\end{pmatrix}
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n V_i.
$$

Therefore, the multivariate CLT gives

$$
(\mathbb G_nf_1,\ldots,\mathbb G_nf_k)
\rightsquigarrow N_k(0,\Sigma),
$$

where

$$
\Sigma_{jl}
=
P[(f_j-Pf_j)(f_l-Pf_l)].
$$

### Expand the covariance

For two functions $f,g\in\mathcal F$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P[(f-Pf)(g-Pg)]
&=P(fg)-(Pg)(Pf)-(Pf)(Pg)+(Pf)(Pg)\\
&=P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the limiting covariance is

$$
K(f,g)
=
P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg)
=
\operatorname{Cov}(f(X_1),g(X_1)).
$$

Both functions are evaluated on the same observation $X_1$.
Their dependence determines the covariance.

### This covariance already holds at finite n

Independence across observations gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb G_nf,\mathbb G_ng)
&=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\operatorname{Cov}(f(X_i),g(X_i))\\
&=P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
\end{aligned}
$$

Terms involving different observations vanish by independence.

The empirical process already has the limiting mean and
covariance. The CLT establishes convergence of its distributions
to Gaussian distributions.

Matching moments alone would not establish that convergence.

### Why must the process limit be Gaussian?

If

$$
\mathbb G_n\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
$$

then evaluating at any fixed finite collection of functions
gives convergence of the corresponding coordinate vector.

The multivariate CLT identifies its limiting distribution as

$$
(\mathbb Gf_1,\ldots,\mathbb Gf_k)
\sim N_k(0,\Sigma).
$$

Since every finite coordinate vector is multivariate normal,
$\mathbb G$ is a Gaussian process.

Its mean and covariance are

$$
E[\mathbb Gf]=0,
$$

and

$$
E[\mathbb Gf\,\mathbb Gg]
=
P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
$$

### The P-Brownian bridge

This centered Gaussian limit, when realized as a tight,
Borel measurable element of $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$,
is called the **P-Brownian bridge**.

Its index set can be a general function class. It need not
be the interval $[0,1]$.

The familiar Brownian bridge on $[0,1]$ is a special case.

### Example: indicator functions

For $f=\mathbf 1_A$ and $g=\mathbf 1_B$,

$$
fg=\mathbf 1_{A\cap B}.
$$

Hence,

$$
K(\mathbf 1_A,\mathbf 1_B)
=
P(A\cap B)-P(A)P(B).
$$

If $A$ and $B$ are independent under $P$, this covariance is zero.

Because the limiting coordinates are jointly Gaussian,
zero covariance then implies their independence.

## 6. Donsker Classes Are Glivenko–Cantelli

The lecture next connects the functional central limit theorem
to the uniform law of large numbers.

If $\mathcal F$ is $P$-Donsker, then it is also
$P$-Glivenko–Cantelli in probability.

The textbook states that the outer almost sure conclusion
also holds in its canonical i.i.d. setting, although that
stronger conclusion requires an additional argument.

### The key identity

Recall

$$
\mathbb G_n=\sqrt n(P_n-P).
$$

Taking the supremum over $\mathcal F$ gives

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Thus the unscaled estimation error is the scaled empirical
process divided by $\sqrt n$.

### What does the Donsker property provide?

If

$$
\mathbb G_n\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
$$

then continuity of the supremum norm gives

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F}
\rightsquigarrow
\|\mathbb G\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The limit is finite because $\mathbb G$ takes values in
$\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

Consequently, the norms are bounded in outer probability:

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F}=O_{P^\star }(1).
$$

This means that, for every $\eta>0$, there is a finite $M$
such that

$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star (\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F}>M)<\eta.
$$

The norm may fluctuate, but it does not escape to infinity
with substantial probability.

### Divide by sqrt n

For a fixed $\epsilon>0$,

$$
P^\star\left(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}>\epsilon\right)
=
P^\star\left(\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F}>
\epsilon\sqrt n\right).
$$

Since $\epsilon\sqrt n\to\infty$ and the norms are bounded
in outer probability, this probability tends to zero.

Therefore,

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

This is precisely the probability Glivenko–Cantelli property.

### A rate as well as convergence

The same identity gives

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
O_{P^\star }(n^{-1/2}).
$$

So the Donsker property supplies more than vanishing uniform error:
it controls that error at the usual central-limit scale and
identifies the limiting process after scaling.

### The converse does not generally hold

A Glivenko–Cantelli class only requires

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\to 0.
$$

It does not necessarily provide the $n^{-1/2}$ rate or a
tight Gaussian limit for the scaled errors.

Thus,

$$
P\text{-Donsker}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P\text{-Glivenko–Cantelli},
$$

but the reverse implication fails in general.

### Interpreting the distinction

The Glivenko–Cantelli property concerns uniform accuracy:
sample expectations approach population expectations.

The Donsker property also describes the joint random fluctuations
around those expectations at the $\sqrt n$ scale.

The former supports consistency arguments. The latter supports
limiting-distribution arguments for statistical inference.

## 7. Example: The Empirical CDF and the Brownian Bridge

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. real-valued observations with
distribution $P$ and cumulative distribution function

$$
F(t)=P(X_1\leq t).
$$

Consider the class of indicator functions

$$
\mathcal F
=
\{f_t:f_t(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x\leq t\}},\ t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

### Recovering the empirical CDF

For each $t$,

$$
P_nf_t
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf 1_{\{X_i\leq t\}}
=
F_n(t).
$$

Also,

$$
Pf_t=F(t).
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathbb G_nf_t
=
\sqrt n\bigl(F_n(t)-F(t)\bigr).
$$

We can index this process by the functions $f_t$ or,
equivalently, by the thresholds $t$.

### This class is Donsker

The class of indicators of left half-lines is $P$-Donsker
for every probability distribution $P$ on $\mathbb R$.

Consequently,

$$
\sqrt n(F_n-F)\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G
\qquad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathbb R),
$$

where $\mathbb G$ is a centered Gaussian process.

This conclusion does not require $F$ to be continuous.

### Deriving the covariance

Since

$$
f_s(x)f_t(x)
=
\mathbf 1_{\{x\leq\min(s,t)\}},
$$

the covariance formula becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
K(s,t)
&=P(f_sf_t)-(Pf_s)(Pf_t)\\
&=F(\min(s,t))-F(s)F(t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $F$ is nondecreasing,

$$
F(\min(s,t))=\min(F(s),F(t)).
$$

Thus,

$$
K(s,t)=\min(F(s),F(t))-F(s)F(t).
$$

### Connection to the standard Brownian bridge

A standard Brownian bridge $B$ on $[0,1]$ is a centered
Gaussian process with covariance

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(B(u),B(v))
=
\min(u,v)-uv.
$$

Therefore, the empirical-CDF limit can be represented in
distribution as

$$
\mathbb G(t)=B(F(t)).
$$

The CDF transforms the threshold $t$ into the probability
coordinate $F(t)\in[0,1]$.

### Why is it called a bridge?

A standard Brownian bridge satisfies

$$
B(0)=B(1)=0
\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

For uniform observations on $[0,1]$, $F(t)=t$ on that interval.
The limiting empirical process is therefore the standard
Brownian bridge itself:

$$
\sqrt n(F_n(t)-t)\rightsquigarrow B(t).
$$

The process fluctuates between its endpoints but is pinned
to zero at both ends.

One construction is

$$
B(t)=W(t)-tW(1),
$$

where $W$ is standard Brownian motion. Subtracting $tW(1)$
forces the value at $t=1$ to be zero.

### The multivariate example in the lecture

For observations in $\mathbb R^d$, use lower rectangles:

$$
f_t(x)
=
\mathbf 1_{\{x_1\leq t_1,\ldots,x_d\leq t_d\}},
\qquad t\in\mathbb R^d.
$$

Then

$$
P_nf_t
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf 1_{\{X_i\leq t\}},
$$

where the inequality is coordinatewise.

This is the multivariate empirical CDF. The class of lower
rectangles is also $P$-Donsker for every probability law $P$
on $\mathbb R^d$.

## 8. Pre-Gaussian Classes: A Suitable Limit Exists

We have identified the candidate limit of the empirical process:
a centered Gaussian process with covariance

$$
K(f,g)=P(fg)-(Pf)(Pg).
$$

However, specifying its finite-dimensional distributions does
not automatically give a tight random element of
$\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

### Constructing a Gaussian process

Assume every $f\in\mathcal F$ is square-integrable under $P$.

For any finite collection $f_1,\ldots,f_k$, the matrix

$$
\Sigma_{ij}=P[(f_i-Pf_i)(f_j-Pf_j)]
$$

is positive semidefinite because it is a covariance matrix.

These matrices specify consistent multivariate normal
distributions. Kolmogorov's extension theorem then constructs
a Gaussian process $\{\mathbb Gf:f\in\mathcal F\}$ with the
required mean and covariance.

The process exists on a suitable probability space. It need
not be constructed on the original data's probability space.

### Professor's comment: adding simulation randomness

When we combine observed data with additional simulated randomness,
we introduce random quantities beyond those in the original
data-generating setup.

Formally, we can represent this by enlarging the probability space,
for example using a product space carrying both the original data
and independent simulation variables.

The original data distribution can be preserved, while the enlarged
space specifies how the data and simulation randomness coexist.

### Existence is not enough

The construction alone does not guarantee that:

- The sample paths $f\mapsto\mathbb Gf$ are bounded.
- The map into $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$ is Borel measurable.
- Its distribution in that space is tight.

These properties are needed for the process to serve as
a Donsker limit.

### Definition of pre-Gaussian

A class $\mathcal F$ is **P-pre-Gaussian** if the centered
Gaussian process with covariance $K$ has a version that is
a tight, Borel measurable random element of
$\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

This is a statement about the candidate limiting process.

It does not assert that the empirical processes $\mathbb G_n$
converge to that process.

### The distinction from Donsker

The pre-Gaussian property says:

> A suitable Gaussian limit exists.

The Donsker property says:

> The empirical processes converge weakly to that limit.

Therefore,

$$
P\text{-Donsker}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
P\text{-pre-Gaussian},
$$

but pre-Gaussianity alone does not imply the Donsker property.

We still need control of the approximating empirical processes.

### The centered L2 seminorm

Define

$$
\rho_P(f)
=
\left(P[(f-Pf)^2]\right)^{1/2}.
$$

This is the standard deviation of $f(X_1)$.

It induces a semimetric on the class:

$$
d_P(f,g)
=
\rho_P(f-g)
=
\left(
P[((f-g)-P(f-g))^2]
\right)^{1/2}.
$$

From the covariance formula,

$$
d_P(f,g)^2
=
E[(\mathbb Gf-\mathbb Gg)^2].
$$

Thus this distance measures the size of increments of the
candidate Gaussian limit.

### Why only a semimetric?

If $f-g$ is constant $P$-almost surely, then

$$
d_P(f,g)=0.
$$

The functions need not be equal: their centered versions agree.

This matches the empirical process's invariance under adding
constants:

$$
\mathbb G_n(f+c)=\mathbb G_nf,
$$

because both $P_n$ and $P$ integrate a constant $c$ to $c$.

### Characterizing pre-Gaussianity

The Chapter 1 results give the following characterization:

$\mathcal F$ is P-pre-Gaussian if and only if

1. $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded under $d_P$.
2. The candidate Gaussian process has a version whose sample
   paths are uniformly $d_P$-continuous almost surely.

The geometry of the function class and the continuity of the
Gaussian paths determine whether a suitable limit exists.

## 9. Asymptotic Equicontinuity of the Empirical Process

Pre-Gaussianity gives a suitable candidate Gaussian limit.
To establish the Donsker property, we must also control the
empirical processes approaching that limit.

The key condition is asymptotic equicontinuity.

### The distance between functions

Recall the centered L2 semimetric

$$
d_P(f,g)
=
\rho_P(f-g)
=
\left(
P[((f-g)-P(f-g))^2]
\right)^{1/2}.
$$

Functions are close under this distance when their centered
difference has small variance under $P$.

### The equicontinuity condition

For every $\epsilon>0$, require

$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star\left(
\sup_{\substack{f,g\in\mathcal F\\d_P(f,g)<\delta}}
|\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng|>\epsilon
\right)
=0.
$$

Because the empirical process is linear,

$$
\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng=\mathbb G_n(f-g).
$$

Thus the condition controls empirical-process increments
between nearby functions.

### Reading the condition

For a fixed $\delta$, inspect every pair of functions within
distance $\delta$ and find the largest process increment.

Then ask whether that increment exceeds a fixed tolerance
$\epsilon$.

The condition says that its eventual outer probability can
be made arbitrarily small by choosing $\delta$ sufficiently small.

The order of limits matters: we examine large-sample behavior
at a fixed resolution before shrinking the resolution.

### Why individual increment bounds are insufficient

For each fixed pair,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb G_n(f-g))
=
d_P(f,g)^2.
$$

A small distance therefore gives a small variance for that
particular increment.

However, equicontinuity controls the supremum over all nearby
pairs simultaneously. A large or complex class may contain
many opportunities for a large increment.

This is why the size and geometry of $\mathcal F$ matter.

### The difference class

Define

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=
\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ d_P(f,g)<\delta\}.
$$

This is a class of small centered differences, rather than
a neighborhood consisting of the original functions themselves.

Using the notation

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}
=
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_\delta}|\mathbb G_nh|,
$$

equicontinuity becomes

$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
P^\star\left(
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_\delta}>\epsilon
\right)=0.
$$

### Equivalent shrinking-neighborhood formulation

The condition is equivalent to requiring that, for every
deterministic sequence $\delta_n\downarrow0$,

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

The word “every” is essential. Finding one convenient shrinking
sequence is not the stated characterization.

### How this establishes a Donsker limit

Assume $\mathcal F\subseteq L^2(P)$ and that the empirical
processes take values in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

Finite-dimensional convergence follows from the multivariate CLT.

Total boundedness under $d_P$ supplies finite approximations
of the index class.

Asymptotic equicontinuity controls the errors between functions
and their nearby representatives.

Together, total boundedness and this equicontinuity condition
give the Donsker property.

If using the uncentered L2 distance

$$
\|f-g\|_{P,2}=\left(P[(f-g)^2]\right)^{1/2},
$$

the textbook's corresponding characterization assumes

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|Pf|<\infty.
$$

Keep this qualification when replacing the centered geometry
with the uncentered one.

## 10. Why We Need Tools for Controlling Suprema

The lecture introduced two main goals:

- A uniform law of large numbers: the Glivenko–Cantelli property.
- A functional central limit theorem: the Donsker property.

Both require controlling a supremum over a function class.

### The Glivenko–Cantelli target

We want

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|(P_n-P)f|\to 0.
$$

This controls the largest unscaled estimation error over
the entire class.

### The equicontinuity target

For the Donsker property, we must control nearby-function
increments at the central-limit scale:

$$
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|\mathbb G_nh|
=
\sqrt n\,
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|(P_n-P)h|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
$$

for every deterministic $\delta_n\downarrow0$.

Here

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=
\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ d_P(f,g)<\delta\}.
$$

This resembles a uniform law of large numbers, but it concerns
a shrinking difference class and includes the factor $\sqrt n$.

An ordinary Glivenko–Cantelli statement alone does not provide
this stronger control.

### Why definitions are not enough

For a particular statistical method, proving these supremum
bounds directly can be difficult.

The course will develop inequalities and sufficient conditions
that make such proofs manageable.

The textbook's roadmap includes:

- Maximal inequalities to bound maxima and suprema.
- Covering and bracketing numbers to measure class complexity.
- Symmetrization to simplify empirical-process bounds.
- Chaining to connect finite approximations across resolutions.

These are upcoming tools, rather than additional results
proved in this lecture.

### The practical goal

Given a class of functions arising from a statistical method,
we want to check usable conditions that establish uniform
consistency or a functional Gaussian limit.

The definitions identify what must be proved.
The upcoming techniques provide ways to prove it.
