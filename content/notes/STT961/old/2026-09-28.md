## 1. Bernstein-Type Tails: Two Different Regimes

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_m$ be random variables satisfying

$$
P(|X_i|>x)
\leq
2\exp\left\{
-\frac{x^2}{2(b+ax)}
\right\},
\qquad x>0,
$$

for constants $a,b>0$ and every $i=1,\ldots,m$.

Independence is not required for this maximal inequality.

The denominator $b+ax$ gives the bound two different regimes:
Gaussian behavior for smaller deviations and exponential behavior
for larger deviations.

### Small deviations: Gaussian-type behavior

Suppose

$$
0<x\leq \frac{b}{a}.
$$

Then $ax\leq b$, so

$$
b+ax\leq 2b.
$$

Consequently,

$$
P(|X_i|>x)
\leq
2\exp\left\{-\frac{x^2}{4b}\right\}.
$$

The exponent is proportional to $-x^2$, which gives a
Gaussian-type tail bound.

The natural scale in this regime is $\sqrt b$.

### Large deviations: exponential-type behavior

Suppose

$$
x>\frac{b}{a}.
$$

Then $b<ax$, so

$$
b+ax\leq 2ax.
$$

Consequently,

$$
P(|X_i|>x)
\leq
2\exp\left\{-\frac{x}{4a}\right\}.
$$

The exponent is proportional to $-x$, which gives an
exponential-type tail bound.

The natural scale in this regime is $a$.

### Why use two Orlicz functions?

Recall

$$
\psi_1(t)=e^t-1,
\qquad
\psi_2(t)=e^{t^2}-1,
\qquad t\geq 0.
$$

The professor's strategy is to match each tail regime with its
corresponding Orlicz function:

- Use $\psi_2$ for the Gaussian-type part.
- Use $\psi_1$ for the exponential-type part.

This leads to the maximal inequality

$$
\boxed{
\left\|
\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|
\right\|_{\psi_1}
\leq
K\left[
a\log(1+m)
+
\sqrt{b\log(1+m)}
\right],
}
$$

where $K$ is a universal constant.

The two terms reflect the two tail regimes:

$$
\underbrace{\sqrt{b\log(1+m)}}_{\text{Gaussian contribution}}
+
\underbrace{a\log(1+m)}_{\text{exponential contribution}}.
$$

Taking a maximum over more variables increases the size of the
maximum. Gaussian tails produce a square-root-logarithmic
contribution, while exponential tails produce a logarithmic
contribution.

**Interpretation:** A Bernstein-type bound controls moderate
deviations like a Gaussian tail, but allows a slower exponential
bound for extreme deviations. These are upper bounds; they do not
assert that the actual distribution has exactly these tails.

### Check your understanding

Why does the transition occur at $x=b/a$?

Because this is where the two denominator terms are equal:

$$
ax=b.
$$

Below that threshold, $b$ controls the denominator up to a
constant factor. Above it, $ax$ does.

## 2. Proof of the Bernstein Maximal Inequality

The strategy is to split each variable at the threshold $b/a$,
then apply the appropriate Orlicz bound to each part.

### Step 1: Split each variable

Set $c=b/a$ and define

$$
U_i=|X_i|\mathbf 1\{|X_i|\leq c\},
\qquad
V_i=|X_i|\mathbf 1\{|X_i|>c\}.
$$

Thus

$$
|X_i|=U_i+V_i,
$$

and therefore

$$
\max_i|X_i|
\leq
\max_i U_i+\max_i V_i.
$$

By monotonicity and the triangle inequality for the Orlicz norm,

$$
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}
\leq
\left\|\max_i U_i\right\|_{\psi_1}
+
\left\|\max_i V_i\right\|_{\psi_1}.
$$

### Step 2: Bound the individual truncated variables

The small-deviation part satisfies, for every $x>0$,

$$
P(U_i>x)\leq 2e^{-x^2/(4b)}.
$$

For $x<c$, use the Gaussian-type bound from the previous section.
For $x\geq c$, the probability is zero.

The large-deviation part satisfies, for every $x>0$,

$$
P(V_i>x)\leq 2e^{-x/(4a)}.
$$

For $x\geq c$, use the exponential-type bound directly.
For $0<x<c$, observe that

$$
P(V_i>x)
=
P(|X_i|>c)
\leq
2e^{-c/(4a)}
\leq
2e^{-x/(4a)}.
$$

**Why check both ranges?** The tail-to-Orlicz lemma requires
a bound valid for every positive threshold, even though we
originally derived the bounds in separate regimes.

Recall that

$$
P(|Z|>x)\leq A e^{-Cx^p}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\|Z\|_{\psi_p}
\leq
\left(\frac{1+A}{C}\right)^{1/p}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\|U_i\|_{\psi_2}\leq \sqrt{12b},
\qquad
\|V_i\|_{\psi_1}\leq 12a.
$$

The exact numerical constants will be absorbed into a
universal constant.

### Step 3: Apply the maximal Orlicz inequality

The earlier maximal inequality gives

$$
\left\|\max_i Z_i\right\|_{\psi_p}
\leq
K_p\,\psi_p^{-1}(m)\,
\max_i\|Z_i\|_{\psi_p}.
$$

Also, the comparison between Orlicz norms gives

$$
\|Z\|_{\psi_1}\leq C_0\|Z\|_{\psi_2},
$$

where $C_0$ is a numerical constant.

Hence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\max_i|X_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}
&\leq
C_0\left\|\max_i U_i\right\|_{\psi_2}
+
\left\|\max_i V_i\right\|_{\psi_1}\\
&\leq
C_0K_2\psi_2^{-1}(m)\max_i\|U_i\|_{\psi_2}
+
K_1\psi_1^{-1}(m)\max_i\|V_i\|_{\psi_1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Step 4: Substitute the inverse Orlicz functions

Because

$$
\psi_2^{-1}(m)=\sqrt{\log(1+m)},
\qquad
\psi_1^{-1}(m)=\log(1+m),
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq m}|X_i|\right\|_{\psi_1}
\leq
K\left[
\sqrt{b\log(1+m)}
+
a\log(1+m)
\right].
}
$$

The constant $K$ is universal: it does not depend on
$a$, $b$, $m$, or the distributions of the $X_i$.

### Proof pattern to remember

**Split → match tails to Orlicz norms → bound maxima → combine.**

### Check your understanding

Why is the final bound in the $\psi_1$ norm?

The large-deviation assumption only supplies exponential-type
control. We can convert the $\psi_2$ bound for the small part
into a $\psi_1$ bound and then combine both parts.


## 3. Symmetrization: Introducing Random Signs

We now begin §2.3, **Symmetrization and Measurability**.

The goal is to compare the empirical fluctuation $P_n-P$
with a process built using independent random signs.

### The original process

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be i.i.d. observations with distribution $P$.
For a measurable function $f$,

$$
P_n f=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i),
\qquad
Pf=E[f(X_1)].
$$

The centered empirical measure is

$$
(P_n-P)f
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl[f(X_i)-Pf\bigr].
$$

As $f$ varies over a class $\mathcal F$, this defines a
stochastic process indexed by functions.

Its uniform size is

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|(P_n-P)f|.
$$

The usual empirical process includes a $\sqrt n$ scaling:

$$
\mathbb G_n f=\sqrt n\,(P_n-P)f.
$$

### The symmetrized process

Introduce i.i.d. Rademacher random variables
$\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$, independent of the data:

$$
P(\varepsilon_i=1)
=
P(\varepsilon_i=-1)
=
\frac12.
$$

Define

$$
P_n^\circ f
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i f(X_i).
$$

Each observed value receives an independent random sign.

For example, one realization might give

$$
P_n^\circ f
=
\frac1n
\bigl[
f(X_1)-f(X_2)-f(X_3)+f(X_4)+\cdots
\bigr].
$$

### Both processes are centered

Assuming $f$ is integrable,

$$
E[(P_n-P)f]=0.
$$

For the symmetrized process, an even stronger statement holds:

$$
E_\varepsilon[P_n^\circ f\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n]
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
f(X_i)E[\varepsilon_i]
=
0.
$$

Here $E_\varepsilon$ means expectation over the random signs,
with the data held fixed.

The signs center the process without requiring subtraction
of the unknown quantity $Pf$.

### Why conditioning helps

Once we condition on the data, the values

$$
f(X_1),\ldots,f(X_n)
$$

are fixed coefficients. The remaining randomness comes entirely
from the Rademacher signs.

Thus, conditionally on the observations, $P_n^\circ$ is a
Rademacher process and has sub-Gaussian increments.

This connects the empirical-process problem to the maximal
inequalities and chaining tools developed in §2.2.

The professor's main point was:

> After conditioning on the data, we can apply the
> sub-Gaussian bounds already proved. We must then track
> how their quantities depend on the observations.

### What symmetrization actually compares

The original and symmetrized processes generally do **not**
have the same distribution.

Instead, symmetrization provides inequalities comparing their
sizes, such as

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E^\star\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The symbol $E^\star $ denotes outer expectation. It accommodates
the possibility that a supremum over an uncountable function
class is not measurable.

These comparisons are a central tool in proving uniform
laws of large numbers and empirical-process limit theorems.

### Check your understanding

After conditioning on $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, what remains random?

Only the signs $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$.
Each function $f$ supplies a fixed vector of coefficients
$(f(X_1),\ldots,f(X_n))$.


## 4. The Conditional Sub-Gaussian Distance

This derivation expands the professor's observation that the
symmetrized process is sub-Gaussian after conditioning on the data.

### Step 1: Consider an increment

For $f,g\in\mathcal F$,

$$
P_n^\circ f-P_n^\circ g
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\varepsilon_i\bigl[f(X_i)-g(X_i)\bigr].
$$

Condition on $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, and write

$$
a_i=f(X_i)-g(X_i).
$$

The increment becomes a sum of independent random signs
with fixed coefficients:

$$
P_n^\circ f-P_n^\circ g
=
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i a_i.
$$

### Step 2: Bound the conditional moment-generating function

For a Rademacher variable,

$$
E[e^{t\varepsilon}]
=
\frac{e^t+e^{-t}}2
=
\cosh(t)
\leq e^{t^2/2}.
$$

By independence of the signs,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\varepsilon
\left[
e^{\lambda(P_n^\circ f-P_n^\circ g)}
\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n
\right]
&=
\prod_{i=1}^n
\cosh\left(\frac{\lambda a_i}{n}\right)\\
&\leq
\exp\left\{
\frac{\lambda^2}{2n^2}
\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2
\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is a sub-Gaussian MGF bound with squared scale

$$
d_n(f,g)^2
=
\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n
\bigl[f(X_i)-g(X_i)\bigr]^2.
$$

Consequently, when $d_n(f,g)>0$,

$$
P_\varepsilon
\left(
|P_n^\circ f-P_n^\circ g|>u
\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n
\right)
\leq
2\exp\left\{
-\frac{u^2}{2d_n(f,g)^2}
\right\}.
$$

If $d_n(f,g)=0$, the increment is identically zero.

### Step 3: Recognize the empirical $L_2$ distance

Define

$$
\rho_n(f,g)
=
\|f-g\|_{P_n,2}
=
\left[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
\bigl[f(X_i)-g(X_i)\bigr]^2
\right]^{1/2}.
$$

Then

$$
d_n(f,g)=\frac{\rho_n(f,g)}{\sqrt n}.
$$

For the scaled symmetrized process

$$
\mathbb G_n^\circ f
=
\sqrt n\,P_n^\circ f
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i f(X_i),
$$

the conditional sub-Gaussian distance is exactly $\rho_n$:

$$
E_\varepsilon
\left[
e^{\lambda(\mathbb G_n^\circ f-\mathbb G_n^\circ g)}
\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n
\right]
\leq
e^{\lambda^2\rho_n(f,g)^2/2}.
$$

### Interpretation: compare functions through their sample values

Given the data, each function corresponds to a vector:

$$
f\longmapsto
\bigl(f(X_1),\ldots,f(X_n)\bigr).
$$

The distance $\rho_n(f,g)$ is the root-mean-square difference
between these vectors.

It is a **semimetric** on the function class: distinct functions
can have distance zero if they agree at every sampled point.

For example, if

$$
f(X_i)=g(X_i)
\qquad\text{for all }i,
$$

then

$$
\rho_n(f,g)=0
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathbb G_n^\circ f=\mathbb G_n^\circ g
$$

for every choice of signs.

### Connection to covering numbers

Conditionally on the observations, chaining uses covering numbers

$$
N(\delta,\mathcal F,\rho_n).
$$

These measure how many representative sample-value vectors
are needed to approximate the class at accuracy $\delta$.

The distance and covering numbers depend on the data.
To obtain unconditional conclusions, we must then control
these quantities as the sample varies.

In particular, functions agreeing on the sample need not agree
under the true distribution $P$.

### Check your understanding

Why does the $1/\sqrt n$ scaling give the distance $\rho_n$?

Because the squared coefficients sum to

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n
\left(
\frac{f(X_i)-g(X_i)}{\sqrt n}
\right)^2
=
P_n(f-g)^2
=
\rho_n(f,g)^2.
$$

## 5. The First Symmetrization Lemma and the Ghost Sample

### Statement of the lemma

Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable, $P$-integrable
functions, and let $\Phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be
nondecreasing and convex.

Then

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\Phi\left(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(2\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}\right).
}
$$

Here

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-Pf]
\right|,
$$

and

$$
\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
\right|.
$$

The right-hand expectation includes both the observations
and the independent Rademacher signs.

The textbook uses product probability spaces when outer
expectations are needed.

### Useful choices of $\Phi$

Taking $\Phi(t)=t$ gives

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E^\star\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

For $p\geq1$, use
$\Phi(t)=(t_+)^p$, where $t_+=\max(t,0)$, to obtain

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}^p
\leq
2^pE^\star\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}^p.
$$

The positive-part extension makes $\Phi$ nondecreasing
and convex on all of $\mathbb R$.

### Step 1: Introduce an independent copy of the data

Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ be an independent sample from $P$,
also independent of the Rademacher signs.

This is often called a **ghost sample**.

For every $f\in\mathcal F$,

$$
E_Y[f(Y_i)]=Pf.
$$

Thus, holding the original observations fixed,

$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-Pf]
=
E_Y\left[
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right].
$$

We have expressed the unknown population mean as an
expectation over a second sample.

The ghost sample is a proof device; we do not need to
collect another dataset.

### Step 2: Move the supremum outside the expectation

Define

$$
H(X,Y)
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right|.
$$

Using $|E Z|\leq E|Z|$ and then bounding each function's
contribution by the supremum gives

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
E_Y^\star H(X,Y).
$$

For measurable quantities, the underlying principle is

$$
\sup_f |E_Y Z_f|
\leq
E_Y\sup_f|Z_f|.
$$

On the right, the maximizing function may depend on the
ghost sample, which can only increase the bound.

### Step 3: Apply monotonicity and Jensen's inequality

Because $\Phi$ is nondecreasing,

$$
\Phi\left(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\leq
\Phi\left(E_Y^\star H(X,Y)\right).
$$

Because $\Phi$ is convex, Jensen's inequality gives

$$
\Phi\left(E_Y^\star H(X,Y)\right)
\leq
E_Y^\star\Phi\left(H(X,Y)\right).
$$

For outer expectations, this step is justified using
measurable majorants.

Taking outer expectation over $X$, and bounding the
repeated outer expectation by the joint outer expectation,
yields

$$
\boxed{
E_X^\star\Phi\left(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\leq
E_{X,Y}^\star\Phi\left(H(X,Y)\right).
}
$$

### What have we achieved?

We have replaced deviations from the population mean,

$$
f(X_i)-Pf,
$$

with differences between independent copies,

$$
f(X_i)-f(Y_i).
$$

These differences are symmetric under exchanging
$X_i$ and $Y_i$. That symmetry will allow us to
introduce the Rademacher signs.

### Check your understanding

Where did we use each assumption on $\Phi$?

- **Nondecreasing:** to preserve the first inequality.
- **Convex:** to apply Jensen's inequality.


## 6. Completing the Proof: Random Signs and the Factor $2$

From the ghost-sample argument, we have

$$
E^\star\Phi\left(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\leq
E_{X,Y}^\star\Phi\left(
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right|
\right).
$$

We now introduce the Rademacher signs and separate the two samples.

### Step 4: Use symmetry to insert random signs

Because $X_i$ and $Y_i$ are independent copies,

$$
(X_i,Y_i)\overset d=(Y_i,X_i).
$$

Exchanging the pair changes

$$
f(X_i)-f(Y_i)
$$

into its negative, simultaneously for every $f\in\mathcal F$.

For any fixed signs $e_1,\ldots,e_n\in\{-1,1\}$, swap
$X_i$ and $Y_i$ whenever $e_i=-1$. The joint distribution
of the samples remains unchanged.

Therefore, introducing independent Rademacher signs preserves
the expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E_{X,Y}^\star\Phi\left(
\sup_f\left|
\frac1n\sum_i[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right|
\right)\\
&\qquad=
E_{X,Y,\varepsilon}^\star\Phi\left(
\sup_f\left|
\frac1n\sum_i
\varepsilon_i[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right|
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Important:** We use symmetry of the entire indexed process,
not merely symmetry of each individual coordinate.

Also, being mean zero alone would not justify inserting signs.
The symmetry comes from exchanging independent copies.

### Step 5: Separate the two samples

Define

$$
A=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
\right|,
$$

and

$$
B=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i f(Y_i)
\right|.
$$

The triangle inequality gives

$$
\sup_f
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right|
\leq A+B.
$$

Since $\Phi$ is nondecreasing,

$$
\Phi\left(
\sup_f
\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i[f(X_i)-f(Y_i)]
\right|
\right)
\leq
\Phi(A+B).
$$

### Step 6: Apply convexity

Write the sum as a convex combination:

$$
A+B=\frac12(2A)+\frac12(2B).
$$

Convexity then gives

$$
\Phi(A+B)
\leq
\frac12\Phi(2A)+\frac12\Phi(2B).
$$

This is the step that introduces the factor $2$.

Because $X$ and $Y$ have the same distribution and are
independent of the signs,

$$
E^\star\Phi(2A)=E^\star\Phi(2B).
$$

The two half-weighted expectations therefore combine into
one:

$$
E^\star\Phi(A+B)\leq E^\star\Phi(2A).
$$

The variables $A$ and $B$ need not be independent:
they use the same signs. Equality of their distributions
is sufficient.

### Final conclusion

Since $A=\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}$,

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\Phi\left(\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(2\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}\right).
}
$$

This completes the first symmetrization lemma.

For potentially nonmeasurable suprema, the textbook justifies
the expectation steps on product spaces using measurable
majorants and outer-expectation inequalities.

### Proof pattern to remember

1. Introduce an independent ghost sample.
2. Move the expectation outside the supremum using an inequality.
3. Apply monotonicity and Jensen's inequality.
4. Swap independent pairs to insert random signs.
5. Separate the samples by the triangle inequality.
6. Use convexity and identical distributions to obtain the factor $2$.

### Check your understanding

Why can we insert signs into $f(X_i)-f(Y_i)$, but generally
not directly into $f(X_i)-Pf$?

The difference of independent copies is symmetric.
A centered variable need not be symmetric.

## 7. Generalized, Two-Sided Symmetrization

The second lemma generalizes the first in two ways:

1. It applies to sums of independent stochastic processes.
2. It provides a comparison in both directions.

### Setup

Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ be independent stochastic processes
indexed by $\mathcal F$, with measurable, integrable coordinates
and mean zero:

$$
E[Z_i(f)]=0
\qquad
\text{for every }i\text{ and }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

They need not be identically distributed or symmetric.

Let $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$ be independent
Rademacher signs, independent of the processes.

For any process $H$, write

$$
\|H\|_{\mathcal F}
=
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}|H(f)|.
$$

### Statement of the lemma

For every nondecreasing, convex
$\Phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, and arbitrary deterministic
functions $\mu_i:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
E^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
&\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)\\
&\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
2\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n
\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
\end{aligned}
}
$$

As before, outer expectations are interpreted on the
appropriate product probability spaces.

### What does “two-sided” mean?

Take $\Phi(t)=t$ and $\mu_i=0$. Then

$$
\frac12
E^\star\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
E^\star\left\|\sum_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E^\star\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The expected uniform sizes of the original and randomized
sums are comparable up to a factor of $2$.

The right inequality is **symmetrization**.
The left inequality is often called **desymmetrization**.

These are expectation comparisons, not pointwise inequalities
between realizations.

### Recovering the empirical-process inequality

Choose

$$
Z_i(f)=f(X_i)-Pf.
$$

These processes are independent and have mean zero.

To obtain averages rather than sums, apply the lemma with
$\Phi(t)=t/n$. Choosing $\mu_i=0$ gives

$$
\frac12 E^\star
\sup_f\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i[f(X_i)-Pf]
\right|
\leq
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
$$

and

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E^\star
\sup_f\left|
\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i[f(X_i)-Pf]
\right|.
$$

Thus the centered symmetrized process is comparable to
the centered empirical measure in both directions.

### Why allow the functions $\mu_i$?

The deterministic shifts provide flexibility in the
right-hand bound.

For the empirical-process example, choosing

$$
\mu_i(f)=-Pf
$$

gives

$$
Z_i(f)-\mu_i(f)=f(X_i).
$$

The right-hand inequality then becomes the first
symmetrization bound:

$$
E^\star\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
2E^\star\|P_n^\circ\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

Notice that the left-hand side of the two-sided lemma
still uses $\varepsilon_i Z_i$, without the shifts.

### Why centering matters for the reverse comparison

Consider a class containing only the constant function
$f(x)=1$.

Then

$$
(P_n-P)f=0,
$$

but the uncentered symmetrized process is

$$
P_n^\circ f=\frac1n\sum_i\varepsilon_i,
$$

which is generally nonzero.

Therefore, a reverse bound comparing the uncentered
symmetrized process to $P_n-P$ cannot hold in general.

### Check your understanding

Does $E[Z_i(f)]=0$ imply that $Z_i(f)$ is symmetric?

No. The proof must introduce independent copies and use
the symmetry of their differences.

## 8. Proof of the Reverse Symmetrization Inequality

We want to prove

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
}
$$

Here the $Z_i$ are independent, mean-zero processes,
and $\Phi$ is nondecreasing and convex.

### Step 1: Fix the signs and introduce independent copies

First fix a deterministic sign vector

$$
e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)\in\{-1,1\}^n.
$$

Let $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ be independent copies of
$Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$, independent of the original processes.

Because the processes have mean zero,

$$
E_Y[Y_i(f)]=0.
$$

For every $f$,

$$
\sum_i e_iZ_i(f)
=
E_Y\left[
\sum_i e_i\bigl(Z_i(f)-Y_i(f)\bigr)
\right].
$$

### Step 2: Move the expectation outside

Using the supremum inequality, monotonicity of $\Phi$,
and Jensen's inequality, as in the first lemma,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E_Z^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i e_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)\\
&\qquad\leq
E_{Z,Y}^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i e_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Centering is what allows us to introduce the copies
without changing the original sum inside the expectation.

### Step 3: Remove the signs from the differences

Each difference $Z_i-Y_i$ is symmetric as an indexed
process: exchanging $Z_i$ and $Y_i$ negates it.

Independence of the pairs therefore gives

$$
\bigl(e_i(Z_i-Y_i)\bigr)_{i=1}^n
\overset d=
\bigl(Z_i-Y_i\bigr)_{i=1}^n.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&E_{Z,Y}^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i e_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)\\
&\qquad=
E_{Z,Y}^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

We remove the signs from the **differences**, not directly
from the original mean-zero processes.

### Step 4: Separate the copies

Write

$$
A=\left\|\sum_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F},
\qquad
B=\left\|\sum_i Y_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The triangle inequality gives

$$
\frac12\left\|\sum_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\leq
\frac12A+\frac12B.
$$

By monotonicity and convexity,

$$
\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i(Z_i-Y_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
\frac12\Phi(A)+\frac12\Phi(B).
$$

Since $A$ and $B$ have the same distribution,

$$
E^\star\left[\frac12\Phi(A)+\frac12\Phi(B)\right]
\leq
E^\star\Phi(A).
$$

Thus, for every fixed sign vector $e$,

$$
E_Z^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i e_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
E_Z^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
$$

### Step 5: Average over the random signs

The inequality holds for all $2^n$ sign vectors.
Average over them, each with probability $2^{-n}$, to obtain

$$
E^\star\Phi\left(
\frac12\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
$$

For nonmeasurable suprema, the expectation steps use
measurable majorants and the product-space conventions
from the textbook.

### Why the factor $1/2$?

After introducing the copies and applying the triangle
inequality, we have two contributions.

The initial factor $1/2$ makes their sum a convex combination:

$$
\frac12A+\frac12B.
$$

Convexity and identical distributions then reduce the bound
to the expectation for one original sum.

### Check your understanding

Where does independence enter?

It ensures that each pair can be swapped without changing
the joint law of all the pairs. This justifies removing
an arbitrary vector of signs from the differences.

## 9. Completing the Generalized Lemma: Deterministic Shifts

The remaining inequality is

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i Z_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
2\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right),
}
$$

where each $\mu_i:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$ is deterministic.

### Step 1: Subtract the shifts

Define

$$
W_i(f)=Z_i(f)-\mu_i(f).
$$

Since $E[Z_i(f)]=0$,

$$
E[W_i(f)]=-\mu_i(f).
$$

Therefore,

$$
W_i(f)-E[W_i(f)]=Z_i(f).
$$

Subtracting a deterministic function does not change
the centered process.

### Step 2: Introduce independent copies

Let $W_i'$ be independent copies of $W_i$.
For fixed original processes,

$$
\sum_i Z_i(f)
=
E_{W'}\left[
\sum_i\bigl(W_i(f)-W_i'(f)\bigr)
\right].
$$

The supremum inequality and Jensen's inequality give

$$
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i(W_i-W_i')\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
$$

### Step 3: Insert signs and split the sum

The differences $W_i-W_i'$ are independent and symmetric.
We may therefore introduce independent Rademacher signs:

$$
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i(W_i-W_i')\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
=
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(W_i-W_i')\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
$$

Define

$$
A=\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_iW_i\right\|_{\mathcal F},
\qquad
B=\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_iW_i'\right\|_{\mathcal F}.
$$

The triangle inequality and convexity give

$$
\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i(W_i-W_i')\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
\Phi(A+B)
\leq
\frac12\Phi(2A)+\frac12\Phi(2B).
$$

The two terms have the same expectation. Hence

$$
E^\star\Phi\left(
\left\|\sum_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right)
\leq
E^\star\Phi\left(
2\left\|\sum_i\varepsilon_iW_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
\right).
$$

Substituting $W_i=Z_i-\mu_i$ proves the desired inequality.

As in the previous proofs, potentially nonmeasurable suprema
are handled using measurable majorants on product spaces.

### Why the deterministic shifts cancel

If $Y_i$ is an independent copy of $Z_i$, then

$$
W_i-W_i'
=
(Z_i-\mu_i)-(Y_i-\mu_i)
=
Z_i-Y_i.
$$

The shifts disappear from the difference of copies, but
remain in the final randomized sum. This gives us flexibility
in choosing the right-hand bound.

## 10. September 28: Main Ideas to Remember

### Bernstein tails combine two scales

The maximal bound contains both

$$
\sqrt{b\log(1+m)}
\quad\text{and}\quad
a\log(1+m),
$$

reflecting Gaussian-type and exponential-type tail control.

### Independent copies create symmetry

A mean-zero variable need not be symmetric. However,

$$
Z_i-Y_i\overset d=-(Z_i-Y_i)
$$

when $Y_i$ is an independent copy of $Z_i$.

This is what permits random signs in the proofs.

### Conditioning makes the randomized process tractable

Given the observations,

$$
\mathbb G_n^\circ f
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i\varepsilon_i f(X_i)
$$

has sub-Gaussian increments under the empirical distance

$$
\rho_n(f,g)=\sqrt{P_n(f-g)^2}.
$$

We can then apply the maximal inequalities and chaining
tools from §2.2.

### Three recurring proof tools

- Move a supremum outside an expectation using an inequality.
- Use convexity through Jensen's inequality.
- Split a difference using the triangle inequality.
