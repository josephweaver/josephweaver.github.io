## 1. The Donsker Theorem Without Bracketing

### Goal: convergence of the empirical process

Recall

$$
\mathbb G_n f
=
\sqrt n\,(P_n-P)f
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n[f(X_i)-Pf].
$$

We want to establish

$$
\boxed{
\mathbb G_n\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G_P
\qquad
\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
}
$$

where $\mathbb G_P$ is a tight, Borel-measurable
centered Gaussian process.

When this holds, $\mathcal F$ is called $P$-Donsker.

### The envelope condition

Let $F$ be a nonnegative envelope for $\mathcal F$:

$$
|f(x)|\leq F(x)
\qquad
\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

Assume

$$
\boxed{P^\star F^2<\infty.}
$$

For a measurable envelope, this means $PF^2<\infty$.

Compare this with the previous Glivenko–Cantelli theorem:
there we required an integrable envelope; here we require
a square-integrable envelope.

### The uniform entropy integral

Define

$$
\boxed{
J(\mathcal F,F)
=
\int_0^\infty
\sup_Q
\sqrt{
\log N\left(
\varepsilon\|F\|_{Q,2},
\mathcal F,
L_2(Q)
\right)
}\,d\varepsilon.
}
$$

The theorem assumes

$$
J(\mathcal F,F)<\infty.
$$

The supremum is taken over finitely supported probability
measures $Q$ on $\mathcal X$ for which

$$
0<\|F\|_{Q,2}<\infty.
$$

Such a measure has the form

$$
Q=\sum_{j=1}^m q_j\delta_{x_j},
\qquad
q_j\geq0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=1}^m q_j=1.
$$

Its $L_2$ distance is

$$
\|f-g\|_{Q,2}
=
\left[
\sum_{j=1}^m q_j(f(x_j)-g(x_j))^2
\right]^{1/2}.
$$

The empirical measure $P_n$ is one such finitely
supported measure.

### Why normalize by the envelope?

The covering radius is

$$
\varepsilon\|F\|_{Q,2}.
$$

Thus $\varepsilon$ measures approximation accuracy
relative to the envelope's size under $Q$.

Multiplying the class and its envelope by the same
positive constant leaves these normalized covering
numbers unchanged.

### The classes requiring measurability

For $\delta>0$, define

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=
\{f-g:\ f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta\}.
$$

Also define

$$
\mathcal F_\infty=\{f-g:\ f,g\in\mathcal F\},
$$

and

$$
\mathcal F_\infty^2
=
\{h^2:\ h\in\mathcal F_\infty\}.
$$

Here the superscript $2$ means squaring each difference;
it does not mean taking arbitrary products of differences.

Assume that every $\mathcal F_\delta$ and the class
$\mathcal F_\infty^2$ are $P$-measurable.

### Conclusion of the theorem

Under the uniform entropy condition, the measurability
conditions, and $P^\star F^2<\infty$,

$$
\boxed{\mathcal F\text{ is }P\text{-Donsker}.}
$$

### What makes the entropy condition “uniform”?

The bound considers every eligible finitely supported
measure $Q$, rather than only the observed $P_n$.

This allows us to control the conditional empirical
geometry regardless of which sample is observed.

### Check your understanding

Why is the supremum over $Q$ useful after conditioning
on the observations?

Because the conditional process uses the empirical
$L_2(P_n)$ distance, and $P_n$ is among the measures
covered by the uniform bound.

## 2. Proof Strategy: Total Boundedness and Equicontinuity

The proof separates into two main tasks:

1. Show that $\mathcal F$ is totally bounded in $L_2(P)$.
2. Show asymptotic equicontinuity of the empirical process.

The finite-dimensional central limit theorem supplies
the Gaussian limits for each finite collection of functions.

### Why finite-dimensional convergence is available

The square-integrable envelope gives

$$
Pf^2\leq PF^2<\infty
\qquad
\text{for every }f\in\mathcal F.
$$

For any fixed $f_1,\ldots,f_k$, the multivariate CLT gives

$$
\bigl(\mathbb G_n f_1,\ldots,\mathbb G_n f_k\bigr)
\Rightarrow
\bigl(\mathbb G_P f_1,\ldots,\mathbb G_P f_k\bigr),
$$

with centered Gaussian limit and covariance

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb G_P f_j,\mathbb G_P f_l)
=
P(f_jf_l)-(Pf_j)(Pf_l).
$$

The remaining difficulty is controlling the entire
function class uniformly.

### Task 1: Total boundedness

We need

$$
\boxed{
N(\delta,\mathcal F,L_2(P))<\infty
\qquad
\text{for every }\delta>0.
}
$$

At each fixed accuracy, finitely many representative
functions must approximate the whole class.

This task was deferred to the next lecture.

### Task 2: Asymptotic equicontinuity

Recall the small-difference class

$$
\mathcal F_\delta
=
\{f-g:\ f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta\}.
$$

For every deterministic sequence $\delta_n\downarrow0$,
we aim to show

$$
\boxed{
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\sup_{\substack{f,g\in\mathcal F\\
\|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta_n}}
|\mathbb G_n f-\mathbb G_n g|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Nearby functions should produce uniformly small
empirical-process increments.

Here we use the uncentered $L_2(P)$ distance specified
in the theorem. It differs from the centered variance
semimetric used earlier.

### Step 1: Apply Markov's inequality

For any fixed threshold $\eta>0$,

$$
P^\star\left(
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}>\eta
\right)
\leq
\frac{
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
}{\eta}.
$$

Thus it is sufficient to prove

$$
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

### Step 2: Apply symmetrization on the difference class

For $h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}$,

$$
\mathbb G_n h
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i[h(X_i)-Ph].
$$

The first symmetrization lemma gives

$$
\boxed{
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\leq
2E_X^\star E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\left|
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i)
\right|.
}
$$

Measurability of the difference class justifies the
conditional expectation over the signs.

### Step 3: Recognize the conditional Rademacher process

Define

$$
R_n(h)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i).
$$

Given the observations, this process has sub-Gaussian
increments under the empirical distance

$$
\|h-k\|_{P_n,2}
=
\left[
\frac1n\sum_i(h(X_i)-k(X_i))^2
\right]^{1/2}.
$$

In particular,

$$
E_\varepsilon
e^{\lambda[R_n(h)-R_n(k)]}
\leq
\exp\left\{
\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\|h-k\|_{P_n,2}^2
\right\}.
$$

We can now apply the chaining bounds from §2.2.

### The key issue to resolve

The difference class is small under the population norm:

$$
Ph^2<\delta_n^2.
$$

But conditional chaining uses the empirical norm:

$$
P_nh^2.
$$

We must show that population-small differences are also
uniformly small in the empirical geometry.

This is where a Glivenko–Cantelli result for squared
differences will enter the Donsker proof.

### Check your understanding

Why does the proof introduce random signs?

After conditioning on the data, the randomized process
is sub-Gaussian, so its expected supremum can be bounded
using covering numbers and chaining.

## 3. The Empirical Radius and the Chaining Integral

After symmetrization, we need to control

$$
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|,
\qquad
R_n(h)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_i\varepsilon_i h(X_i),
$$

conditionally on the observations.

### Step 1: Define the empirical radius

Set

$$
\boxed{
\theta_n
=
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\|h\|_{P_n,2}
=
\left[
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}P_nh^2
\right]^{1/2}.
}
$$

This is the largest empirical $L_2$ size of a
population-small difference.

The distinction matters:

$$
Ph^2<\delta_n^2
$$

holds by the definition of the difference class, but
does not immediately imply that $P_nh^2$ is small
uniformly over that class.

### Radius versus diameter

The quantity $\theta_n$ is a radius around the zero
function, rather than necessarily the diameter.

For $h,k\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}$,

$$
\|h-k\|_{P_n,2}
\leq
\|h\|_{P_n,2}+\|k\|_{P_n,2}
\leq2\theta_n.
$$

Thus the diameter is at most $2\theta_n$.

The zero function belongs to the difference class, and

$$
R_n(0)=0.
$$

This gives a natural anchor for chaining.

### Step 2: Apply conditional chaining

The conditional Rademacher process is sub-Gaussian
under the distance $\|h-k\|_{P_n,2}$.

An anchored entropy-integral bound gives, up to a
universal constant,

$$
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|
\leq
C\int_0^{\theta_n}
\sqrt{
\log N\left(
u,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(P_n)
\right)
}\,du.
$$

We use the convention that covering centers may lie
in the ambient function space. Equivalent chaining
formulations may change numerical constants or
covering radii.

### Why can the integral stop at $\theta_n$?

For every $u>\theta_n$, the entire difference class
fits inside one ball centered at zero:

$$
\|h\|_{P_n,2}<u
\qquad
\text{for every }h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
N(u,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(P_n))=1,
$$

and hence

$$
\sqrt{\log N(u,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(P_n))}=0.
$$

There is no contribution from larger scales.

If $\theta_n=0$, all the sample coefficients vanish,
and the randomized process is identically zero on
the difference class.

### Step 3: Bound the radius by the envelope

Every difference satisfies

$$
|h|=|f-g|\leq |f|+|g|\leq2F.
$$

Consequently,

$$
P_nh^2\leq4P_nF^2,
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
\theta_n\leq2\|F\|_{P_n,2}.
}
$$

The empirical radius is finite whenever the sampled
envelope values are finite.

### What remains to connect with the theorem?

The chaining bound currently involves

$$
N(u,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(P_n)).
$$

The hypothesis instead controls normalized covering
numbers of the original class:

$$
N(\varepsilon\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q)).
$$

We must therefore:

1. Compare covering numbers of differences with those
   of the original class.
2. Normalize the integration variable by
   $\|F\|_{P_n,2}$.
3. Show that $\theta_n\to0$ in outer probability.

### Check your understanding

Why does a small empirical radius help?

It restricts the chaining integral to a short interval
near zero. The uniform entropy assumption will ensure
that the contribution from this interval vanishes.

## 4. Covering the Difference Class

The chaining bound involves the local difference class
$$
\mathcal F_{\delta_n}
=
\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta_n\}.
$$

The theorem’s entropy assumption, however, concerns $\mathcal F$. We connect these classes by constructing a cover of the difference class from pairs of covering centers for $\mathcal F$.

### Step 1: Cover the original class

Fix a probability measure $Q$ and a radius $u>0$.

Suppose $a_1,\ldots,a_N$ form an $L_2(Q)$-cover of $\mathcal F$ with radius $u/2$, where
$$
N=N(u/2,\mathcal F,L_2(Q)).
$$

For every $f\in\mathcal F$, we can choose $a_j$ such that
$$
\|f-a_j\|_{Q,2}\le \frac u2.
$$

### Step 2: Use differences of covering centers

Write
$$
\mathcal F_\infty=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F\}.
$$

For $h=f-g\in\mathcal F_\infty$, choose centers $a_j$ and $a_k$ approximating $f$ and $g$, respectively. Then
$$
h-(a_j-a_k)
=
(f-a_j)-(g-a_k).
$$

By the triangle inequality,
$$
\|h-(a_j-a_k)\|_{Q,2}
\le
\|f-a_j\|_{Q,2}
+
\|g-a_k\|_{Q,2}
\le u.
$$

Thus the collection
$$
\{a_j-a_k:1\le j,k\le N\}
$$
covers $\mathcal F_\infty$ with radius $u$.

There are at most $N^2$ such centers, so
$$
N(u,\mathcal F_\infty,L_2(Q))
\le
N(u/2,\mathcal F,L_2(Q))^2.
$$

Since $\mathcal F_{\delta_n}\subseteq\mathcal F_\infty$, under the ambient-center covering convention,
$$
\boxed{
N(u,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(Q))
\le
N(u/2,\mathcal F,L_2(Q))^2.
}
$$

If centers are required to belong to the covered class, the same argument holds with an additional universal change in radius.

### Step 3: Substitute into the chaining bound

Taking logarithms and square roots gives
$$
\sqrt{\log N(u,\mathcal F_{\delta_n},L_2(Q))}
\le
\sqrt{2}\,
\sqrt{\log N(u/2,\mathcal F,L_2(Q))}.
$$

Apply this with $Q=P_n$. The conditional chaining bound becomes
$$
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|
\le
C\int_0^{\theta_n}
\sqrt{\log N(u/2,\mathcal F,L_2(P_n))}\,du,
$$
where the factor $\sqrt 2$ is absorbed into $C$.

### Step 4: Normalize by the empirical envelope norm

Set
$$
L_n=\|F\|_{P_n,2}
=
\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n F(X_i)^2\right)^{1/2}.
$$

When $L_n>0$, make the substitution
$$
v=\frac{u}{2L_n},
\qquad
du=2L_n\,dv.
$$

Then
$$
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|
\le
C L_n
\int_0^{\theta_n/(2L_n)}
\sqrt{\log N(vL_n,\mathcal F,L_2(P_n))}\,dv.
$$

Define the deterministic uniform entropy function
$$
H(v)
=
\sup_Q
\sqrt{
\log N\bigl(v\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q)\bigr)
},
$$
where $Q$ ranges over finitely supported probability measures with
$0<\|F\|_{Q,2}<\infty$.

Because $P_n$ is such a measure whenever $L_n>0$,
$$
\boxed{
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|
\le
C L_n
\int_0^{\theta_n/(2L_n)}H(v)\,dv.
}
$$

If $L_n=0$, every function in $\mathcal F$ vanishes at the sample points, so the conditional Rademacher process is identically zero.

### Why this helps

The uniform entropy assumption says
$$
\int_0^\infty H(v)\,dv<\infty.
$$

Consequently,
$$
\int_0^a H(v)\,dv\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }a\downarrow0.
$$

Our next task is therefore to prove that the empirical local radius
$$
\theta_n
=
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\|h\|_{P_n,2}
$$
converges to zero in probability.

### Check your understanding

1. Why does a cover with $N$ centers for $\mathcal F$ yield at most $N^2$ centers for its difference class?
2. Why must each original covering radius be $u/2$ to obtain radius $u$ for the differences?
3. Why do we normalize by $L_n$ before applying the supremum over $Q$?
4. How does integrability of $H$ help when the upper integration limit tends to zero?


## 5. Why the Empirical Local Radius Converges to Zero

Recall that
$$
\theta_n^2
=
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}P_nh^2,
\qquad
\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

The definition of the local difference class guarantees
$$
Ph^2<\delta_n^2.
$$

This controls the population norm. We need to transfer that control to the empirical norm, uniformly over the local class.

### Step 1: Add and subtract the population mean

For each $h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}$,
$$
P_nh^2
=
(P_n-P)h^2+Ph^2
\le
|(P_n-P)h^2|+\delta_n^2.
$$

Taking the supremum gives
$$
\theta_n^2
\le
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
|(P_n-P)h^2|
+\delta_n^2.
$$

### Step 2: Introduce a fixed class of squared differences

Define
$$
\mathcal H
=
\mathcal F_\infty^2
=
\{(f-g)^2:f,g\in\mathcal F\}.
$$

Every squared local difference belongs to this fixed class. Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
\theta_n^2
\le
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal H}
+\delta_n^2.
}
$$

Here,
$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal H}
=
\sup_{q\in\mathcal H}|P_nq-Pq|.
$$

If $\mathcal H$ is $P$-Glivenko–Cantelli, then
$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal H}\longrightarrow0,
$$
and consequently
$$
\theta_n^2\xrightarrow{P^\star }0,
\qquad
\theta_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Thus proving that the squared-difference class is Glivenko–Cantelli will supply the empirical-radius control we need.

### Step 3: Check its envelope

Since $F$ is an envelope for $\mathcal F$,
$$
|f-g|
\le |f|+|g|
\le 2F.
$$

Squaring,
$$
(f-g)^2\le4F^2.
$$

Hence $\mathcal H$ has envelope $4F^2$. The theorem assumes
$$
P^\star F^2<\infty,
$$
so this envelope is integrable:
$$
P^\star (4F^2)<\infty.
$$

The theorem also assumes the required $P$-measurability of $\mathcal H$.

The remaining task is to verify its empirical covering-number condition using the uniform entropy assumption on $\mathcal F$.

### Why use a larger class?

Although $\mathcal F_{\delta_n}$ shrinks with $n$, it changes with $n$. The larger class $\mathcal H$ is fixed.

A uniform law of large numbers for $\mathcal H$ controls every local squared-difference class simultaneously.

### The proof’s connection

We are using a Glivenko–Cantelli result inside a Donsker proof:
$$
\mathcal H\text{ is Glivenko–Cantelli}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta_n\to0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{the local chaining integral shrinks}.
$$

The squared-difference class controls the empirical geometry needed for the original empirical process.

1. Why does $Ph^2<\delta_n^2$ alone fail to give uniform control of $P_nh^2$?
2. Why do we apply the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem to squared differences?
3. Why is $4F^2$ an envelope for $\mathcal H$?
4. Why is a fixed larger class useful when the local class changes with $n$?

## 6. Proving the Squared-Difference Class Is Glivenko–Cantelli

We now verify the entropy condition for
$$
\mathcal H
=
\{h^2:h\in\mathcal F_\infty\},
\qquad
\mathcal F_\infty=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F\}.
$$

The key step is to turn an $L_2(P_n)$-cover of the difference class into an $L_1(P_n)$-cover of its squares.

### Step 1: Factor the difference of squares

For $h_1,h_2\in\mathcal F_\infty$,
$$
|h_1^2-h_2^2|
=
|h_1-h_2|\,|h_1+h_2|.
$$

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz under $P_n$:
$$
P_n|h_1^2-h_2^2|
\le
\|h_1-h_2\|_{P_n,2}
\|h_1+h_2\|_{P_n,2}.
$$

Because each difference satisfies $|h_j|\le2F$,
$$
|h_1+h_2|\le4F.
$$

Writing
$$
L_n=\|F\|_{P_n,2},
$$
we obtain
$$
\boxed{
\|h_1^2-h_2^2\|_{P_n,1}
\le
4L_n\|h_1-h_2\|_{P_n,2}.
}
$$

Thus squaring converts a small $L_2(P_n)$ distance into a small $L_1(P_n)$ distance, with a factor depending on the empirical envelope norm.

### Step 2: Transfer covering numbers

Suppose we cover $\mathcal F_\infty$ using centers from that class, with $L_2(P_n)$-radius
$$
\frac{\varepsilon}{4L_n}.
$$

Squaring those centers gives an $L_1(P_n)$-cover of $\mathcal H$ with radius $\varepsilon$. Hence, with internal covering centers,
$$
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_1(P_n))
\le
N\left(
\frac{\varepsilon}{4L_n},
\mathcal F_\infty,
L_2(P_n)
\right).
$$

Combining this with the difference-class covering argument gives
$$
\boxed{
N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_1(P_n))
\le
N\left(
\frac{\varepsilon}{cL_n},
\mathcal F,
L_2(P_n)
\right)^2,
}
$$
for a universal constant $c>0$.

The constant accounts for the choice of internal or ambient covering centers. Its exact value does not affect the entropy argument.

If $L_n=0$, every squared difference vanishes on the sample, so one center suffices.

### Step 3: Put the radius into normalized form

The uniform entropy assumption controls covering radii proportional to the envelope norm.

Observe that
$$
\frac{\varepsilon}{cL_n}
=
\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{cL_n^2}\right)L_n.
$$

Recall
$$
H(v)
=
\sup_Q
\sqrt{
\log N(v\|F\|_{Q,2},\mathcal F,L_2(Q))
}.
$$

Therefore, when $L_n>0$,
$$
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_1(P_n))
\le
2H\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{cL_n^2}\right)^2.
$$

The theorem assumes
$$
\int_0^\infty H(v)\,dv<\infty.
$$

Since $H$ is nonincreasing, this implies
$$
H(a)<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }a>0.
$$

### Step 4: Control the random normalization

Using a measurable square-integrable envelope for this calculation,
$$
L_n^2=P_nF^2\xrightarrow{P}PF^2<\infty.
$$

In particular,
$$
L_n^2=O_P(1).
$$

Fix a finite $B>0$. On the event $L_n^2\le B$,
$$
\frac{\varepsilon}{cL_n^2}
\ge
\frac{\varepsilon}{cB}.
$$

Because $H$ is nonincreasing,
$$
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_1(P_n))
\le
2H\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{cB}\right)^2.
$$

For fixed $\varepsilon$ and $B$, the right side is a finite deterministic constant. Consequently, on this event,
$$
\frac1n
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_1(P_n))
\longrightarrow0.
$$

The probability of $L_n^2>B$ can be made arbitrarily small by choosing $B$ sufficiently large. Thus
$$
\boxed{
\frac1n
\log N(\varepsilon,\mathcal H,L_1(P_n))
\xrightarrow{P}0
\qquad
\text{for every }\varepsilon>0.
}
$$

### Step 5: Apply the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem

The class $\mathcal H$ has:

- integrable envelope $4F^2$;
- the required $P$-measurability;
- empirical log covering numbers that are $o_P(n)$.

The entropy condition also holds for the envelope-truncated classes used in the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem. Multiplication by a common truncation indicator cannot increase $L_1(P_n)$ distances.

Therefore, the theorem from Section 2.4 gives
$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal H}\longrightarrow0
$$
in the required outer-probability sense.

Returning to our earlier bound,
$$
\theta_n^2
\le
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal H}+\delta_n^2,
$$
we conclude
$$
\boxed{
\theta_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
}
$$

2. Why do we cover the squared class in $L_1(P_n)$?
3. Why does the normalized radius involve $L_n^2$?
4. How does $L_n^2=O_P(1)$ turn a random entropy bound into a deterministic bound on a high-probability event?

## 7. Completing the Asymptotic Equicontinuity Argument

We have established
$$
\theta_n\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

We now use this to finish the local empirical-process bound.

### Step 1: Return to the conditional chaining bound

Recall
$$
R_n(h)
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_i h(X_i),
$$
and
$$
E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|
\le
C L_n
\int_0^{\theta_n/(2L_n)}H(v)\,dv,
$$
where
$$
L_n=\|F\|_{P_n,2},
\qquad
J=\int_0^\infty H(v)\,dv<\infty.
$$

For the expectation calculations below, use a measurable square-integrable envelope. Such an envelope can be obtained from the outer-integrability assumption.

### Step 2: Show the integral shrinks

First suppose
$$
PF^2>0.
$$

By the law of large numbers,
$$
L_n\xrightarrow{P}\|F\|_{P,2}>0.
$$

Together with $\theta_n\to0$, this gives
$$
\frac{\theta_n}{2L_n}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Integrability of $H$ implies
$$
\int_0^a H(v)\,dv\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{as }a\downarrow0.
$$

Therefore,
$$
B_n
:=
C L_n
\int_0^{\theta_n/(2L_n)}H(v)\,dv
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0.
$$

Define $B_n=0$ when $L_n=0$.

If $PF^2=0$, then $F(X_i)=0$ almost surely for every sample point. The Rademacher process is then identically zero, so the conclusion holds directly.

### Step 3: Justify convergence of expectations

Convergence in probability alone does not imply convergence of expectations. We need uniform integrability.

Because the partial integral is bounded by $J$,
$$
0\le B_n\le CJL_n.
$$

Moreover,
$$
EL_n^2
=
E\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nF(X_i)^2\right]
=
PF^2.
$$

Thus
$$
\sup_n EL_n^2<\infty.
$$

Consequently, $\{L_n\}$ is uniformly integrable, and the bound above provides uniform integrability of $\{B_n\}$. Together with convergence to zero in outer probability, this yields
$$
E^\star B_n\longrightarrow0.
$$

It follows that
$$
\boxed{
E_X^\star E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|
\longrightarrow0.
}
$$

The square-integrable envelope supplies the expectation control needed here; merely having bounded first moments would not suffice.

### Step 4: Apply symmetrization

The symmetrization inequality gives
$$
E^\star
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|\mathbb G_nh|
\le
2E_X^\star E_\varepsilon
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|R_n(h)|.
$$

Hence
$$
E^\star
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|\mathbb G_nh|
\longrightarrow0.
$$

By Markov’s inequality, for every $\eta>0$,
$$
P^\star\left(
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|\mathbb G_nh|>\eta
\right)
\le
\frac1\eta
E^\star
\sup_{h\in\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}|\mathbb G_nh|
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Since
$$
\mathbb G_n(f-g)=\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng,
$$
we have proved
$$
\boxed{
\sup_{\substack{f,g\in\mathcal F\\
\|f-g\|_{P,2}<\delta_n}}
|\mathbb G_nf-\mathbb G_ng|
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
\qquad
\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
}
$$

This is the required asymptotic equicontinuity statement.

### What remains in the Donsker proof?

Finite-dimensional convergence follows from the multivariate central limit theorem, and the argument above establishes asymptotic equicontinuity.

The remaining step is to prove that
$$
(\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|_{P,2})
$$
is totally bounded, with functions at zero distance identified.
