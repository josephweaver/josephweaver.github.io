## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced $C_b(\mathbb D)$ as the collection of all bounded, continuous, real-valued functions defined on the metric space $\mathbb D$. These functions will be important because **weak convergence will be defined using expectations of bounded continuous functions**.

The Borel $\sigma$-field is generated by the open sets of the metric space. The professor recalled that a $\sigma$-field is a collection of subsets containing the empty set and closed under complements and countable unions. Open sets are characterized by the property that every point has a sufficiently small neighborhood contained entirely within the set.

The professor then discussed continuity for functions between general metric spaces. Rather than thinking only about familiar functions from $\mathbb R^d$ to $\mathbb R$, we should allow the domain and codomain to be more complicated spaces, potentially spaces of functions with their own metrics.

Continuity can be characterized using inverse images of open sets: **the inverse image of every open set must be open**. This is closely related to Borel measurability because the Borel $\sigma$-field is generated by open sets. Consequently, continuous functions are naturally Borel measurable.

The lemma establishes the converse relationship at the level of generated $\sigma$-fields: the Borel $\sigma$-field on a metric space is exactly the smallest $\sigma$-field needed to make all bounded continuous real-valued functions measurable.

The professor emphasized that these connections explain why bounded continuous functions are a natural foundation for the definition of weak convergence.

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced Borel measures as measures defined on the Borel $\sigma$-field of a metric space. A Borel measure is finite on compact sets, whereas a finite Borel measure has finite total mass.

The discussion then turned to **compactness**, which is important for weak convergence. A set is compact if every collection of open sets covering it contains a finite subcollection that still covers the entire set. The professor emphasized that the original cover might contain infinitely many sets, but compactness guarantees that finitely many suffice.

In metric spaces, compactness is equivalent to completeness together with total boundedness. Total boundedness requires that, for every positive radius $\varepsilon$, the set can be covered by finitely many $\varepsilon$-balls. Completeness concerns Cauchy sequences: whenever the elements of a sequence become arbitrarily close to one another, the sequence must converge to a point within the space.

A student asked how completeness relates to closedness, observing that a Cauchy sequence in an open interval can approach an endpoint outside the interval. The professor discussed how these familiar intuitions work in Euclidean spaces but cautioned that **function spaces can have less familiar metrics and notions of convergence**, including pointwise and uniform convergence.

The professor noted that in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces these properties may seem straightforward, but in general functional analysis they are substantially more delicate. Counterexamples help demonstrate why familiar intuitions do not always extend to arbitrary metric spaces.

# 4. Tightness of Borel Probability Measures — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Lemma 1.3.2

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Def.** A Borel prob measure $L$ is tight if

$$
\forall \varepsilon>0,\quad \exists\text{ a compact }K
\text{ s.t. }L(K)\geq1-\varepsilon.
$$

---

**Remark.** Tightness of $X$ is equivalent to

$$
\exists\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}K_j
:\quad
P\left(X\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}K_j\right)=1
$$

where $K_j$ are compact sets.

**Lemma.** On a complete metric space

(therefore these 2 concepts are equivalent).

*Textbook clarification: The remark characterizes tightness by concentration on a $\sigma$-compact set. The lemma's reference to "these 2 concepts" concerns tightness and separability of a Borel probability measure. Lemma 1.3.2 establishes that separability, pre-tightness, and tightness are equivalent on a complete metric space.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced **tightness** as a property of Borel probability measures. A probability measure is tight when, for any arbitrarily small $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a compact set containing at least $1-\varepsilon$ of the probability mass.

The professor connected this definition to familiar arguments from classical probability. When proving convergence results, we often first identify a sufficiently large region containing nearly all the probability mass, work within that region, and control the remaining probability separately. Tightness formalizes this approach using compact sets.

The professor then introduced **separability**. A metric space is separable if it contains a countable dense subset. The subset may be finite or countably infinite, and density means that every point in the space can be approximated arbitrarily closely by points from that subset. For a probability measure, separability means that the measure assigns probability one to a measurable separable subset.

The professor explained that tightness can also be characterized by probability one being concentrated on a countable union of compact sets. Since compact metric spaces are separable, tightness implies separability.

The converse does not necessarily hold in an arbitrary metric space. However, **on a complete metric space, separability and tightness are equivalent**. The professor indicated that the proof involves constructing finite covers using sufficiently small balls and connecting these constructions to compactness.

These properties will be important when establishing weak convergence and determining whether limiting probability measures behave appropriately in general metric spaces.

# 5. Weak Convergence — van der Vaart & Wellner §1.3, Definition 1.3.3

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Def.** Let $(\Omega_\alpha,\mathcal A_\alpha,P_\alpha)$ be a net of probability spaces

and let

$$
X_\alpha:\Omega_\alpha\to\mathbb D
$$

be arbitrary.

We say $X_\alpha$ converges weakly to a $L$ if

$$
E^*f(X_\alpha)\to\int f\,dL
\qquad\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

$$
X_\alpha\rightsquigarrow L,\qquad
X_\alpha\Rightarrow L,\qquad
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{d}L,\qquad
X_\alpha\xrightarrow{\mathcal L}L.
$$

**Note:** a net $\approx$ a sequence.

$$
\{X_\alpha,\ \alpha\in A\}
$$

$A$ a directed set with order $\preceq$.

$$
\forall\alpha,\beta\in A,\quad
\exists\gamma\in A:
\quad\alpha\preceq\gamma,\quad\beta\preceq\gamma.
$$

*Textbook clarification: Definition 1.3.3 specifies that $L$ is a Borel probability measure on $\mathbb D$. The maps $X_\alpha$ need not be measurable. The index set $A$ is directed, meaning it has a partial order under which every pair of elements has a common upper bound.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced the formal definition of **weak convergence**, which will be central to the course. Instead of requiring the random elements themselves to converge pointwise or in probability, weak convergence is defined through the convergence of expectations of bounded continuous functions.

For every $f\in C_b(\mathbb D)$, we evaluate $f(X_\alpha)$ and take its outer expectation. This is necessary because $X_\alpha$ may not be measurable. The limiting expression is an ordinary integral with respect to the Borel probability measure $L$.

The professor connected this definition to classical convergence in distribution. For ordinary real-valued random variables, convergence in distribution can be described through convergence of distribution functions at continuity points, or through convergence of characteristic functions. Using bounded continuous functions provides a more general formulation that applies to arbitrary metric spaces, where a conventional distribution function may not exist.

The professor emphasized that the index $\alpha$ need not be a natural number. A **net generalizes a sequence** by allowing its indices to belong to a directed set rather than simply $\mathbb N$.

A directed set has an ordering in which two elements need not be comparable, but there must exist another element succeeding both. The professor suggested imagining a graph structure in which two separate branches have a common descendant. The ordering need not represent numerical magnitude; it can describe a property, refinement, or increasing complexity of mathematical objects.

This generalization is useful when working with collections of functions or other mathematical structures that do not have a natural sequential ordering. Ordinary sequences are a special case of nets, so the definition includes the familiar notion of convergence in distribution.

The professor also briefly introduced weak convergence directly for Borel probability measures. Rather than considering arbitrary random maps $X_\alpha$, we can consider Borel probability measures $L_\alpha$ on $\mathbb D$. Weak convergence is then characterized by

$$
\int f\,dL_\alpha\to\int f\,dL
\qquad\forall f\in C_b(\mathbb D).
$$

Because these measures are already defined on the Borel $\sigma$-field, ordinary integrals replace the outer expectations needed for potentially nonmeasurable random elements.
