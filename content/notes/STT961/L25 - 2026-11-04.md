# 1. Completion of the Random Entropy CLT Proof - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.11.1, Theorem 2.11.1

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

We are proving the general CLT for $\{Z_{ni}\}$. We need to show asymptotic equicontinuity.

**(1)** Probability symmetrization lemma.

**(2)** Define

$$
A_n=\left\{
\bigl(Z_{n1}(f)-Z_{n1}(g),\ldots,Z_{nm_n}(f)-Z_{nm_n}(g)\bigr):
(f,g)\in\mathcal F\times\mathcal F,\ \rho(f,g)<\delta_n
\right\}.
$$

*Correction: As in the preceding lecture, my notes put centered $Z_{ni}^{\circ}$ in this set, although the covering metric is the uncentered $d_n$. The textbook proof defines $A_n$ using $Z_{ni}$ after symmetrization; that version is used consistently here. See pp. 206–207.*

The Rademacher process is

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\epsilon_i a_i,
\qquad a\in A_n.
$$

Let

$$
\theta_n^2=
\sup_{a\in A_n}\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2
=
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}.
$$

We need to show $\theta_n\overset{P^*}{\longrightarrow}0$.

**(3)** Let $B_n$ be an $\varepsilon$-net of $A_n$ in the Euclidean norm on $\mathbb R^{m_n}$. The idea is to use symmetrization to relate $\theta_n^2$ and

$$
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\epsilon_i a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}.
$$

In the preceding lecture we showed

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\epsilon_i a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}>t\right)
\leq{}&P^*(|B_n|>M)\\
&+\frac Kt\left[
\varepsilon^2+
\bigl(\varepsilon+\eta_n\sqrt{\log M}\bigr)
E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}^{1/2}
\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\|Z_{ni}\|_{\mathcal F}\leq\eta_n\downarrow0$ after truncation and

$$
|B_n|\leq N^2(\varepsilon/2,\mathcal F,d_n)
$$

is bounded in probability. We will choose $M$.

**(4)** Apply the Hoffmann–Jørgensen inequality to

$$
\|S_n\|=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\epsilon_i a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}
$$

with $p=1$.

Choose $M$ such that, for sufficiently large $n$,

$$
P^*(|B_n|>M)\leq\frac{1-v_1}{2}.
$$

Choose

$$
t_n=\frac{2K}{1-v_1}\left[
\varepsilon^2+
\bigl(\varepsilon+\eta_n\sqrt{\log M}\bigr)
E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}^{1/2}
\right].
$$

Then

$$
P(\|S_n\|>t_n)
\leq\frac{1-v_1}{2}+\frac{1-v_1}{2}
=1-v_1,
$$

so $G_n^{-1}(v_1)\leq t_n$, where $G_n^{-1}$ is the quantile function of $\|S_n\|$.

The Hoffmann–Jørgensen inequality yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\epsilon_i a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}
&\lesssim E\left\|\max_{1\leq i\leq m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}+t_n\\
&\lesssim\eta_n^2+\varepsilon^2+
\bigl(\varepsilon+\eta_n\sqrt{\log M}\bigr)
E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}^{1/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

**(5)** By the expectation symmetrization lemma and the increment second-moment condition,

$$
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}Ea_i^2\right\|_{A_n}\longrightarrow0,
$$

and hence, up to constants,

$$
E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}
\lesssim
E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\epsilon_i a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}+o(1).
$$

*Correction: My notes omit the deterministic mean contribution when applying symmetrization to the nonnegative $a_i^2$. The term $\|\sum_iEa_i^2\|_{A_n}$ must first be retained; it is $o(1)$ by the second assumption of Theorem 2.11.1. This is the first step on p. 208.*

Write

$$
\tau_n=E\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}a_i^2\right\|_{A_n}.
$$

For an arbitrarily small $\zeta>0$, choose $\varepsilon$ sufficiently small and then $n$ sufficiently large. The preceding bounds and Jensen's inequality give

$$
0\leq\tau_n\leq\zeta+\zeta\sqrt{\tau_n}.
$$

Thus

$$
\tau_n-\zeta\sqrt{\tau_n}-\zeta\leq0,
$$

$$
\sqrt{\tau_n}\leq\frac{\zeta+\sqrt{\zeta^2+4\zeta}}2,
$$

and

$$
\tau_n\leq\frac14\bigl(\zeta+\sqrt{\zeta^2+4\zeta}\bigr)^2.
$$

*Correction: My notes use an equality with the two quadratic roots for $\sqrt{\tau_n}$. The argument supplies an inequality, so the nonnegative quantity is bounded above by the positive root. Also the coefficient of $\sqrt{\tau_n}$ requires $\varepsilon$ itself, not only $\varepsilon^2$, to be small. See the closing argument on p. 208.*

Letting $\zeta\downarrow0$ gives $\tau_n\longrightarrow0$, and therefore $\theta_n\overset{P^*}{\longrightarrow}0$. This completes the equicontinuity argument.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor retraced the proof to explain why this last step is necessary: symmetrization and the Rademacher process lead to the random radius, but the general setting lacks the uniform Glivenko-Cantelli result used in the earlier empirical-process proof. The Hoffmann-Jorgensen inequality supplies a different route through that gap. The choices of $M$ and $t_n$ are designed to match the quantile appearing in the inequality, after which another symmetrization step produces an inequality involving the desired quantity and its square root.

In response to a question about choosing constants only near the end, the professor explained that this is often how such proofs are developed. One initially leaves truncation levels, covering bounds, and other quantities flexible, sees how the estimates interact, and then chooses their relations so the argument closes. Presenting those choices at the beginning can make the polished proof harder to understand. The same approach appears in finding minimax rates, where committing to a choice too early may conceal the rate that works.

The recurring ingredients are still symmetrization, Rademacher processes, tail and maximal inequalities, and covering or chaining arguments. Some of the chaining is hidden inside previously proved inequalities rather than displayed anew. The lecture establishes convergence only; tracing the rates of convergence would be a further, difficult problem.

# 2. The Iid Empirical Process - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.11.1, Example 2.11.3

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 1.** Set

$$
Z_{ni}(f)=\frac1{\sqrt n}f(X_i),
$$

where the $X_i$ are i.i.d.

The theorem says that $\mathcal F$ is Donsker if it is suitably measurable, totally bounded in $L_2(P)$, possesses a square-integrable envelope $F$, and, for every $\delta_n\downarrow0$,

$$
\int_0^{\delta_n}
\sqrt{\log N\bigl(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2(\mathbb P_n)\bigr)}\,d\varepsilon
\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0.
$$

*Correction: My notes write $L_2(P)$ in this random entropy integral. The empirical measure $\mathbb P_n$ is required: $d_n^2(f,g)=\mathbb P_n(f-g)^2$. This is the condition in Example 2.11.3, p. 208.*

Recall the Donsker theorem without bracketing, with uniform entropy condition

$$
\int_0^\infty\sup_Q
\sqrt{\log N\bigl(\varepsilon\|F\|_{L_2(Q)},\mathcal F,L_2(Q)\bigr)}\,d\varepsilon
<\infty,
$$

where $Q$ ranges over discrete probability measures.

*Textbook clarification: The transcript describes these criteria as unordered, but with the square-integrable envelope, the earlier uniform entropy condition implies the preceding random entropy condition. This implication and the comparison with Theorem 2.5.2 are stated in Example 2.11.3, p. 208.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

Returning to the i.i.d. empirical process shows what the general theorem gives in the familiar setting. The professor stressed that the random entropy condition has a different form from the earlier uniform entropy condition, so this is not simply a verbatim restatement of the previous Donsker theorem. The precise implication between the two conditions is recorded in the textbook clarification above.

# 3. Truncated Processes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.11.1, Example 2.11.4

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 2.** Consider $Z_{n1},\ldots,Z_{nm_n}$ such that

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}P^*(\|Z_{ni}\|_{\mathcal F}>\eta)
\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{for every }\eta>0.
$$

Introduce

$$
Z_{ni,\eta}(f)
:=Z_{ni}(f)I(\|Z_{ni}\|_{\mathcal F}\leq\eta).
$$

The truncated processes satisfy

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}Z_{ni}-\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}Z_{ni,\eta}
\overset{P^*}{\longrightarrow}0
\quad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
\qquad\text{for every }\eta>0.
$$

For every $\eta_n\downarrow0$, the processes $Z_{ni,\eta_n}$ satisfy the Lindeberg condition.

*Textbook clarification: To retain the approximation of the original sum as well, choose $\eta_n\downarrow0$ sufficiently slowly. The handwritten quantifier "for every $\eta_n\downarrow0$" correctly qualifies the Lindeberg property of the bounded truncated terms, not this approximation. See Example 2.11.4, p. 208.*

If the truncated processes satisfy the remaining conditions of the theorem, then

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}\bigl(Z_{ni}-EZ_{ni,\eta_n}\bigr)
$$

converges weakly in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$.

*Textbook clarification: The remaining requirements include the covariance convergence needed for the central limit conclusion; the centering is by $EZ_{ni,\eta_n}$, not automatically by $EZ_{ni}$. See Example 2.11.4, p. 208.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The value of the truncation example is that the hypotheses can be checked on the truncated processes while the conclusion concerns the original sum with the indicated centering. This can be useful even when the original processes are awkward to control directly.

The professor connected these abstract results to statistics: a functional central limit theorem can yield limiting distributions for statistics built from the process, and the resulting Gaussian functionals can be used to calibrate tests. The examples were deliberately general because different statistical procedures may involve different functionals.

# 4. Weighted Empirical Processes - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.11.1.1, Example 2.11.8

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 3.** For every $n$, let $X_{n1},\ldots,X_{nm_n}$ be independent random elements in a measurable space $(\mathcal X,\mathcal A)$. Let $X_{ni}$ have law $P_{ni}$, and let $P_{ni}f$ exist for every $f\in\mathcal F$, where $\mathcal F$ is a class of measurable functions $f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$.

Given a triangular array of constants $c_{ni}$, define

$$
\mathbb G_n^w(f)
:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}c_{ni}\bigl(f(X_{ni})-P_{ni}f\bigr).
$$

Suppose $\mathcal F$ satisfies the uniform entropy condition,

$$
\max_{1\leq i\leq m_n}|c_{ni}|\longrightarrow0,
$$

and

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}c_{ni}^2P_{ni}\leq P
$$

for a probability measure $P$ with $P^*F^2<\infty$.

Then $\mathbb G_n^w$ converges weakly to a Gaussian process in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$ provided the sequence converges marginally.

*Textbook clarification: Example 2.11.8 states the conclusion under the relevant measurability conditions. The displayed inequality between measures means domination on measurable sets. See p. 210.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The variables remain independent but need not share a distribution. Introducing the triangular array of weights accommodates this change, while the ordinary i.i.d. empirical process is recovered as a special case with the usual $1/\sqrt n$ scaling. The professor presented this as a further extension of the nonbracketing Donsker result.

# 5. The Bracketing Central Limit Theorem - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.11.2, Theorem 2.11.9

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

What about bracketing?

For every $n$, let $Z_{n1},\ldots,Z_{nm_n}$ be independent stochastic processes indexed by $\mathcal F$.

Define $N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2^n)$ as the minimal number $N_\varepsilon$ of sets in a partition

$$
\mathcal F=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_\varepsilon}\mathcal F_{\varepsilon j}^{n}
$$

such that, for each partitioning set,

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}E^*\sup_{f,g\in\mathcal F_{\varepsilon j}^{n}}
|Z_{ni}(f)-Z_{ni}(g)|^2\leq\varepsilon^2.
$$

The partition may depend on $n$.

**Bracketing CLT.** For every $n$, let $Z_{n1},\ldots,Z_{nm_n}$ be independent stochastic processes with finite second moments, indexed by a totally bounded semimetric space $(\mathcal F,\rho)$. Suppose

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}E^*\left[
\|Z_{ni}\|_{\mathcal F}I(\|Z_{ni}\|_{\mathcal F}>\eta)
\right]\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{for every }\eta>0,
$$

$$
\sup_{\rho(f,g)<\delta_n}\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}
E\bigl(Z_{ni}(f)-Z_{ni}(g)\bigr)^2\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0,
$$

and

$$
\int_0^{\delta_n}\sqrt{\log N_{[]}(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_2^n)}\,d\varepsilon
\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

Then the sequence

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m_n}(Z_{ni}-EZ_{ni})
$$

is asymptotically tight in $\ell^\infty(\mathcal F)$ and converges weakly provided it converges marginally.

If the partitions can be chosen independent of $n$, the second condition is not needed.

*Textbook clarification: The first hypothesis here contains the first power of the norm, as printed in Theorem 2.11.9, p. 211. It differs from the squared-norm Lindeberg hypothesis in Theorem 2.11.1, and marginal convergence is an explicit additional requirement for the weak-convergence conclusion.*
