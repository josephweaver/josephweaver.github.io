# 1. Symmetrization for Probabilities - §2.3.2, Lemmas 2.3.6-2.3.7, pp. 111-113

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Last time: Lemma 1; Lemma 2:

$$
E^\star\Phi\left(\frac12\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\le E^\star\Phi\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right)
\le E^\star\Phi\left(2\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}\right).
$$

Here $\Phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ is nondecreasing and convex, the $\epsilon_i$ are Rademacher, and the $\mu_i$ are arbitrary. Example: $\Phi(x)=x$. The function $\Phi(x)=1\{x>a\}$ is not convex.

*Textbook clarification: The recalled lemma assumes independent mean-zero processes $Z_i$ and independent Rademacher signs, with the product-space convention for outer expectations (Lemma 2.3.6, pp. 111-112).*

**Lemma 3.** For independent stochastic processes $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ and arbitrary functions $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n:\mathcal F\to\mathbb R$,

$$
\beta_n(x)P^\star\left(\left\|\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x\right)
\le2P^\star\left(4\left\|\sum_{i=1}^n\epsilon_i(Z_i-\mu_i)\right\|_{\mathcal F}>x\right),
$$

for every $x>0$ and

$$
\beta_n(x)\le\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}P\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i(f)\right|<\frac x2\right).
$$

In particular, this holds for i.i.d. mean-zero processes with

$$
\beta_n(x)=1-\frac{4n}{x^2}\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}\operatorname{Var}(Z_1(f)).
$$

*Textbook clarification: This is useful as a bound on the unsymmetrized probability when $\beta_n(x)>0$; a nonpositive lower bound does not permit division to obtain useful probability control (Lemma 2.3.7, pp. 112-113).*

# 2. Asymptotic Equicontinuity in Probability and in Mean - §2.3.2, Lemma 2.3.11 and Corollary 2.3.12, pp. 113-115

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall: $\mathcal F$ is Donsker if and only if $(\mathcal F,\rho_P)$ is totally bounded and satisfies asymptotic equicontinuity,

$$
\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\xrightarrow{P^\star }0,
\qquad \mathbb G_n=\sqrt n(P_n-P),
$$

where

$$
\mathcal F_{\delta_n}
=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \rho_P(f,g)<\delta_n\},
\qquad
\rho_P(f,g)=\sqrt{P(f-g)^2-[P(f-g)]^2}.
$$

*Correction: My notes state total boundedness in $L_2(P)$ in the recalled equivalence. The necessary-and-sufficient criterion here uses the centered variance semimetric $\rho_P$, consistent with the equicontinuity condition. These metrics are not interchangeable without additional control of the means. See Corollary 2.3.12, p. 115.*

If

$$
E^\star\|\mathbb G_n\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}\longrightarrow0
\qquad\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0,
$$

then asymptotic equicontinuity holds.

*Textbook clarification: My notes use the one-argument notation $\rho_P(h)=\sqrt{P(h-Ph)^2}$ and apply it to $h=f-g$. The two-argument notation above spells out the same variance semimetric, consistent with $\rho_Z$ below and Corollary 2.3.12, p. 115. The Donsker criterion is read for square-integrable coordinates so that the finite-dimensional Gaussian limit and this semimetric are defined.*

Symmetrization lemmas help generalize from $f-Pf$ to $Z(f)$.

**Lemma.** Let $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$ be i.i.d. stochastic processes, linear in $f$. Set

$$
\rho_Z(f,g)=\sigma(Z_1(f)-Z_1(g)),
\qquad
\mathcal F_\delta=\{f-g:f,g\in\mathcal F,\ \rho_Z(f,g)<\delta\}.
$$

Then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)
$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\rightsquigarrow G
\quad\text{for a tight limit in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F).
$$

(ii) $(\mathcal F,\rho_Z)$ is totally bounded and

$$
\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\xrightarrow{P^\star }0
\quad\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

(iii) $(\mathcal F,\rho_Z)$ is totally bounded and

$$
E^\star\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F_{\delta_n}}
\longrightarrow0
\quad\text{for every }\delta_n\downarrow0.
$$

These conditions imply

$$
E^\star\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}^{r}
\longrightarrow E\|G\|_{\mathcal F}^{r},\qquad 0<r<2.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The lecture follows Lemma 2.3.11 (p. 115). Its equivalence invokes the general weak-convergence results of §1.5, which also require finite-dimensional convergence. In the intended i.i.d. centered, finite-variance setting, the ordinary multivariate CLT supplies this. The abbreviated statement must not be read as saying that increment equicontinuity alone controls arbitrary uncentered processes: the needed coordinate assumptions are not explicit in my notes (or the abbreviated printed lemma). No proof absent from the handwritten lecture is added here.*

# 3. Covering Numbers, Brackets, and Envelopes - §2.1.1, Definitions 2.1.5-2.1.6, pp. 83-84

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Chapter 2.4: Glivenko-Cantelli theorems. Let $\mathcal F=\{f:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R\}$.

**Definition.** The covering number $N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)$ is the minimal number of $\epsilon$-balls

$$
\{g:\|g-f\|<\epsilon\}
$$

needed to cover $\mathcal F$. The quantity $\log N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)$ is called the entropy without bracketing.

**Definition.** Given two functions $l,u$, the bracket $[l,u]$ is the set of all functions $f$ with

$$
l(x)\le f(x)\le u(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathcal X.
$$

An $\epsilon$-bracket is a bracket with $\|u-l\|<\epsilon$.

The handwritten diagram shows three curves, with $l$ below $f$ and $u$ above $f$ at every point of $\mathcal X$.

**Definition.** The bracketing number $N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)$ is the minimal number of $\epsilon$-brackets needed to cover $\mathcal F$. Its logarithm is called the entropy with bracketing.

*Textbook clarification: Covering-ball centers and bracket endpoints need not belong to $\mathcal F$, but must have finite norms (Definitions 2.1.5-2.1.6, p. 83). This qualification is needed for the midpoint argument and the later strong-law proof.*

**Remark.** For a norm with the property

$$
|f|\le|g|\implies\|f\|\le\|g\|,
$$

consider $f$ in a $2\epsilon$-bracket $[l,u]$. Since $l\le f\le u$,

$$
f-\frac{l+u}{2}\le u-\frac{l+u}{2}=\frac{u-l}{2},
$$

$$
f-\frac{l+u}{2}\ge l-\frac{l+u}{2}=-\frac{u-l}{2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\left|f-\frac{l+u}{2}\right|\le\frac{|u-l|}{2},
\qquad
\left\|f-\frac{l+u}{2}\right\|\le\frac12\|u-l\|<\epsilon.
$$

Thus $f$ lies in an $\epsilon$-ball centered at $(l+u)/2$, and

$$
N(\epsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|)
\le N_{[]}(2\epsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|).
$$

**Definition.** An envelope function of $\mathcal F$ is any function $F:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$ such that

$$
|f(x)|\le F(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathcal X,\ f\in\mathcal F.
$$

# 4. Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem with Bracketing - §2.4, Theorem 2.4.1, p. 122

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

Recall: $\mathcal F$ is a Glivenko-Cantelli class when

$$
\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}\longrightarrow0
$$

in outer probability, or outer almost surely for the strong version.

**Theorem.** Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of measurable functions such that

$$
N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P))<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0.
$$

Then $\mathcal F$ is a Glivenko-Cantelli class.

**Proof.** Fix $\epsilon>0$. Choose finitely many $\epsilon$-brackets $[l_i,u_i]$ whose union covers $\mathcal F$, with

$$
P(u_i-l_i)=\int(u_i-l_i)\,dP<\epsilon
\qquad\text{for every }i.
$$

For every $f\in\mathcal F$, there is $i$ with $f\in[l_i,u_i]$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
(P_n-P)f
&=P_nf-Pf\\
&\le P_nu_i-Pf\\
&=(P_n-P)u_i+P(u_i-f)\\
&\le(P_n-P)u_i+\epsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\sup_{f\in\mathcal F}(P_n-P)f
\le\max_i(P_n-P)u_i+\epsilon,
$$

and the finite maximum converges to zero almost surely by the strong law.

At the other end,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(P_n-P)f
&\ge P_nl_i-Pf\\
&=(P_n-P)l_i+P(l_i-f)\\
&\ge(P_n-P)l_i-\epsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\inf_{f\in\mathcal F}(P_n-P)f
\ge\min_i(P_n-P)l_i-\epsilon,
$$

and the finite minimum converges to zero almost surely. Consequently,

$$
\limsup_n\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}^\star\le\epsilon
\qquad\text{almost surely, for every fixed }\epsilon>0.
$$

Take $\epsilon_m\downarrow0$ to conclude

$$
\limsup_n\|P_n-P\|_{\mathcal F}^\star =0\qquad\text{almost surely}.
$$

*Textbook clarification: The superscript $*$ denotes a measurable majorant of the supremum. Using a countable sequence $\epsilon_m\downarrow0$ allows a common probability-one event; the result is outer almost-sure convergence, not only convergence in outer probability (Theorem 2.4.1, p. 122).*

# 5. The Classical Empirical Distribution Example - §2.4, Example 2.4.2, p. 123

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example.** The classical case:

$$
\mathcal F=\{1_{(-\infty,c]}:c\in\mathbb R\},
$$

the class indexing distribution functions. A bracket $[I_l,I_u]$ has width

$$
\int|I_u-I_l|\,dP=P((l,u]).
$$

When $P$ has a density $p$, this is

$$
\int_l^u p(t)\,dt,
\qquad \int_{\mathbb R}p(t)\,dt=1.
$$

Choose cutpoints so that the bracket widths are less than $\epsilon$. Then

$$
N_{[]}(\epsilon,\mathcal F,L_1(P))<\infty
\qquad\text{for every }\epsilon>0.
$$

*Correction: My notes write the exact count as $1/\epsilon$. A bracketing number is an integer and the exact count depends on endpoint conventions and the distribution, so this equality is not justified. The needed conclusion is finiteness. Example 2.4.2, p. 123, constructs a finite grid for any underlying distribution, using open/closed endpoint choices to accommodate atoms. The density integral in my notes describes the density case only; a density is not required for the example.*
