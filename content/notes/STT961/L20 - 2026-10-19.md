## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Corollary.** Let

$$
\mathbb R^d=\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty I_j
$$

be a partition into bounded convex sets with nonempty interior. Let $\mathcal F$ be a class of functions $f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R$ such that

$$

for every $\varepsilon>0$, $V\geq d/\alpha$, and probability measure $Q$.

# 3. Donsker Examples from Bracketing Entropy - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.7.1, pp. 157-159

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Example 1.** $C_1^\alpha([0,1]^d)$ is universally Donsker for $\alpha>d/2$.

To check this, use the entropy integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1\sqrt{\log N_{[]}\bigl(\varepsilon,C_1^\alpha([0,1]^d),L_2(Q)\bigr)}\,d\varepsilon
&\leq\sqrt K\int_0^1\varepsilon^{-d/(2\alpha)}\,d\varepsilon\\
&=\sqrt K\left.
\frac{\varepsilon^{1-d/(2\alpha)}}{1-d/(2\alpha)}
\right|_{\varepsilon=0}^{\varepsilon=1}<\infty,
\end{aligned}
$$

provided

$$
-\frac d{2\alpha}+1>0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac d{2\alpha}<1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha>\frac d2.
$$

**Example 2.** $C_1^\alpha(\mathbb R)$, for $\alpha>1/2$:

$$
\log N_{[]}\bigl(\varepsilon,C_1^\alpha(\mathbb R),L_2(P)\bigr)
\leq K\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{2-\delta}
$$

for sufficiently small $\delta>0$. Hence the class is $P$-Donsker, under the tail condition below.

*Textbook clarification: My notes omit the distributional qualification for the unbounded domain. The example on p. 159 assumes $\sum_jP(I_j)^s<\infty$ for a partition of $\mathbb R$ into intervals of fixed length and some $s<1/2$. A sufficient condition is $\int |x|^{2+\eta}\,dP(x)<\infty$ for some $\eta>0$. The displayed bound is not claimed uniformly over arbitrary $P$ on the whole real line.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor emphasized that smoothness must be sufficient relative to the dimension. It is the behavior of the entropy integral near zero that gives the restriction: at the threshold the integrand has the nonintegrable $1/\varepsilon$ behavior. Thus continuity by itself is not enough for this argument. On a one-dimensional bounded interval, a Hölder exponent strictly greater than one half suffices within the stated uniformly bounded smoothness class; the differentiable examples discussed have more than enough smoothness.

The next example replaces the bounded interval by the whole real line. The professor used the preceding partition result to aim for an entropy exponent slightly smaller than two, which again makes the square-root entropy integral finite. In discussing the conclusion, the professor initially said “universal” and then corrected this to $P$-Donsker. The distributional qualification omitted from the written statement is supplied in the clarification above.

# 4. Monotone Functions - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.7.2, Theorem 2.7.5, p. 159

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Theorem.** The class $\mathcal F$ of monotone functions $f:\mathbb R\to[0,1]$ satisfies

$$
\log N_{[]}\bigl(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,L_r(Q)\bigr)
\leq K\frac1\varepsilon
$$

for every $r\geq1$, $\varepsilon>0$, and probability measure $Q$, where $K$ depends on $r$ only.

**Idea of proof.** Create brackets using a grid. The diagram approximates an increasing function by horizontal steps with upper and lower boundaries.

The crude grid count is of the form

$$
N_{[]}\lesssim\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{1/\varepsilon},
$$

so it gives a bound of order

$$
\log N_{[]}\lesssim\frac1\varepsilon\log\frac1\varepsilon.
$$

Getting rid of the logarithm is difficult. It is done with a clever choice of brackets.

*Textbook clarification: The notes use “$\sim$” in the crude grid calculation. It is a preliminary upper-counting argument, not an assertion of the actual asymptotic order of $N_{[]}$. The discussion preceding the full proof on p. 159 makes this distinction.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor did not give the full proof, but illustrated a preliminary construction that nearly attains the stated rate. Divide both the horizontal and vertical ranges into intervals and form upper and lower step functions. The resulting brackets act like a sequence of gates: monotonicity forces each function to pass through at least one admissible sequence of them.

This simple count leaves an extra logarithmic factor. Removing it requires a more carefully chosen partition and a more intricate constructive argument. The professor referred students to the textbook and its references for the sharper proof, noting that the book is useful as a guide to the original results and their sources.

# 5. Convex Lipschitz Functions - van der Vaart & Wellner, Corollary 2.7.10, p. 164

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma.** Let $\mathcal F$ be the class of convex functions $f:C\to[0,1]$ defined on a compact, convex set $C\subset\mathbb R^d$ such that

$$
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L\|x-y\|
\qquad\text{for every }x,y\in C.
$$

Then

$$
\log N\bigl(\varepsilon,\mathcal F,\|\cdot\|_\infty\bigr)
\leq K(1+L)^{d/2}\left(\frac1\varepsilon\right)^{d/2},
$$

for a constant $K$ depending only on $d$ and $C$.

*Textbook clarification: My notes call $C$ compact but omit that the domain is also convex. Corollary 2.7.10, p. 164, states both conditions. The lecture labels the result a lemma; the textbook labels it a corollary.*

*Textbook clarification: The transcript informally refers to a Lipschitz derivative when motivating the convex-function bound. Such a derivative is not guaranteed by convexity and is not assumed in Corollary 2.7.10. The stated hypothesis is the Lipschitz bound on $f$ itself; the proof on p. 164 uses the geometry of its supergraph.*

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor presented convex Lipschitz functions as another class with a useful entropy bound and described geometry as important in its proof. A possible relation to smooth approximation was mentioned as intuition rather than developed into a proof.

In response to a question about Hölder smoothness and moduli of continuity, the professor connected those ideas to the positive exponent $\alpha-\underline\alpha$ controlling the highest derivative in the earlier smoothness class. That control makes the Taylor remainders small enough to discretize and cover. The professor also noted that integrating such bounds can introduce assumptions on a density or geometric quantities associated with the domain; these contributions may be contained in the theorem's constant.

The discussion then returned to statistical motivation. Entropy bounds can establish a functional central limit theorem for a class underlying a statistical method, such as a least-squares construction over a function class. Appropriate functionals can then inherit limits involving a Gaussian process. Uniform control is valuable when the particular function used by the method is itself selected from the data.

The professor cautioned against strengthening assumptions on the domain merely to improve an entropy rate. Such assumptions also restrict where the data may come from and may be unrealistic for the intended method. The practical aim is often to allow the largest useful class that remains Donsker. Additional geometric assumptions can be exploited when they are justified by the application. The professor concluded Chapter 2.7 and announced that Chapter 2.8 would be skipped before turning to Chapter 2.9.

# 6. Multiplier Central Limit Theorems - van der Vaart & Wellner §2.9, pp. 176-177

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

### Chapter 2.9. Multiplier CLTs

$$
Z_i=\delta_{X_i}-P.
$$

The central limit theorem is

$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n Z_i\rightsquigarrow G
\quad\text{in }\ell^\infty(\mathcal F),
$$

where $G$ is a tight Brownian bridge. This is equivalent to $\mathcal F$ being $P$-Donsker.

*Textbook clarification: The handwritten arrow labels $\delta_{X_i}$ as an “indicator.” Here it denotes the Dirac measure at $X_i$, acting on $f$ by $\delta_{X_i}f=f(X_i)$; thus $Z_i(f)=f(X_i)-Pf$ (§2.9, p. 176).*

Given i.i.d. real-valued $\xi_1,\xi_2,\ldots$, independent of $Z_1,Z_2,\ldots$, the multiplier CLT studies

$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\rightsquigarrow\widetilde G.
$$

**Intuition:** $E\xi_1=0$, $\operatorname{Var}(\xi_1)=1$.

$$
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^nZ_i\rightsquigarrow G
\quad\stackrel{?}{\Longleftrightarrow}\quad
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\rightsquigarrow G.
$$

“Probably (conditions?)” Weighted empirical processes.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The professor introduced the multipliers as independent random weights attached to the building blocks of the empirical process. The question is whether the weighted process converges, under what assumptions, and whether it has the same limiting Brownian bridge as the unweighted process. Mean zero and variance one suggest this possibility, but the professor explicitly left open the need for an additional moment condition at this stage.

Rademacher weights provide the familiar starting point from symmetrization. The professor then suggested standard normal weights as another natural example: conditional on the observations, Gaussian weights produce a Gaussian process. This makes the desired convergence plausible, but was offered as motivation rather than as a proof that arbitrary weights work.

# 7. The Multiplier Inequality - van der Vaart & Wellner, Lemma 2.9.1, p. 177

## Professor Wrote (verbatim)

**Lemma (multiplier inequality).** Let $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$ be i.i.d. stochastic processes with

$$
E^*\|Z_i\|_{\mathcal F}<\infty,
$$

independent of Rademacher random variables $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_n$. For every i.i.d. sequence $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$ of mean-zero random variables independent of $Z_1,\ldots,Z_n$, and every $1\leq n_0\leq n$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac12\|\xi_1\|_1 E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}
&\leq E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F}\\
&\leq2(n_0-1)E^*\|Z_1\|_{\mathcal F}\,
E\left[\frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|\xi_i|}{\sqrt n}\right]\\
&\quad+2\sqrt2\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}
\max_{n_0\leq k\leq n}
E^*\left\|\frac1{\sqrt k}\sum_{i=n_0}^k\varepsilon_iZ_i\right\|_{\mathcal F},
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}:=\int_0^\infty\sqrt{P(|\xi_1|>x)}\,dx.
$$

*Correction: The tail integral in my notes omits the absolute value around $\xi_1$. It must measure the two-sided tail $P(|\xi_1|>x)$, as defined immediately before Lemma 2.9.1 on p. 177.*

**Remark.** For symmetrically distributed $\xi_1,\ldots,\xi_n$, the constants $1/2$, $2$, and $2\sqrt2$ can all be replaced by $1$.

---

## Professor Said (Paraphrased)

The multiplier inequality plays the role that the earlier symmetrization inequalities played for Rademacher processes: it compares the size of a weighted process with the size of a symmetrized one. The extra maximum term and the cutoff $n_0$ make this version more complicated, and the professor indicated that their balance would be important in the proof.

The professor contrasted the integral defining $\|\xi_1\|_{2,1}$ with the ordinary tail-integral formula for $E|\xi_1|$. The square root of the tail probability changes the quantity being controlled. Symmetric weights improve the constants, but optimizing constants was not the goal of this lecture.

The planned proof would introduce an independent “ghost” copy of the weights, symmetrize their differences, and connect them to Rademacher variables. The proof was deferred to the next class. In response to a question about an eventual Gaussian limit, the professor emphasized that this inequality itself is more general: the $Z_i$ are arbitrary i.i.d. stochastic processes satisfying its conditions, not necessarily centered Dirac measures from an empirical process.
